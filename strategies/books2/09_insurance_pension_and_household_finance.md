# Insurance, pensions and household finance: how a single-digit insurance loading and a two-stock household set the demand side

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads twelve papers from the insurance, pension and household-finance slice of the local arXiv q-fin.GN harvest (3095 records, 1997-2026). The slice itself is 115 records spanning 2002-2026, 32 of them with a journal reference. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, and it asks a narrow question: what does the household and retirement-income literature say about who sits on the other side of the order book, how their demand responds to prices, fees and taxes, and which liabilities (longevity, drawdown, mortgage, credit) drive their flows.

## What this covers

The slice runs from optimal retirement-income design (tontines, variable annuities, life insurance) through public-pension means tests to the measured behaviour of retail portfolios, household leverage and consumer credit. Its single most consequential finding for a trading platform is that retirement-income demand is not smoothly price-elastic: `1610.10078v1` shows a retiree will abandon a fairly priced life annuity for a tontine once the insurer's one-time loading passes about 7.5% (p.17), while `2503.17778v1` shows the median Finnish household portfolio holds an effective two stocks or fewer (p.16). The demand side this platform faces is therefore lumpy, concentrated and switched by small fee or tax changes, not a diversified mean-variance investor.

## The short answer

1. A retiree switches from a life annuity to a tontine at a modest insurance loading: for high longevity-risk aversion the threshold is about 7.5%, and for low risk aversion and large pools it falls to tens of basis points; `1610.10078v1`.
2. The "natural" tontine whose payout is proportional to survival probabilities is exactly optimal under log utility and loses only about 1% of certainty-equivalent wealth versus the individually optimal design; `1610.10078v1`.
3. A pure lifetime-income tontine cannot be fair to every cohort: at least one cohort must have expected present value below 1 per dollar, so any honest tontine carries a hidden cross-subsidy; `1610.09384v1`.
4. The household demand side is extremely concentrated: the median Finnish retail portfolio never falls below an effective two stocks over 2001-2021, even as the single-stock share fell from 52.9% to 31.3%; `2503.17778v1`.
5. Optimal decumulation is state-dependent, and simple deterministic drawdown rules are dominated: a trained policy beat six rules on all 100,000 test paths in under ten minutes; `2007.09911v2`.
6. Means-tested public pensions break proportionality: a safety net lets low-wealth retirees draw down at a higher rate, and housing becomes part of the optimisation; `2007.09911v2`, `1611.08330v1`.
7. Taxation and stochastic interest rates roughly halve the fair break-even fee on a guaranteed-withdrawal variable annuity (43.18 bp with tax versus 69.35 bp without) and lift the policyholder's subjective value above the insurer's cost; `1901.11296v2`.
8. The option to buy life insurance makes the optimal risky portfolio more conservative, not more aggressive, because insurance substitutes for self-insurance; `1102.2263v1`.
9. Household leverage is a causal driver of local booms and busts: a one-standard-deviation rise in private-label mortgage growth explains 59% of the leverage boom and 98% of the bust; `2407.01539v1`.
10. Consumer credit risk converges with loan age: deep-subprime auto loans season to subprime risk in about three years and to super-prime in about 52 months, and borrowers forgo roughly $1,153-$2,327 of refinancing savings; `2211.09176v3`.
11. Household inflation expectations respond asymmetrically to news, and the channel weakened after 1983 as the share hearing inflation news roughly halved (11.33% to 5.61%); `2009.11557v1`.
12. Most of these results are single-country, model-based or illustrative: only three papers here measure a real market at scale, and none links household demand to order flow; `2503.17778v1`, `2407.01539v1`, `2211.09176v3`.

## 1. Tontines versus annuities: the loading that flips the choice

`1610.10078v1` sets out the canonical illustration: 1,000 retirees each pool $1,000 into a 3% perpetual bond that pays $30,000 a year, so each gets $30 while all survive, $37.50 when only 800 are alive (p.2), and $1,000 each when only 30 remain (p.3). The traditional tontine is therefore a lottery on longevity. The paper's central result is that the payout maximising lifetime utility can be found from an Euler-Lagrange condition, and that the natural tontine, whose payout is proportional to survival probabilities, is exactly optimal for logarithmic utility and near-optimal otherwise (p.12). Its welfare cost is tiny: the certainty-equivalent loss versus the individually optimal design is about 1% at age 80 (p.39). The economically important number is the switch point. A retiree with longevity-risk aversion as high as 9 will prefer a tontine of pool size at least 20 to a life annuity whenever the one-time insurance loading exceeds 7.5% (p.17), and a low-risk-aversion retiree (gamma = 0.5) will accept only 72.6 bp of loading at n = 20 before switching, falling to 0.30 bp at n = 5,000 (p.38). Convergence is slow: the asymptotic aggregate loading is 0.6593, but pools of 10, 100 and 1,000 deliver only 0.2858, 0.3377 and 0.3671 (p.35).

`1610.09384v1` takes the same machinery to heterogeneous cohorts. It prices shares by age so that every cohort has equal expected present value, showing a 55-year-old might pay $200 per share while a 75-year-old pays $40 (p.1). But fairness is provably impossible for a pure lifetime-income tontine: whatever the participation rates, at least one cohort must have expected present value strictly below 1 (p.12). In the limit of a large pool the proportional tontine imposes zero utility loading and reproduces a standard fixed annuity (p.16); in small or lopsided pools it does not, and a two-cohort example with 200 age-65 and 50 age-85 members produces payout volatility at advanced ages once few survivors remain (p.31). `1307.2824v1` adds the historical case: the 1693 English tontine raised only $377,000 of a $1,000,000 target with about 3,750 nominees (p.6), and at the 6% official rate its 10%/7% stream was worth about $133 for a typical age-10 nominee against almost $185 for the 14% life annuity offered in its place (p.6). The longest-lived nominee reached age 100 and drew a final dividend of $1,081, against $14 had she converted (p.7).

## 2. Optimal decumulation and the means-tested safety net

`2007.09911v2` solves the decumulation problem with a deep neural network policy trained on a seven-factor economic scenario generator calibrated to Australian data. For a 67-year-old single male homeowner with A$500,000 and a Balanced (70% growth) allocation, the policy consumes $51,917 in the first year and holds $440,506 at age 68 (p.9). On one simulated path the realised lifetime utilities are -5.62e4 for the trained policy, -7.61e4 for the rule-of-thumb drawdown, -2.22e5 for the regulatory minimum, and -1.29e6 for a 4% rule; the comfortable and luxury target-consumption rules are not sustainable and exhaust wealth at ages 106 and 96 (p.9). After 200 training iterations (two minutes) the policy beats most rules in more than 90% of scenarios, and after fewer than 1,000 iterations (ten minutes) it beats all six on all 100,000 test paths (p.11). Sensitivity is asymmetric: cutting risk aversion from 5 to 2 raises first-year consumption from $51,910 to $59,270 (p.12), while removing the bequest motive entirely changes it by only $336 (p.12). The means-tested Age Pension makes consumption non-proportional to wealth, because the pension is a safety net (p.13), and mortality differences make men consume slightly more than women until age 84, with life expectancy at 65 of 85.5 versus 87.9 (p.13).

`1611.08330v1` studies the 2015-2017 Australian means-test changes directly. As at September 2016 the full Age Pension was $22,721 (single) / $34,252 (couple) a year with an income-test taper of $0.5 and an asset-test taper of $0.039 per dollar (p.7); the January 2017 rebalancing doubles the asset-test taper to 0.078 (p.8). Only 5% of retirees exhaust their allocated pension accounts, so the regulatory minimum withdrawal rates (4% under 65 rising to 14% at 95 and over) often bind as guidelines rather than constraints (p.3, p.9). For a 65-year-old couple with $500,000 the unconstrained optimum is about 11.1%, above the 5% minimum (p.10). The income test overtakes the asset test as the binding constraint around $508,066 (single non-homeowner) and $574,242 (couple non-homeowner) (p.12), and under the new rules it becomes optimal to hold slightly less housing up to about $735,000 (single) and $1,155,000 (couple) total wealth (p.14). This is liability-driven allocation with a kinked, policy-defined payoff.

## 3. Life insurance, human capital and portfolio conservatism

`1102.2263v1` extends the Merton/Pliska-Ye life-cycle problem to one risk-free and N risky assets driven by an M-dimensional Brownian motion, with a random lifetime independent of the market and infinitesimal-term life insurance paying p(t)/eta(t) to the estate (p.1). For discounted CRRA utility the optimal controls are explicit (p.11). In the illustrative calibration - income 50,000 exp(0.03t), risk-free 4%, discount 3%, risk aversion -3, hazard 0.001 + exp(-9.5 + 0.1t), loading eta = 1.05 lambda, drifts 7% and 11% - the optimal insurance purchase falls with wealth and can be negative, so a wealthy wage earner optimally shorts his own life (p.15). The result worth carrying into a platform is Corollary 4.13: access to life insurance makes the optimal risky portfolio more conservative than that of the same wage earner without it (p.19). A household hedging a mortality liability does not add risk to the book; it withdraws it.

## 4. Guaranteed-withdrawal products: fees, taxes and rates

`1901.11296v2` prices a guaranteed-minimum-withdrawal-benefit variable annuity in a Black-Scholes Hull-White world, solved by a trinomial tree. US variable-annuity sales exceeded $100 billion in 2019, about half of all annuity sales, and GMWB maturities can run almost 25 years (p.2), which is why the constant-rate model is inadequate. The headline result is that taxation and rate dynamics roughly halve the fair break-even fee: 43.18 bp with tax versus 69.35 bp without at r0 = 0.03 and sigma = 0.16 (p.15). Higher interest-rate volatility raises the cost because of the chance of very low or negative rates (p.15). With a 3% premium tax the insurer needs a gross premium of 103.09 to deliver a net 100, while the policyholder's subjective value is 110.13, so any sale price between the two is mutually beneficial (p.16); with taxation the subjective value rises to 110.13-115.91 against exactly 100.00 without it (p.16). Taxation also discourages withdrawals: the optimal withdrawal with tax is never greater than without (p.16). For a platform, the embedded guarantee is a short-volatility, short-rate liability whose value is set by tax and rate assumptions, not by the fund.

## 5. Household portfolios: concentration, dispersion and who owns the market

`2503.17778v1` uses a Finnish register of all legal entities' stock ownership over 2001-2021. Household portfolios grew from 592,465 to 776,621 (p.7). The single-stock share fell from 52.9% to 31.3%, but the median HHI fell only from 1 to below 0.6 and the mean from about 0.8 to about 0.6 (p.8); the median HHI for retail investors never goes below 0.5, so more than half of all household portfolios hold an effective two stocks or fewer (p.16). Wealth is concentrated: the top 1% owned 55.5% of household stock value in 2001 and 47.9% in 2021 (p.9). The market index itself became less concentrated, its HHI falling from about 0.4 to 0.1 in roughly ten years and then hovering near 0.06 (p.16), and women's portfolios are more concentrated than men's at both the individual and the aggregate level (p.16). The practical reading is that retail order flow is dominated by a few large, undiversified positions plus a long tail of tiny holdings, so retail flow is far less diversified and far more idiosyncratic than an index-flow model assumes.

## 6. Household leverage and the credit cycle

`2407.01539v1` identifies the household leverage cycle around the Great Recession with a gravity-model instrument on net export growth. In the 1999-2005 boom household leverage (debt-to-income) rose more in high net-export-growth metros than low (0.696 versus 0.643, p.9); it kept rising 2005-2008 (0.288 versus 0.130) and then fell sharply 2008-2014 (0.550 versus 0.288) (p.10). The instrument is strong (clustered Kleibergen-Paap F = 11.43, p.24), and one standard deviation of six-year private-label mortgage growth explains 59.19% of the leverage boom and 97.93% of the bust (p.24), 168.59% of the house-price boom and 192.06% of the bust (p.28). A placebo on government-sponsored-enterprise mortgages fails the first stage (F = 0.773, p.26). The lesson for a platform is that the household balance sheet is a slow-moving regime variable: credit expansion and contraction propagate to house prices, construction and employment with multi-year lags, and that is the macro backdrop against which any long-horizon allocation is set.

## 7. Consumer credit risk converges with loan age

`2211.09176v3` studies 58,118 securitised US auto loans drawn from four ABS trusts holding 55,000, 67,797, 80,636 and 72,515 loans (p.9, p.10). Default rates fall monotonically with credit quality, from 52% in the deep-subprime band to 4% in super-prime, for a 37% overall rate (p.14). The central result is convergence: a deep-subprime loan seasons to subprime risk after about three years, to prime after 50 months and to super-prime after 52 months; subprime loans reach prime risk after 42 months; near-prime loans become prime after about one year and super-prime after 34 months (p.24). Risk-adjusted lender returns are roughly equalised at about 7.5% across deep-subprime through prime, with super-prime lower at about 2.5% (p.35). Borrowers forgo $1,153-$2,327 in credit-based refinancing savings through delayed prepayment (p.1), and deep-subprime borrowers begin overpaying $11-$63 a month around loan age 36 (p.38). For a trading platform, loan age is a state variable: hazard-based curves, not static ratings, are what describe the cash flows.

## 8. Inflation expectations and the news channel

`2009.11557v1` uses 208,777 Michigan Survey records from January 1978 to February 2016 (p.9) and finds a structural break in September 1983 (p.10). Median expected inflation fell from 6% to 3% and the cross-sectional variance from 34.3 to 15.2 (p.11). The share of households hearing inflation news roughly halved, from 11.33% to 5.61%, and monetary-policy news from 12.47% to 5.96% (p.12). Hearing higher-inflation news raised expectations by 1.03% in the first sub-sample and contractionary-policy news by 0.49% (p.13); the effect is asymmetric, with higher-inflation news moving expectations by 0.70% against -0.49% for lower-inflation news in the first sub-sample and 0.46% against -0.22% in the second (p.21). At the zero lower bound the inflation-news coefficient was larger (1.22, p.25) and monetary-policy news moved consumer sentiment by -7.56 (p.26). This is the microfoundation for treating inflation-expectation shocks as an asymmetric, sentiment-mediated flow that hits risk assets unevenly.

## Caveats

Much of this slice is theory or illustrative calibration rather than measured data. `1610.10078v1`, `1610.09384v1`, `1307.2824v1`, `1102.2263v1` and `1901.11296v2` solve models and report parameter sweeps; none is fitted to a live market, and the tontine papers share one mortality basis (Gompertz m = 88.72, b = 10, r = 4%) and one risk-free rate. `2109.03740v1` proposes a long-short bounce-basket strategy but tests it only on one hundred simulated securities over about 252 trading days, so it is a hypothesis, not evidence. `1611.08330v1` reuses an earlier Australian calibration and holds deeming rates constant. The measured papers are each single-country and single-period: Finland 2001-2021 (`2503.17778v1`), US metros around 1999-2014 (`2407.01539v1`), US auto ABS (`2211.09176v3`) and the US Michigan survey (`2009.11557v1`). The slice also disagrees with itself on the direction of retail concentration: individual household portfolios stay concentrated while the market index becomes less so (`2503.17778v1`). No paper here links household demand to order flow, fills or impact; none covers FX, futures, crypto or prediction-market participants; and none establishes that any of these behavioural effects is exploitable after costs. Treat the numbers as mechanisms and calibration anchors, not as return forecasts.

## Papers read in depth

- `2007.09911v2` - Deep neural network for optimal retirement consumption in defined contribution pension system (2020). State-dependent decumulation beats six deterministic rules on all 100,000 simulated paths; evidence is Australian, model-simulated and single-agent.
- `1610.09384v1` - Equitable retirement income tontines: Mixing cohorts without discriminating (2016). Shows fair cohort-mixed tontines are impossible in general and gives existence conditions; the design is conjectural and numerics use one mortality law.
- `1610.10078v1` - Optimal retirement income tontines (2016). Derives the utility-optimal payout and the natural tontine, and the annuity-loading switch point; purely theoretical with illustrative pools.
- `1307.2824v1` - Optimal Retirement Tontines for the 21st Century (2013). Reconstructs the 1693 English tontine and explains the annuity conversion; historical reconstruction with a Gompertz mortality proxy.
- `1102.2263v1` - Optimal Life Insurance Purchase, Consumption and Investment on a financial market with multi-dimensional diffusive terms (2011). Explicit feedback rules and the result that insurance access makes portfolios conservative; theoretical, no data.
- `1901.11296v2` - Taxation of a GMWB Variable Annuity in a Stochastic Interest Rate Model (2020). Quantifies tax and rate effects on break-even fees; illustrative calibration, no market fit.
- `1611.08330v1` - The 2015-2017 policy changes to the means-tests of Australian Age Pension (2016). Models the means-test reform and the housing/liquidity split; reuses one calibration and fixed deeming rates.
- `2109.03740v1` - Behavioral Bias Benefits: Beating Benchmarks By Bundling Bouncy Baskets (2021). Specifies a short-interest, high-beta strategy; simulation only, no out-of-sample test.
- `2503.17778v1` - Heterogeneity of household stock portfolios in a national market (2025). Full-population Finnish register showing extreme retail concentration; nominee accounts cannot be split by owner type.
- `2407.01539v1` - Household Leverage Cycle Around the Great Recession (2024). Causal IV evidence that mortgage credit drove the local boom-bust; identifies a cross-sectional incentive, not the aggregate cause.
- `2211.09176v3` - On the Convergence of Credit Risk in Current Consumer Automobile Loans (2024). Survival/competing-risks convergence ages for auto-loan risk bands; ABS-trust data only, with truncation and censoring limits.
- `2009.11557v1` - A note on the impact of news on US household inflation expectations (2020). Microdata evidence on asymmetric news effects; OLS/correlational and reliant on self-reported news exposure.
