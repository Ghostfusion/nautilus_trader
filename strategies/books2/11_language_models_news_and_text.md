# Language models, news and text signals: model choice, cutoff discipline, and what filing text still carries

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads twelve papers from the arXiv `q-fin.GN` (General Finance) harvest, the general-finance half of the corpus rather than the market-microstructure and trading half covered by `strategies/books/`. It covers the records that turn text into numbers: large language models used as market simulators and return forecasters, disclosure and filing text as a firm-level signal, sentiment and narrative measurement, and the evaluation problems that are specific to these methods. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, who has to decide whether a text-derived feature can be trusted in a backtest and in production. The companion brief `strategies/books/14_news_and_language_model_signals.md` already covers the trading corpus's text evidence (lexicon versus model, cost and turnover, freshness leakage); this brief deliberately adds the general-finance material and does not restate that work.

## What this covers

The slice's arc runs from hand-built word lists scored over U.S. filings, through the first wave of transformer sentiment classifiers, to generative models that are queried as analysts, as forecaster agents, and as frozen snapshots of the public record. The single most consequential finding is that the model is itself a research choice: seven independently developed LLMs scoring the same earnings-call transcripts produce a mean pairwise rank correlation of only 0.52, and the provider term accounts for as much score variance as the transcript term (`2609.31013v1`, p.4). Every other result in this brief has to be read through that lens, because a text feature is a measurement with a provider attached, not an objective extraction.

## The short answer

1. Model choice changes the number: seven providers scoring the same 1,946 earnings calls give a mean pairwise rank correlation of 0.52, and provider identity explains 33.4% of score variance against 34.4% for the transcript itself (`2609.31013v1`, p.3, p.4).
2. Contextual sentiment beats word lists on a human benchmark: transformer indices reach a macro-F1 of 0.67 to 0.69 against 0.187 for the lexicon measures on the same 588 articles (`2607.13968v1`, p.23).
3. Frozen model checkpoints extract information the market has not fully priced: a sector-neutral outlook score predicts one-month returns with t = 6.02 in a pooled panel of 14,918 observations (`2604.21433v1`, p.3).
4. Anonymising company names improves in-sample GPT news returns, meaning look-ahead bias is smaller than the distraction effect it competes with (`2309.17322v1`, p.8).
5. LLM return forecasts inherit human extrapolation: the estimated degree of extrapolation is 38.1 for GPT-4 against 12.09 for the Forcerank crowd (`2409.11540v1`, p.18).
6. A prompted GPT-4o with few-shot examples reaches 0.86 accuracy on the Financial PhraseBank, close to a fine-tuned FinBERT at 0.88 (`2410.01987v1`, p.5).
7. GPT earnings forecasts underperform analysts on average but beat them in more than one quarter of firm-quarters (`2412.01069v2`, p.19).
8. GPT-4 clears a mock CFA Level I in most settings but only scrapes past Level II with prompting, and its dominant failure mode is missing knowledge, not arithmetic (`2310.08678v1`, p.3, p.6).
9. Task-specific disclosure dictionaries beat both general psychological dictionaries and the Loughran-McDonald lists on bankruptcy classification (`2101.00719v1`, p.23, p.25).
10. A firm that merely mentions AI in its 10-K earns a cumulative average abnormal return of 17.25% around the ChatGPT launch, 5.68 points above firms that do not (`2501.01763v1`, p.21).
11. Analysts over-react to earnings-call sentiment and under-react to uncertainty and guidance, a gap measurable only because the text can be morphed while numbers are held fixed (`2511.15214v2`, p.5).
12. The literature itself is young: in finance, 64.7% of the reviewed ChatGPT papers are classified as potential applications rather than value-realization studies (`2412.05731v1`, p.17).

## 1. Language models as return forecasters

`2409.11540v1` puts GPT-4 into a crowd-sourced ranking contest. Across 1,283 weekly contests covering 200 tickers (p.8), the correlation between the average human rank and the GPT-4 rank is 0.279 (p.4), and the regressions show both place positive, decaying weight on the past twelve weekly returns (p.14). The catch is that realised weekly returns show short-term reversals, so the extrapolation is counterproductive. Fitting an exponential-decay model, the degree of extrapolation is 38.1 for GPT-4 against 12.09 for humans (p.18): the model is more recency-biased than the crowd it was trained to imitate. It is also optimistic: the mean expected next-month return is 2.2% against a historical mean of 1.4% and a realised 1.1% (p.6), and only 0.45% of forecasts are below zero (p.22). Its 80% interval contains the realised value 76.9% of the time, versus 79.0% for simply using the 10th and 90th historical percentiles (p.6), so calibration is better than human executives but still not better than a naive percentile rule. A second provider, Claude, produces a sentiment series correlated 0.78 with ChatGPT's (p.5), which is the first hint that these are measurement instruments with a common bias, not independent opinions.

`2412.01069v2` gives the counterweight. Over 7,114 earnings press releases from 1,000 firms around GPT-4 Turbo's April 2023 cutoff (p.4), GPT's mean absolute forecast error falls monotonically from 0.117 when its chosen financial metrics do not overlap the analysts' top-three metrics to 0.061 at the highest overlap, roughly 48% lower (p.19). In the multivariate specification, a one-standard-deviation increase in metric overlap is associated with a 16.8% lower forecast error (p.21). Against analysts, the mean error difference is positive, so GPT loses on average, but the 25th percentile of the difference is -0.001, meaning GPT beats the analyst consensus in more than a quarter of firm-quarters (p.19). The useful operational point is conditional: GPT is not uniformly good or bad, and an observable feature of its processing predicts when it is good.

## 2. Look-ahead bias, memorisation and the knowledge cutoff

`2309.17322v1` makes the cleanest decomposition in the slice. GPT-3.5's training runs through September 2021, leaving October 2021 to December 2022 as the only out-of-sample window (p.4). The authors build long-short strategies from GPT sentiment on 129,431 scraped headlines (6,723 companies) and 181,908 Thomson Reuters headlines (678 S&P 500 names) (p.4), then repeat everything with company names and products replaced by a random string. In-sample the anonymised headlines do better: 30.97 versus 25.08 basis points per day on the scraped set (p = 0.066) and 81.64 versus 77.27 on the Reuters set (p = 0.017) (p.8). That is surprising only until the classification table: the original headline is wrong 17.6% of the time against 38.95% for the anonymised one, yet when the original is wrong and the anonymised one is neutral the strategy loses 242 basis points on average, versus 97 basis points in the reverse case (p.10). The model is confident when it is wrong, which is the opposite of a useful self-check.

`2604.21433v1` turns the cutoff into the design. Twelve frozen OpenAI checkpoints with knowledge cutoffs from September 2021 to August 2025 are queried once per firm for about 7,000 U.S. equities, with no retrieval, so the parameters act as a timestamped digest of pre-cutoff text (p.2). A 100-question trivia check returns a mean accuracy of zero percent on post-cutoff events, which is the audit that the design needs (p.17). The sector-neutral outlook score predicts one-month returns with t = 4.25 in the primary specification and t = 6.02 in a pooled panel with model fixed effects (N = 14,918), and a long-short sort would have earned 2.55% monthly in sample (p.3); seven of twelve checkpoints are individually significant, four survive a Bonferroni threshold (p.3). The limit is stated plainly: only six unique analysis months, so no formal time-series alpha test, and the joint-hypothesis problem is not resolved (p.3). Read it as evidence that a broad text proxy conditions on information standard valuation controls miss, not as a deployable strategy.

The practical rule for both papers is that a text feature derived from a model with a training window must carry that window as metadata. `2511.15214v2` shows how far the discipline can be pushed: it masks every numeral before embedding so magnitudes and dates cannot act as forward references, and it uses a FinBERT checkpoint pretrained only through 2017 on text that predates its evaluation windows (p.16).

## 3. Disclosure and filing text as a signal

`2101.00719v1` builds a task-specific dictionary and shows it beats general ones. From 500 bankrupt and 500 matched non-bankrupt Management Discussion and Analysis sections (p.3), bankrupt firms use debt words at 3.04% versus 2.58% for healthy firms, and distress words at 0.33% versus 0.21% (p.15); the debt-word gap appears four years before the filing (p.17). The evidence is parametric and directional rather than large: debt words t = 12.32, negative words t = 8.28, restructure words t = 7.67 (p.21). In logistic models the Loughran-McDonald word lists reach 0.74 test accuracy against 0.63 for the broad psychological LIWC dictionary (p.23), and the custom stress dictionary improves on both; a one-percent increase in distress words multiplies the odds of bankruptcy by exp(5.03), about 154 (p.25). The result matters because it shows the value is in the task-specific lexicon, not in tone for its own sake; the paper also warns that bankrupt and non-bankrupt disclosures do not differ significantly on simple negative-emotion counts (p.21).

`2501.01763v1` extends filing text to a thematic index. It measures AI engagement from 10-K filings of 3,395 NASDAQ firms, 2010 to 2022, using AI-term frequency and TF-IDF normalised by document length (p.2, p.14). AI mentions grow from 7 filings in 2010 to 527 in 2022 (p.15). Validation is an event study on the ChatGPT launch: 582 AI-classified stocks had a cumulative average abnormal return of 17.25% (t = 8.24) against 11.59% for the other 1,959, a statistically significant 5.68-point gap (p.21). Constructed indices average a 17.48% cumulative abnormal return over the event window, comparable to the best of 14 existing AI ETFs, while only 6 of those 12 ETFs (the comparison set) show significant returns (p.26). The honest caveat is the one the paper itself gives: low R-squared and no alternative classification baseline (p.23).

`2412.05731v1` is the map of this literature and a warning about its maturity. It screens 264 SSRN papers and 144 published papers, retaining 33 (p.14). Sentiment analysis is the single most common task at 21 papers (22.3%), ahead of question-answering at 18 (19.1%) (p.15); GPT-4 is the most-used model at 26 papers (17.7%) (p.15). In finance, 44 of 68 reviewed studies (64.7%) are "potential applications" and only 11 (16.2%) are value-realization studies (p.17). Quoting Gartner, it notes enterprise adoption was 5% in 2023 with a forecast above 80% by 2026 (p.2). The practical reading is that most of what is published is demonstration, not validated production evidence.

## 4. Lexicon versus contextual sentiment

`2607.13968v1` builds daily news-mood indices from 143,755 Factiva articles published in 2025, scoring sentence-level sentiment with FinBERT and aggregating to article and daily measures (p.8). The validation is unusually strong: 444 paid participants rated 588 articles on a five-point scale, three ratings each, 1,776 evaluations, with a performance bonus tied to consensus (p.19). Against those human labels the transformer indices reach accuracies of 66% to 68% and macro-F1 of 0.67 to 0.69, while the Shapiro-style and Barbaglia lexicon indices reach 39% accuracy and 0.187 macro-F1 (p.23). Correlations with the human benchmark are about 0.75 for transformer measures against 0.70 for lexicon measures (p.23). Within the transformer family the daily indices move together at a mean lag-0 correlation of 0.903, while lexicon indices correlate with them at only about 0.765, so the lexicon signal is a smoother, lower-amplitude version of the same thing (p.17). The limit is that the corpus is one year and one publisher mix.

`2410.01987v1` benchmarks prompt engineering against fine-tuning on the 4,848-sentence Financial PhraseBank (p.3). A fine-tuned FinBERT reaches 0.88 accuracy and 0.87 F1; GPT-4o with few-shot examples reaches 0.86 and 0.85, and zero-shot 0.85 and 0.84; GPT-3.5-turbo reaches 0.78 and 0.80 (p.5). The narrow claim is that, on a clean sentence-level task, a prompted general model can match a domain fine-tune; the broader claim the authors do not make is that prompting removes the need for domain training. The dataset is small and the result is task-specific.

## 5. Model choice is a measurement choice

`2609.31013v1` is the paper to read before wiring any LLM-derived feature into a platform. It holds the text, the prompt, the construct definition and the numeric scale fixed, and varies only the provider: seven models from seven developers score 1,946 S&P 500 earnings calls on thirteen measures (p.2, p.9). Across the thirteen measures the mean pairwise rank correlation is 0.52 (p.3). Agreement is construct-dependent: sentiment 0.85, innovation 0.76, integrity 0.32, and a rule-based specificity ratio only 0.23 (p.3). The level differences are large enough to matter: the average within-call standard deviation across providers is 0.75 times the total standard deviation, reaching 0.97 for the specificity ratio and 0.91 for uncertainty, so on those constructs nearly all the variation in a score reflects which model produced it (p.3, p.16). The variance decomposition puts 34.4% on the transcript, 33.4% on the provider, and 32.2% on the provider-by-transcript residual; for sentiment the transcript term is 74%, for integrity 13%, and for the specificity ratio 4% (p.4). Making the rule explicit does not help, which rules out pure construct ambiguity as the cause. Model self-reported confidence is uninformative: about 30% of scores are exactly 0.80, and conditioning on both models being confident lowers the pairwise correlation from 0.52 to 0.48 (p.16). Averaging across providers fixes relative rankings for ten of thirteen measures but absolute score levels for only five (p.5). Correlation with the traditional dictionary measures tops out near 0.55 for culture, innovation and specificity, is 0.29 for sentiment and 0.13 for management clarity (p.5), and 98 of 177,086 requested scores, 0.06%, failed to parse (p.11).

The operational consequence is direct: an LLM-derived feature is a stochastic, provider-contingent measurement. Store the provider, the prompt, the scale and the score's dispersion, and treat a change of provider as a protocol change that invalidates prior calibration.

## 6. Narrative, attention and analyst belief

`2511.15214v2` predicts analyst forecasts from earnings-call text alone and then intervenes on the text. Using 35,203 transcripts from 5,370 companies (p.9), embeddings of management remarks predict 25% to 40% of the variation in expected changes and forecast dispersion even without numerical fundamentals (p.19), and a six-dimension narrative score reaches about 40% of the full-embedding performance (p.5). The method is a text-morphing experiment: LLMs generate counterfactual executive remarks that vary one narrative dimension while holding quantitative content fixed, and the predicted treatment effect is compared for analyst expectations (AEE) against future realised earnings (FRE) (p.5). Analysts under-react to uncertainty (-9.22 bps for AEE versus -41.09 for FRE), confidence (6.09 versus 25.76) and guidance (10.84 versus 19.43), and over-react to sentiment (34.88 versus 25.76) and global perspective (10.84 versus -19.43) (p.5). Text adds a 6% to 13% mean-squared-error reduction over a 300-characteristic numerical benchmark, with forecast gains of 8.38% at one year, 27.50% at two years and 31.64% at three years (p.20, p.21). The limit is that the embeddings and the intervention share a model and the design is in-silico, not a market experiment.

`2412.01069v2` adds the attention result: GPT's narrative selection is stable across firms, favouring sentences placed early (-11.0% probability per position), longer (+5.2% per standard deviation) and forward-looking, and this is human-like except for the preference for length (p.12, p.13). Forecasts are more accurate when the selected sentences are more readable and forward-looking, and less accurate when they are more negatively toned, so GPT overweights a salient cue that adds noise (p.5, p.21).

## 7. Evaluation problems specific to text methods

`2310.08678v1` supplies the capability ceiling. On mock CFA exams, GPT-4 scores 73.2% zero-shot, 74.0% with chain-of-thought and 74.6% with ten-shot prompts on Level I, and 57.4%, 61.4% and 60.5% on Level II; ChatGPT scores 58.8% to 63.0% on Level I and 46.6% to 47.6% on Level II (p.3). Level II prompts are about ten times longer (p.5), and the dominant error type when chain-of-thought makes an answer worse is missing knowledge, 55.2% of such errors for ChatGPT and 50.0% for GPT-4, with pure calculation errors at 17.2% and 28.6% (p.6). So the failure is comprehension of long, table-heavy cases and gaps in financial knowledge, not arithmetic per se. That is the same regime this platform's long filings and call transcripts live in.

Three evaluation rules fall out of the slice. First, do not treat self-reported confidence as a reliability signal (`2609.31013v1`, p.16). Second, diagnose conditionally: `2412.01069v2` builds a composite score from observable processing features (readable and forward-looking selection, analyst-consistent metrics, reported confidence) that rises monotonically with forecast accuracy, which is a better gate than the average error (p.6). Third, remember the multiple-testing and short-window limits that the strongest papers state for themselves (`2604.21433v1`, p.3).

## Caveats

Several of these papers have no outcome data at all. `2412.05731v1` is a review, and `2609.31013v1` studies measurement reliability, not returns; neither establishes that any measured construct predicts anything tradable. The sentiment and narrative studies are single-market and mostly single-period: `2607.13968v1` covers one year (2025) of one publisher mix, `2511.15214v2` covers U.S. annual earnings calls, and `2604.21433v1` rests on six unique analysis months, which the authors say prevents a formal alpha test. `2309.17322v1` and `2409.11540v1` use strategies and contests that are explicitly infeasible (no transaction costs, no short-sale constraints, a crowd platform that shut down). `2101.00719v1` is a balanced 500-versus-500 design, so its accuracies are not base rates for a live screen, and `2501.01763v1` concedes low R-squared and no alternative classification baseline. The slice disagrees with itself on how much context helps: `2609.31013v1` finds the strongest inter-model agreement on sentiment and the weakest on a rule-based specificity task, while `2607.13968v1` and `2410.01987v1` find contextual models clearly better than lexicons on their benchmarks, which suggests the answer depends on the construct and the validation target rather than on "transformers beat dictionaries" as a general law. No paper here establishes that any of these measures produces net-of-cost alpha, that a provider ranking is stable across time, or that an in-silico narrative intervention transfers to live prices.

## Papers read in depth

- `2309.17322v1` - Assessing Look-Ahead Bias in Stock Return Predictions Generated By GPT Sentiment Analysis (2023). Cleanly separates look-ahead bias from a distraction effect via entity anonymisation; no costs, and the out-of-sample comparison is borderline.
- `2101.00719v1` - Bankruptcy prediction using disclosure text features (2021). Builds a task-specific stress dictionary that beats LIWC and Loughran-McDonald on 500-versus-500 MDAs; a balanced design, so the reported accuracy is not a base rate.
- `2310.08678v1` - Can GPT models be Financial Analysts? An Evaluation of ChatGPT and GPT-4 on mock CFA Exams (2023). Capability ceiling and error taxonomy; mock exams, single snapshot of models.
- `2409.11540v1` - What Does ChatGPT Make of Historical Stock Returns? Extrapolation and Miscalibration in LLM Stock Return Forecasts (2024). Measures LLM extrapolation against a human crowd; the contest setting has no costs and no live market.
- `2410.01987v1` - Financial Sentiment Analysis on News and Reports Using Large Language Models and FinBERT (2024). Prompted GPT-4o versus fine-tuned FinBERT on 4,848 sentences; one small labelled dataset.
- `2412.01069v2` - The Promise and Peril of Generative AI: Evidence from GPT as Sell-Side Analysts (2025). Conditional forecast diagnostics over 7,114 press releases; average performance still trails analysts.
- `2412.05731v1` - A Scoping Review of ChatGPT Research in Accounting and Finance (2024, journal: Intl. J. Account. Inf. Syst. 55: 100715). Maps the field and its immaturity; a review, so it carries no independent evidence.
- `2501.01763v1` - Quantifying A Firm's AI Engagement: Constructing Objective, Data-Driven, AI Stock Indices Using 10-K Filings (2025, journal: Technological Forecasting and Social Change 212: 123965). Filing-text theme with an event-study validation; low R-squared and no classification baseline.
- `2511.15214v2` - Corporate Earnings Calls and Analyst Beliefs (2025). Text-morphing intervention that separates what analysts attend to from what predicts earnings; in-silico and model-dependent.
- `2604.21433v1` - ChatGPT as a Time Capsule: The Limits of Price Discovery (2026). Uses frozen checkpoints as timestamped information sets with a leakage audit; only six unique analysis months.
- `2607.13968v1` - Measuring Sentiment News with Transformer-Based Language Models (2026). Human-validated comparison of transformer and lexicon mood indices; one year and one publisher mix.
- `2609.31013v1` - Same Text, Different Numbers: The Divergence of LLM-Based Measures (2026). The core measurement-invariance result across seven providers; studies reliability, so it makes no return claim.
