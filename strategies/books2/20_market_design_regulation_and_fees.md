# Market design, regulation and fees: rule changes reroute flow and rewrite what prices on

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads the general-finance half of market design: what regulatory interventions, disclosure regimes and venue rules do to prices, liquidity and the location of trading. Its slice is the q-fin.GN harvest of 43 records matching regulation, disclosure and market-structure policy between 2007 and 2026, 11 of which carry a journal reference; twelve papers were read from the PDFs. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, whose strategies are exposed to someone else's rulebook and whose backtests assume today's rulebook will hold.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from single enforcement events (SEC security classifications), through regime switches (ChiNext approval to registration, the US DAO Report, MiCA's stablecoin authorisation), to the design margins where rules bite hardest: venue opacity, settlement horizons, margin thresholds, priority fees and mandated risk measures. Its single most consequential finding is that rules relocate activity rather than destroy it. After the July 2017 DAO Report, Europe gained about 14 additional ICOs per region-month relative to other regions while global issuance kept running (`2602.00138v1`, p.17); after the MiCA delistings of March 2025, aggregate stablecoin trading barely moved while the venue cross-section shifted 0.82 pre-event standard deviations toward USDC (`2607.09514v1`, p.9). A strategy that reads a rule change as a level shock to its market, rather than as a reallocation across venues and assets, will mis-size both the opportunity and the risk.

## The short answer

1. Regulation reroutes issuance rather than ending it: Europe gains 13.88 additional ICOs per region-month after the DAO Report (`2602.00138v1`, p.17).
2. Aggregates absorb a delisting mandate while the venue cross-section moves: the USDC-share difference-in-differences is 0.818 pre-event standard deviations (`2607.09514v1`, p.9).
3. The shift is a contraction on the delisted leg, not growth in the substitute: USDT volume falls about 20 percent where it is delisted while USDC volume is flat (`2607.09514v1`, p.17).
4. SEC security classifications cost the named assets 17.2 percent over 30 days (`2412.02452v1`, p.15).
5. The same events drain volume: cumulative abnormal volume is -4.952 log units over 30 days (`2412.02452v1`, p.16).
6. The damage concentrates in illiquid and volatile assets: volatility beta -0.765 on day 0, deepening to -5.151 at 30 days (`2412.02452v1`, p.8).
7. Volume drains before the announcement too, an informed-trading signature, with no matching move in price (`2412.02452v1`, p.9).
8. A listing-regime change rewrites what prices on: ChiNext moves from demand-driven to value-driven pricing (`2212.11779v1`, p.25).
9. Listing-day caps do not remove overreaction; they stretch it over 21 to 42 days and lift the measured initial return to 334.60 percent (`2212.11779v1`, p.23).
10. Better disclosure changes investor behaviour: the disposition-effect gap falls by 0.0138 in sale probability for high-transparency firms (`2605.07352v1`, p.9).
11. Venue opacity has no uniform effect: dark pools improve price discovery when signals are precise and impair it when they are noisy (`1612.08486v1`, p.3).
12. Contract horizon, not enforcement, removed settlement manipulation in prediction markets (`2606.31675v1`, p.7).

## 1. Rule changes reroute flow; they rarely reduce it

The DAO Report of July 2017 applied US securities law to many ICOs without changing any statute (`2602.00138v1`, p.2). In a region-month panel of eight regions over 96 months, Europe's differential post-2017 ICO count is 13.88 per region-month against a pre-treatment European mean of 2.60 (clustered standard error 6.63, R-squared 0.847, 768 observations) (`2602.00138v1`, p.17). The raw counts move from 109 European ICOs against 145 elsewhere before the report to 1,008 against 1,014 after (`2602.00138v1`, p.15). A placebo that reassigns treatment to each control region gives Europe 13.9 against Asia 4.4 and North America 1.3 (`2602.00138v1`, p.19). Excluding the European crypto hubs (Switzerland, UK, Estonia, Malta, Cyprus, Gibraltar) cuts the coefficient to 5.66 (standard error 2.78) but keeps significance, so the reallocation was broad-based rather than a few jurisdictions (`2602.00138v1`, p.21). The limit is that this is an association, not a causal policy estimate: eight regions, observational location data, and a global boom in the same window (section 5.2, p.12), with fundraising missing for about 38 percent of projects and the top 5 percent of offerings carrying about 65 percent of capital (`2602.00138v1`, pp.10-11).

MiCA shows the same pattern on a two-month event window. USDC's aggregate share of USDT-USDC volume moves only from 17.70 to 18.24 percent over the 15 days either side of 1 April 2025, and the aggregate USDC/USDT volume ratio from 0.215 to 0.223, because the aggregate is dominated by venues that kept listing USDT (`2607.09514v1`, pp.2, 5). In the cross-section of 14 exchanges (four regulated-facing, ten global), the USDC share falls 0.243 standard deviations on global venues and rises 0.576 on regulated-facing ones, a difference-in-differences of 0.818 (exchange-clustered p = 0.004) or about six percentage points raw, from pre-event levels near 7 and 46 percent respectively (`2607.09514v1`, pp.8-9). The relative-volume difference-in-differences is 0.537 (p = 0.043) and exact randomisation inference gives p = 0.006 and 0.021 (`2607.09514v1`, pp.9, 19). The decomposition matters for a trader: USDT volume falls 22.5 log points (about 20 percent, p = 0.003) where it is delisted, while USDC volume changes by an insignificant 3.9 percent (`2607.09514v1`, p.17). Downside peg pressure, USDT less USDC, is positive but imprecise at about 0.6 to 2.1 basis points (`2607.09514v1`, p.10). With 14 exchanges and four treated units the authors lead with randomisation inference; the effect is a venue-level reallocation, not a market-level one.

## 2. What an enforcement event does to prices and depth

The SEC's classifications of specific tokens as securities are the cleanest enforcement shock here: 48 event dates covering 117 asset-level classifications, with a market model estimated over [-150, -10] days and Bitcoin as the benchmark (`2412.02452v1`, pp.4, 6). Cumulative abnormal returns across all events deepen from -0.024 before the event, through -0.052 at [0,2], -0.122 at [0,6] and -0.135 at [0,13], to -0.172 at [0,30] (`2412.02452v1`, p.15). The two-day Binance and Coinbase cluster is worse: -0.065 at [0,2] and -0.226 at [0,13] after a positive pre-announcement 0.025, which the authors read as a momentum reversal (`2412.02452v1`, pp.7, 15). The Bittrex enforcement sub-sample reaches -0.252 at [0,30] (`2412.02452v1`, p.15). The Coinbase insider-trading sub-sample has no post-announcement return effect but a significant -0.039 abnormal return on day -1, which the paper flags as consistent with a leak (`2412.02452v1`, p.7).

Liquidity moves with it, in the same direction. Cumulative abnormal volume over 30 days is -4.952 log units for the full sample, -10.848 for the Coinbase insider-trading window, and positive only in the Bittrex window, which peaks at 3.474 at [0,13] (`2412.02452v1`, p.16). Cross-sectional regressions put the day-0 effect on more volatile assets at -0.765, deepening to -5.151 over 30 days, and the [0,2] effect on more illiquid assets at -1.622, with larger market capitalisation also negative at -0.007 (`2412.02452v1`, p.8). Pre-announcement volume coefficients are significant where price coefficients are not: volatility -8.534 and illiquidity -0.410 in the week before the announcement (`2412.02452v1`, p.9). That pattern, volume moving first and the price moving after, is the shape a market participant should expect around any scheduled enforcement or policy calendar, and the paper is explicit that it cannot attribute it to informed trading without trade-level data (`2412.02452v1`, p.9, footnote 12).

## 3. Regime changes rewrite what prices on, not just how much

ChiNext gives a three-period natural experiment on listing regimes. Before the 2013 reform the board ran an approval regime with intraday listing-day curbs; from 2014 it kept approval but capped the listing day at 44 percent up and 36 percent down with plus or minus 10 percent daily limits thereafter; from August 2020 it moved to registration with no cap for the first five trading days and a plus or minus 20 percent daily limit (`2212.11779v1`, pp.3, 8-9). The IPO pipeline was suspended for 438 days to implement the 2013 round (`2212.11779v1`, p.8). Mean initial return goes from 34.08 percent in period 1 (`2212.11779v1`, p.22; 34.41 percent for the earlier 355-IPO sample, p.7), to 334.60 percent in the capped period (`2212.11779v1`, p.23), to 190.60 percent under registration (`2212.11779v1`, p.25). Under the cap, the initial return is measured as the 21st-day return and the intrinsic value as the 42nd-day return, and the capped period is the most underpriced of the three: 95.33 percent of that initial return survives as one-month intrinsic value, leaving only 4.67 percent as overreaction (`2212.11779v1`, p.24). The uncapped registration period reverses the split: 72.67 percent intrinsic value and 27.33 percent overreaction (`2212.11779v1`, p.25).

The composition of the drivers changes with the regime, which is the more usable finding. In period 1 the initial return is explained by pre-listing demand (incremental adjusted R-squared 0.360) plus market condition (0.130), with issuer value contributing 0.010 (`2212.11779v1`, p.21). In the capped period the same return splits between pre-listing demand (0.140) and post-listing demand (0.107) (`2212.11779v1`, p.23). Under registration the initial return is explained entirely by pre-listing issuer value (0.082), with pre-listing demand dropped out altogether (`2212.11779v1`, p.25). In that final period the one-month return regression reaches an adjusted R-squared of 0.843, of which maximum drawdown alone contributes 0.740, and the overreaction regression reaches 0.507 with the listing-day return contributing 0.421 (`2212.11779v1`, pp.17-19). The caveat is scope: one board, one country, three non-overlapping windows with fewer than a year of post-2020 data, and the paper itself proposes the causal comparison as future work.

## 4. Disclosure regimes move investor behaviour

Corporate transparency is a rule-adjacent lever that operates on the order flow itself. Using 11,661 Xueqiu portfolios from June 2016 to May 2023, cleaned to 1,115,839 transactions from 11,323 portfolios and 14,112,512 stock-day observations (`2605.07352v1`, pp.4-5), the baseline disposition effect is an average marginal effect of 0.0226 (Probit) and 0.0220 (Logit) on sale probability when a position moves from loss to gain (`2605.07352v1`, p.7). Grouping by an annual transparency index built from earnings quality, exchange disclosure grades (A to D scored 4 to 1), analyst coverage, forecast accuracy and Big Four audit status (`2605.07352v1`, pp.5-6), the high group has an average marginal effect of 0.0174 against 0.0312 in the low group, a gap of 0.0138 (`2605.07352v1`, p.9). Restricting to first sales narrows the gap to 0.0143 but keeps it (`2605.07352v1`, p.9). The heterogeneity runs the same way: 0.0157 for state-owned enterprises against 0.0141 for non-SOEs, and 0.0125 for high-capitalisation firms against 0.0051 for low-capitalisation ones (`2605.07352v1`, p.10). Mechanism regressions show transparency extends holding periods (coefficient 0.0886) and reduces trade frequency (-0.0005), both at the 1 percent level (`2605.07352v1`, pp.11-12). The evidence is limited to disclosed Chinese A-share accounts, the index is annual so within-year changes are invisible, and Xueqiu exposes only the most recent 200 transactions per portfolio (`2605.07352v1`, pp.4, 13).

## 5. Venue opacity helps or hurts depending on signal precision

The dark-pool paper resolves an empirical literature that was not contradictory but conditional. US dark-pool share of consolidated volume grew from 7.51 percent in 2008 to 16.57 percent in 2015, with about 300 venues in the US including 11 exchanges and 40 active dark pools, and Australian dark liquidity at 26.2 percent of traded value (`1612.08486v1`, pp.2, 6). Execution is rationed rather than guaranteed: reported execution probabilities are 4.11 percent on NYSE-listed and 2.17 percent on Nasdaq-listed names against 31.47 and 26.48 percent for their exchange counterparts, with an older study reporting 2 to 4 percent (`1612.08486v1`, p.8). With noisy heterogeneous signals and free venue choice, informed traders sort by signal strength: strong signals to the exchange for guaranteed execution, moderate signals to the dark pool for price improvement, weak signals out entirely (`1612.08486v1`, p.3). The resulting amplification effect means an added dark pool improves exchange price discovery when information precision is high and impairs it when precision is low, because in the second case the informed-to-uninformed ratio in the lit market falls (`1612.08486v1`, p.3). The paper's policy advice follows directly: monitor dark trading in high-R&D, young, small and less-analysed firms, improve disclosure and enforcement, and be cautious about permitting dark venues where disclosure precision is low (`1612.08486v1`, p.4). It also predicts an inverted-U relation between dark-trade information content and liquidity (`1612.08486v1`, p.4). This is a theory paper: the numbers above are industry facts it cites, not tests, and 55.28 percent of dark-pool volume sat in bank-owned pools against 24.11 percent independent, 13.79 percent market-maker and 6.82 percent consortium-owned (`1612.08486v1`, p.10), with all non-displayed volume at 43.9 percent of the consolidated tape (`1612.08486v1`, p.11).

## 6. Thresholds and settlement rules create discontinuities

Margin rules put a hard edge in the price function. Modelling short sales under Regulation T (initial margin 50 percent) and a maintenance margin of at least 25 percent, with the short seller returning shares rather than posting cash, clearing prices are found by a fixed-point algorithm (`2102.02176v4`, p.2). There is a threshold short-interest ratio below which the clearing price is continuous in outside purchases and above which it jumps: with a linear inverse demand function and market impact b = 2/V, S-star is about 1.23/b, a short-interest ratio near 0.615, and the outside capital needed to trigger a margin call is about 6.43 percent of average daily volume (`2102.02176v4`, p.6). The case studies use an initial margin of 0.45 and maintenance margin of 0.30: GameStop at a short-interest ratio of about 10.2 implies a jump of about 19.17 times the pre-event price, to a clearing price of $344.84 from $18.96; AMC at 4.17 implies 7.11 times, to $19.18; the counterfactual Rite Aid at 8.08 implies 14.93 times, to $140.39 (`2102.02176v4`, pp.6-7). The model is a comparative static with hedged parameters and three hand-picked case studies, not a causal test, but the qualitative point is robust: the discontinuity is in the rule, not in the news.

Prediction markets show that the design lever can be larger than the enforcement lever. Polymarket's five-minute Bitcoin up/down contract resolves against an oracle that averages major spot venues, and the Binance mid sits about 2.5 basis points from it, finishing on the same side of the strike about 85 percent of the time (`2606.31675v1`, p.6). After the contract's 12 February 2026 launch, Binance spot order flow in the final ten seconds of each five-minute cycle runs about 50 percent above its pre-launch level, and about 3.9 times higher in the roughly 6 percent of cycles priced near even (`2606.31675v1`, p.6). Within ten seconds the move reverts by about a quarter in near-even cycles and a tenth elsewhere, which is the signature of a push rather than news (`2606.31675v1`, p.6). Pushes flip the winner in 65 percent of near-even cycles against 41 percent of ordinary ones, and reverse a 90-to-100 percent favourite 34 percent of the time against 1 percent without a push (`2606.31675v1`, p.7). Identifying manipulators by realised profit in the top-decile flow cycles gives 821 wallets out of about 243,000 traders, taking $8.2 million while breaking even elsewhere, with 93 percent of the loss landing on retail accounts that end on the losing side in 65 percent of manipulated cycles against 48 percent normally (`2606.31675v1`, p.7). The fifteen-minute contract, live since 9 October 2025, shows a much attenuated signature, which is the paper's design remedy: lengthen the horizon (`2606.31675v1`, pp.6, 13).

## 7. Fee and priority design selects who trades at all

On-chain clearing is a fee design that changes participation, not just cost. With discrete blocks, a single clearing round per block, and paid-priority ordering, informed traders bid for queue position in a priority gas auction, and only traders above a valuation cutoff participate; the cutoff is strictly increasing in the number of competing informed traders and converges to the top of the valuation support as competition grows (`2605.17425v1`, pp.20, 12). Equilibrium priority fees are increasing in order size and in the number of informed traders and decreasing in pool depth (`2605.17425v1`, p.18). The consequence reverses the traditional result that competition speeds up price discovery: with paid priority, more informed traders means more truncated information, higher adverse selection per unit of liquidity, and eventually market shutdown beyond a critical block time (`2605.17425v1`, pp.4-5). Ethereum slots are twelve seconds or longer by design (`2605.17425v1`, p.10), and the empirical companion pattern is that average liquidity-taking trade size in the most active Ethereum DEX was about $70,000 over 1 July 2021 to 31 December 2023 against about $1,200 for the same pair on Binance, consistent with selection into large aggressive orders (`2605.17425v1`, p.22). The queue-position prediction is checked on 15 Uniswap v3 pools over 2023, where volume falls with position in the block (`2605.17425v1`, p.22). This is a model with two supporting empirical patterns, and it deliberately excludes the case where informed buyers and sellers coexist in one block, where priority has no value (`2605.17425v1`, p.9).

## 8. Mandated risk measures concentrate the risk they measure

The sharpest result in the slice is structural. Any objective, published risk rule that makes some assets cheaper to hold in regulatory capital terms will be gamed toward estimation noise, because a handful of securities will always show a sample standard deviation far below their true one: with 1,000 securities and 60 monthly observations, about ten should show a sample standard deviation under 80 percent of the truth (`1004.1670v4`, p.8). Under the standard Basel calibration, the minimum market-risk capital is 22 times a portfolio's daily standard deviation (3.16 times 2.33 times 3) (`1004.1670v4`, p.5). The empirical test sorts CRSP stocks on their past five-year standard deviation and measures the next five years: the ratios for the five quantile groups average 1.85, 1.17, 1.00, 0.81 and 0.56, so the lowest-volatility decile's realised risk is 85 percent higher than measured (`1004.1670v4`, p.14). More data helps but does not solve it: at 60 periods the smallest tail expectations are 0.76 and 0.71 of true risk for the 1 percent and 0.1 percent tails (`1004.1670v4`, p.23), and at 1,260 periods the 0.1 percent tail is still 0.93 (`1004.1670v4`, p.15), because fat tails offset the extra observations. Daily S&P 500 kurtosis since 1950 is 25.8 against 3 for the normal, and a simulation matching that kurtosis reproduces the low-volatility artifice (`1004.1670v4`, pp.15-16). The paper's broader claim, that any objective rule raises and correlates risk, is an argument rather than a measurement, and its policy menu runs to nationalisation or abolition, so the usable part is the measurement bias itself.

## 9. Who writes the rules, and how fast the rulebook grows

Regulatory capture is presented as a two-player game with mutual influence rather than independent players. Collusion with a transfer t is jointly profitable exactly when B > t > C, the corporation's benefit exceeds the transfer, which exceeds the regulator's cost, and the regulator's cost rises with the amount of influence granted, the risk of detection, the assets at stake and the regulator's integrity, C = (RA + I) (`1310.0057v1`, pp.8-10). The scale argument is the point: benefits are industrial and costs are personal, so the incentive is usually overwhelming. The 2004 American Jobs Creation Act is the worked example, with about $282.7 million of lobbying against $62.5 billion of tax savings, a return above 220 to 1, or roughly $530,000 per member of Congress and $810,000 per favourable vote against salaries of $174,000 and $223,500 (`1310.0057v1`, p.11). The recommended countermeasures are wide distribution of regulatory control, independent research capacity and, above all, transparency of who benefits, since monitoring transfers is easily circumvented while the benefit signature is large and visible (`1310.0057v1`, p.12). This is a model with a single arithmetic case study; nothing here identifies a captured decision.

The other half of the same theme is measurable: the rulebook itself, as described in filings, has grown monotonically. Across more than 165,000 Form 10-K filings from over 34,000 companies, 4.5 million references resolve to 401 distinct federal Acts and 133 agencies (`1612.09244v2`, pp.1, 4). References per filing rose from 8.4 in 1996 to 20.9 in 2006 and 31.7 in 2016, a 237 percent increase (`1612.09244v2`, p.5), with total references moving from about 40,000 in nearly 5,000 filings in 1996 to an annualised rate above 300,000 in 2016 (`1612.09244v2`, p.5). Diversity rose with it: unique Acts per filing went from 3.1 to 7.9, and the average pairwise Hamming distance between company regulatory bitstrings from under 4 to nearly 10, increasing in 18 of 23 years (`1612.09244v2`, pp.6-8). The measurement is mean-field, counts references rather than exposure, and includes boilerplate, but the trend is the one an operator should assume for any compliance-sensitive strategy: the filing burden in 2015 ran to a mean audit fee of $1.8 million and a median of $522,205 (`1612.09244v2`, p.3).

## Caveats

Three of the twelve papers carry no market data at all: the dark-pool sorting result (`1612.08486v1`), the priority-fee auction (`2605.17425v1`) and the capture game (`1310.0057v1`), and the numbers quoted from them are either industry context or model objects. Two more are theory with illustration rather than test: the short-squeeze threshold rests on three hand-picked cases with assumed margin parameters (`2102.02176v4`), and the risk-measure bias pairs a theorem with a CRSP sorting exercise (`1004.1670v4`). The empirical papers are each one market and one period: 48 SEC events in crypto (`2412.02452v1`), a 30-day window around one MiCA date on 14 exchanges with four treated (`2607.09514v1`), one Chinese board across three non-overlapping regimes (`2212.11779v1`), one Chinese retail platform for the disposition effect (`2605.07352v1`), and two months of one prediction-market contract (`2606.31675v1`). The slice disagrees with itself in two places. On venue opacity the theory delivers opposite conclusions from earlier work and resolves them only by conditioning on information precision, so neither "dark pools help" nor "dark pools hurt" is supported unconditionally (`1612.08486v1`, p.3). On regulation's effect the crypto papers split between reallocation and destruction: MiCA leaves aggregates flat while the SEC classification study finds a 17.2 percent fall for named assets, and the two are not reconciled because they measure different objects, a venue mandate against a security designation. Nothing here establishes an optimal rule, nothing measures maker-taker fees, tick size or auction design (that is the companion trading-corpus brief), and no paper in this slice tests a rule change in the markets this platform actually trades: futures, FX, listed options or perpetuals. The prediction-market and on-chain results are the closest to live exposure and are the newest, so they have the least replication.

## Papers read in depth

- `2602.00138v1` - Regulatory Migration to Europe: ICO Reallocation Following U.S. Securities Enforcement (2026). Region-month panel showing a 13.88 ICO per month shift to Europe; observational, eight regions.
- `2607.09514v1` - Does Regulation Bite at Gateways? Evidence from MiCA and Stablecoins (2026). Difference-in-differences on 14 exchanges showing a venue-level shift to USDC with flat aggregates; 30-day window, four treated units.
- `2412.02452v1` - Uncertain Regulations, Definite Impacts: The Impact of the SEC's Regulatory Interventions on Crypto Assets (2024). 48-event study with 17.2 percent 30-day losses and volume drains; no trade-level identification of informed flow.
- `2212.11779v1` - The Impact of Regulation Regime Changes on ChiNext IPOs (2022). Three listing regimes with sharply different pricing drivers; single board, short post-2020 sample.
- `2605.07352v1` - Corporate Transparency and the Disposition Effect (2026). 14.1 million observations linking disclosure quality to a 0.0138 smaller disposition effect; annual index, single platform.
- `1612.08486v1` - Understanding the Impacts of Dark Pools on Price Discovery (2016). Sorting and amplification effects that condition the dark-pool debate; theoretical, no test of its own predictions.
- `2606.31675v1` - Settlement Manipulation in Prediction Markets (2026). Settlement pushes flip near-certain outcomes and 93 percent of the loss falls on retail; one contract, two months.
- `2102.02176v4` - Clearing prices under margin calls and the short squeeze (2021). A threshold short-interest ratio above which clearing prices jump; comparative statics with case-study calibration.
- `2605.17425v1` - The Viability of Blockchain Markets under Discrete Clearing and Paid Priority (2026). Paid priority selects high valuations and can shut a market down; model with two supporting patterns.
- `1004.1670v4` - Any Regulation of Risk Increases Risk (2010). Measurement bias in mandated risk rules and a 1.85 volatility ratio for the lowest decile; broad claim is an argument.
- `1310.0057v1` - Corporations and Regulators: The Game of Influence in Regulatory Capture (2013). A B > t > C condition for collusion with a 220:1 lobbying example; one arithmetic case study, no identification.
- `1612.09244v2` - Measuring the temperature and diversity of the U.S. regulatory ecosystem (2016). 4.5 million 10-K references showing a 237 percent rise in per-filing regulatory references; counts, not exposure.
