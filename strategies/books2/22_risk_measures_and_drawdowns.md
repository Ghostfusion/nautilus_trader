# Risk measures, tails and drawdowns: why tail-sensitive measures are fragile and the axioms still force VaR

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads eight papers from a local harvest of the arXiv `q-fin.GN` (General Finance) category: 3095 records spanning 1997-2026. Twenty-three records match a risk-measures, tails and drawdowns census pattern across 2009-2026, two carry a journal reference, all twenty-three were screened and eight were read in depth. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets: someone who has to decide what the risk engine reports, how a drawdown limit is enforced, how a tail is estimated from a short sample, and what a stress test is allowed to conclude.

## What this covers

The arc runs from what a capital-acceptance rule is allowed to be, through how the number it reports behaves when the sample is short and the tail is heavy, to what leverage does to a finite-horizon terminal payoff. The single most consequential finding is that the natural axioms for an acceptance set - surplus-invariance, law-invariance and conicity (or numeraire-invariance) - force the set to be the one induced by value-at-risk and admit no non-trivial expected-shortfall rule (`1707.05596v2`, p.7-8); the tail-sensitive alternatives that escape this are exactly the ones whose estimates one large loss can move without bound (`2206.02582v2`, p.10).

Selection rule: the paper must state a quantitative, page-anchored claim about estimating or enforcing a risk measure - VaR, expected shortfall, drawdown, a tail index, a copula dependence, or a stress test - that a trading platform can act on; credit-scoring work was kept only where the estimation lesson transfers to market risk.

## The short answer

1. Any surplus-invariant, law-invariant, conic (or numeraire-invariant) and truncation-closed acceptance set must be a VaR acceptance set; no non-trivial expected-shortfall rule satisfies those axioms (`1707.05596v2`, p.7-8).
2. Tail sensitivity and robustness are a trade-off, not a setting: one data point beyond the VaR can make a historical expected-shortfall estimate arbitrarily large, while the VaR itself needs more than (1-alpha)n points to move (`2206.02582v2`, p.10).
3. CoVaR and CoES reduce to a univariate risk measure at a quantile fixed by the copula, and the ratio Delta-CoES/Delta-CoVaR depends only on the tail index, so it can be used to estimate it (`2206.02582v2`, p.5, p.8).
4. Co-risk and risk-contribution measures are not classically backtestable because they are not elicitable; they need joint or multi-objective elicitability tests, not plain violation counts (`2206.02582v2`, p.14).
5. Finite-sample estimates of these measures are biased downward and need at least 2000 observations; at n=500 the bias of Delta-CoES is -2.099 against -0.559 for Delta-CoVaR, and the variance is 15.348 against 3.883 (`2206.02582v2`, p.16).
6. Maximum drawdown is model-free and encodes path information VaR and CVaR discard, but it is permutation-dependent, and a drawdown-ranked portfolio can be less risky in every reported measure (`1403.8125v4`, p.3, p.8).
7. A VaR number inherits the price-probability definition behind it: the frequency-based (constant-volume) probability is not the market-based one, and market price volatility depends on trade-value and volume volatilities and their correlation (`2101.08559v3`, p.3, p.7).
8. Forecasting price volatility at horizon T requires forecasting the first and second moments of trade values and volumes at T, so usable-horizon VaR forecasts are limited to Gaussian approximations (`2101.08559v3`, p.8-9).
9. Rare-event probabilities need an asymmetric link with a tail parameter: GEV and its non-parametric extension beat logistic regression out of sample on low-default data (`1412.5351v1`, p.7, p.24-25).
10. Missing values in risk data are often informative: weights-of-evidence coding beat multiple imputation for most models and raised the count of significant predictors from 11 to 20 in the UK GEV case (`1412.5351v1`, p.14, p.15).
11. Copula-based systemic measures are dependence-consistent only under the inequality conditioning, and the estimate depends on the copula family: Clayton is the most conservative of the four tested (`2203.10777v1`, p.6, p.14).
12. On a finite-horizon binary payoff, any leverage above one leaves a terminal account shortfall if the position is carried to the adverse outcome, independent of the pre-resolution mark path (`2605.10400v2`, p.7).

## 1. What a capital-acceptance rule is allowed to be

`1707.05596v2` frames the problem as a regulator choosing an acceptance set for a firm's capital position X, the assets net of liability, where the positive part is shareholder surplus and the negative part is the option to default (`1707.05596v2`, p.2-3). The Basel II test accepts a position when its 99% VaR is non-positive (p.2), and the Basel III market-risk test moves to 97.5% expected shortfall from 1 January 2018 (p.5). The central result is Theorem 2.1: a surplus-invariant, law-invariant, conic and truncation-closed acceptance set must satisfy A^-_alpha subset A subset A^0_alpha, or equal A^+_alpha, and if conicity is strengthened to numeraire-invariance it must equal A^-_alpha or A^+_alpha (p.7). These are the sets induced by VaR at a level alpha (p.7). A set satisfying those properties cannot be induced by expected shortfall unless it is trivial, i.e. empty or everything (p.8). Numeraire-invariance implies conicity (p.5), so the stronger currency-independence axiom only tightens the same conclusion.

`0904.0870v1` supplies the vocabulary. The Artzner coherence axioms are monotonicity, positive homogeneity, translation invariance and sub-additivity (p.8-9); VaR fails sub-additivity (p.9); CVaR is the expected loss given that the VaR is exceeded (p.9). The survey also records that VaR entered industry practice at JP Morgan in 1994 and was written into Basel capital rules in 1995 (p.6), that the usual confidence levels are 0.90, 0.95 and 0.99 (p.7), and that there are four implementation routes - historical simulation, parametric (including extreme-value), Monte Carlo and variance-covariance (p.7-8). The operator consequence is blunt: the axioms that protect liability holders select VaR. Choosing expected shortfall is choosing to relax one of them, and the brief should say which one.

## 2. Tail sensitivity versus robustness

`2206.02582v2` makes the trade-off quantitative. It shows that CoVaR, CoES and MES can be written as a univariate risk measure evaluated at a quantile omega determined by the copula: CoVaR is VaR_omega of Y, CoES is ES_omega of Y, and MES is the CoES case with alpha equal to zero (p.5). Under a generalised-Pareto tail, the ratio Delta-CoES/Delta-CoVaR depends only on the tail index (p.8), which is what makes a tail-index estimator possible. Tail sensitivity is the point of ES: its sensitivity function is linear in the size of a loss beyond VaR_omega, whereas the CoVaR sensitivity function is piecewise constant, so one observation beyond the quantile can make the ES estimate arbitrarily large while the VaR estimate would need more than (1-alpha)n points to shift (p.10). The same linearity has a downside the paper states explicitly: Delta-CoES reacts negatively to intermediate losses between VaR_omega and VaR_alpha, so a falling estimate can signal building risk rather than reduced risk (p.10). MES is the alpha=0 special case and is not suitable for measuring extreme risk or tail dependence (p.5, p.8). Under aggregation, a weighted sum of positions has a tail exponent equal to the maximum of the components' exponents (p.11); Delta-CoES stays bounded by the weighted average of ESs while Delta-CoVaR can exceed the weighted average of VaRs (p.11). The paper proposes a time-varying mixture of VaR and ES, the SlideVaR, to dial prudence up and down (p.12). It also notes that ES-based backtests are more informative than VaR backtests because they test the severity of violations as well as their number (p.14).

## 3. Estimating tails and co-risk measures on short samples

The simulation in `2206.02582v2` is the cleanest statement of sample-size requirements in the slice. Data are drawn from a Gumbel copula with dependence parameter 2.22 and t-distributed margins with 3 degrees of freedom; the true Delta-CoVaR at the 95% level is 5.071827, the true Delta-CoES is 7.383257, their ratio is 1.455739, the tail index is 0.3130637 and omega is 0.9974727 (p.13). Across 10,000 datasets of sizes 500 to 20,000 (p.13-14), both estimates are biased downward in finite samples: at n=500 the bias is -0.559 for Delta-CoVaR and -2.099 for Delta-CoES, falling to -0.011 and -0.045 at n=20,000 (p.16). The variance at n=500 is 3.883 and 15.348, falling to 0.131 and 0.989 at n=20,000 (p.16). The paper's own threshold is at least 2000 observations for reliable estimates (p.16), and it warns that at these quantiles omega is so close to its comonotonic bound that a small error in omega becomes a large error in the co-risk estimate (p.16). On the empirical side, the dataset is 6,182,652 daily observations over 18,613 days and 1,564 US financial firms from 1970 to 2020 (p.15); tail-index estimates fall in [0.3296567, 0.3971911], around one third (p.18); daily Delta-CoVaR averages 0.04865 and Delta-CoES 0.05937, a ratio of 1.236, against 0.10118 and 0.13656, a ratio of 1.4099, weekly (p.18); omega sits close to its comonotonic upper bound of 0.9975 (p.18). The median number of observations behind a weekly CoES estimate was 5, against 24 daily (p.18), and across the 73x72 = 5256 institution pairs the network Delta-CoES standard deviation is almost twice that of Delta-CoVaR (p.19). The rolling estimate for Lehman Brothers spikes months before bankruptcy and again five days before it (p.22).

`2203.10777v1` validates its copula estimators on 10,000 simulated observations with 100 repetitions: average violation rates are 0.0489-0.0506 against a 5% nominal level and 0.0082-0.0102 against 1% (p.18). On five cryptocurrencies over 2,283 daily observations from 1 September 2015 to 30 November 2021, covering 64.54% of market capitalisation (p.19), the realised VaR violation rates run 4.78% to 6.00% against the 5% target, where 114.1 violations were expected (p.21).

`1412.5351v1` is the rare-event case. Its generalised-extreme-value link function replaces the symmetric logistic link, with a tail parameter tau that recovers the Gumbel distribution as tau tends to zero (p.7). The data are 39,785 UK and 154,934 Italian small and medium enterprises observed in 2010 and resolved in 2011 (p.10-11), split 70% training and 30% control (p.13). Out of sample with weights-of-evidence coding, the UK GEV model gives MAE+ 0.784, MSE+ 0.722 and AUC 0.741 against logistic 0.798, 0.705 and 0.731, while the non-parametric BGEVA gives 0.782, 0.702 and 0.722 (p.24); for Italy BGEVA reaches MAE+ 0.781, MSE+ 0.651 and AUC 0.824 against GEV AUC 0.813 (p.25). The fitted tail parameters are -0.41 for Italy and -0.9 for the UK (p.25). The transferable estimation lesson is the missing-value result: weights-of-evidence coding beat multiple imputation for most models (p.14) and increased the number of significant variables from 11 to 20 in the UK GEV and BGEVA case (p.15), and only number of directors and solvency ratio were significant in all sixteen models (p.15). Missingness in risk data carries information.

## 4. Copula dependence and what it does to the number

`2206.02582v2` shows why the copula is the right object: it carries the entire dependence structure, so the conditional problem reduces to a marginal risk measure at a copula-determined quantile (p.5). Joint exceedances have an extreme-value copula, and the Gumbel copula is the convenient one-parameter choice that ranges from independence to comonotonicity (p.9). Under independence the delta measures are zero; under comonotonicity they are maximal (p.6). If the copula is symmetric and the margins are equal, the measures are exchangeable, which halves the estimation grid (p.6). For estimation, the empirical beta copula has the best finite-sample mean-squared error among the popular non-parametric copula estimators (p.12).

`2203.10777v1` adds the practical caveats. Conditioning on X equal to its VaR is not dependence-consistent; conditioning on X at or below its VaR is (p.6). CoVaR decreases monotonically with Kendall's tau for all four copulas tested, but the Clayton copula decreases fastest and is the most conservative, while Gumbel and Gaussian decrease slowly (p.14). With standard normal margins the independence and perfect-dependence limits at the 5% level are -1.645 and at the 1% level -2.807 (p.14). The multi-institution MCoVaR is not monotone in dependence, with a minimum around tau=0.2 for Clayton and 0.75 for Gumbel, whereas the vulnerability version VCoVaR is monotone (p.14-15). Empirically every conditional measure sits below the corresponding univariate VaR, and Bitcoin and Litecoin primarily affect each other (p.22). `0904.0870v1` supplies the textbook form of Sklar's theorem and the warning that extreme-value dependence cannot be captured by correlation, only by a copula (p.10).

For joint exceedances `2206.02582v2` works with the Gumbel copula and shows the risk measures inherit the same generalised-Pareto tail behaviour, with the dependence parameter entering the equation that fixes the quantile: at the independence value the contribution measures are zero, and as the parameter grows they approach their comonotonic maximum (p.9). The estimator consequence is that a copula fitted to the body of the data fixes the tail number even though the tail was never used to fit it.

## 5. Drawdown as a risk measure and a ranking rule

`1403.8125v4` treats maximum drawdown as the worst peak-to-trough loss and argues it is more insightful than VaR or CVaR, directly computable from a return series, model-free, and sensitive to the order of returns in a way a distribution is not (p.3). That last property cuts both ways: a random permutation of a time series produces a different maximum drawdown, so it is path-dependent by construction (p.3). The empirical work uses KOSPI 200 from January 2003 to December 2012, SPDR US sector ETFs from January 1999 to December 2012, and the S&P 500 from January 1993 to December 2012 (p.6), with six-month estimation windows for monthly momentum and six-week windows for weekly contrarian portfolios and ten ranking groups (p.6). The risk model is ARMA(1,1)-GARCH(1,1) with classical tempered stable innovations whose tail index lies in (0,2) (p.7).

The results show that drawdown is a usable ranking criterion, not just a reporting statistic. In the KOSPI 200 weekly contrarian test the recovery-ranked long-short portfolio returns 0.1455% per week at 1.7567% volatility against the benchmark cumulative-return portfolio's 0.0731% at 2.8417% (p.8-9), and the recovery portfolio shows the smallest daily VaR and CVaR in the table, 1.149% and 1.391% (p.8). In the KOSPI 200 monthly momentum test the cumulative-return-minus-drawdown portfolio returns 1.4330% at 7.0357% against the benchmark's 1.3305% at 6.8258% (p.10). In the ETF monthly test the composite drawdown-recovery portfolio returns 0.1724% at 3.5652% against the benchmark's 0.1174% at 3.5522% (p.15). In the S&P 500 weekly test the recovery long-short portfolio returns 0.0872% at 1.7852% (p.17), and its maximum drawdown is the lowest of all strategies, about 40% below the benchmark (p.17). The point for the platform is that a portfolio selected to have a shallow drawdown also had thinner tails and lower VaR, so drawdown is not merely a cosmetic overlay.

## 6. Where the VaR number comes from

`2101.08559v3` attacks the input rather than the measure. VaR asks how much can be lost at a given cumulative probability and horizon (p.2-3), and the RiskMetrics normal approximation uses 1.65 standard deviations for a 5% one-sided event (p.3). The frequency-based price probability counts trades at each price and therefore describes the case of constant trade volumes (p.3, p.7). The market-based alternative defines the price of a trade as its value divided by its volume (p.4) and shows that market-based price volatility depends on the volatility of trade values, the volatility of volumes, and their correlation (p.7). To forecast price volatility at a horizon T you must forecast the first and second moments of trade values and volumes at T (p.8); the paper concludes that usable-horizon predictions will be limited to Gaussian approximations and that even the volatility forecast remains uncertain (p.9). `0904.0870v1` lists the four VaR implementation routes - historical simulation, parametric with extreme-value theory, Monte Carlo, and variance-covariance - as the places where this choice enters (p.7-8). The operator consequence is that the sampling convention is part of the number: a trade-weighted volatility and a time-weighted volatility are different inputs to the same VaR formula. The survey adds that the parametric route can call on extreme-value theory to estimate the tail quantile, so the tail assumption and the VaR number are not separable choices (`0904.0870v1`, p.7).

## 7. Stress testing leverage on a terminal payoff

`2605.10400v2` is the only stress-testing paper in the slice with a closed-form result and a replay. A synthetic long of x units entered at price p0 with collateral x p0 / L has terminal equity -x p0 (1 - 1/L) when the binary claim pays zero, so any leverage above one leaves an account shortfall if the position survives to resolution (p.7). The worked example is x=1000, p0=0.60, L=5, collateral 120: a mark to 0.90 shows equity 420, and resolution NO leaves -480, identical to the entry-to-terminal calculation 120 minus 600 (p.2). The mark path changes when the risk is visible and whether a liquidation rule fires, not the terminal amount (p.2, p.7). A second result, the boundary funding trilemma, shows that no bounded transfer can impose uniform pressure proportional to relative basis arbitrarily close to zero or one, and that basis-only funding vanishes for a fixed relative error near the boundary (p.8).

The replay is deliberately negative. The window is 168 hours from 21 to 27 April 2026; the raw archive holds 110,828 market identifiers, 61,087 pass stratified enrichment, 13,298 pass the adequacy gates and 13,115 enter the resolution-zone denominator, with sports at 51.1% of the usable sample (p.6). Two structural diagnostics pass: the pooled median boundary-depth ratio is 1.72 against a 1.5 floor with class medians 1.69 to 1.75, and the median absolute final-hour jump is 0.50 against a 0.10 floor (p.10). Three of five materiality tests then fail (p.12): at L=5 the dynamic configuration produced roughly 6% higher liquidation incidence rather than the pre-specified 30% reduction (p.11); pooled hypothetical drawdown fell only 5.1%, from 545.1M to 517.1M, against a 50% floor (p.11); and the staged halt cut final-hour liquidations by 80.4% while terminal account-shortfall incidence rose 2.4% (p.11). The paper distinguishes account shortfall from exchange bad debt (p.5) and labels its plan pre-specified rather than pre-registered (p.7).

## Caveats

Several papers here cannot support a trading conclusion. `0904.0870v1` is a 2009 review with no data. `2101.08559v3` is a theoretical account of price probability with no empirical calibration and no estimator. `1412.5351v1` is credit-default data, not market returns, for a single year (2010 to 2011), so its rare-event results transfer as an estimation lesson only. `1403.8125v4` covers three equity universes over 1993-2012 with no costs, slippage or capacity analysis, and its risk model is fitted on the same overlapping portfolios it evaluates. `2206.02582v2` reports one dataset (CRSP, 1970-2020) and its tail-index estimator can return negative values when the true index is near zero. `2203.10777v1` uses five cryptocurrencies over six years of daily closes. `2605.10400v2` is one seven-day Polymarket window, sports-heavy, and its replay holds the price path fixed, so it does not identify the equilibrium after traders respond; it explicitly disclaims deployment safety, and three of its five pre-specified tests failed. The slice disagrees with itself on method: `1707.05596v2` says an intuitive acceptance set must be VaR, while `2206.02582v2` and `2203.10777v1` build their analysis on expected shortfall precisely because VaR is tail-blind, and `0904.0870v1` treats VaR as the industry default. No paper here establishes a profitable strategy or a safe leverage limit; the numbers are about measurement, axioms and mechanism.

## Papers read in depth

- `0904.0870v1` - Risk Measures in Quantitative Finance (2009). A review of the coherence axioms, VaR implementation routes and CVaR; no data, so its value is definitional.
- `1403.8125v4` - Maximum drawdown, recovery, and momentum (2014). Shows a drawdown-ranked portfolio can be less risky in every reported measure; single-period equity universes with no costs.
- `1412.5351v1` - A comparative analysis of the UK and Italian small businesses using Generalised Extreme Value models (2014). Rare-event estimation with an asymmetric GEV link and informative missingness; credit data, one year.
- `1707.05596v2` - Surplus-Invariant, Law-Invariant, and Conic Acceptance Sets Must be the Sets Induced by Value-at-Risk (2017). Proves the axiomatic collapse to VaR; a pure theorem with no thresholds.
- `2101.08559v3` - To VaR, or Not to VaR, That is the Question (2021). Argues the price-probability definition, not the formula, limits VaR; theoretical with no calibration.
- `2203.10777v1` - Vulnerability-CoVaR: Investigating the Crypto-market (2022). Copula estimation of systemic measures validated by simulation; five coins, daily closes.
- `2206.02582v2` - Making heads or tails of systemic risk measures (2022). The central paper: copula representation, tail sensitivity, robustness, finite-sample bias and a tail-index estimator; one US equity dataset.
- `2605.10400v2` - Resolution-Aware Perpetual Futures on Binary Prediction Markets (2026). Proves any leverage above one leaves a terminal shortfall and reports a mostly negative mechanical stress test; one week of prediction-market data.
