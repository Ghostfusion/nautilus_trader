# Stylized facts, tails and scaling: why predictable order flow still leaves a random walk

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads twelve papers from a local harvest of the arXiv `q-fin.GN` category: 3095 records spanning 1997-2026, of which 93 match a stylized-facts-and-scaling census pattern across 1998-2026 and 35 carry a journal reference. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets - someone who has to choose distributional assumptions for the risk engine, calibration targets for the backtest engine, and admissible null models for research. The slice runs from a 150-year multi-asset test of the classical stylized facts, through order-flow microstructure and cryptocurrency scaling, to the estimation problems that decide whether a measured exponent is real.

## What this covers

The arc runs from which regularities survive a century and a half, through how fragile the measurement of those regularities is, to what mechanism produces them. The single most consequential finding is that the market's apparent randomness is a consequence of the concavity of price impact: `2608.00988v1` proves that when market-order flow carries long-range correlation, a square-root impact law (exponent at most 0.5) is the necessary and sufficient condition for prices to stay diffusive, so a simulator or execution model that assumes linear impact manufactures predictable returns the real market does not have.

## The short answer

1. Predictable order flow and a random-walk price are compatible only if price impact is sufficiently concave: with order-sign autocorrelation decaying as a power law, an impact exponent below 0.5 is necessary and sufficient for diffusion (`2608.00988v1`).
2. The same square-root law generates the inverse-cubic law, with price-change tail exponent equal to the metaorder-length exponent divided by the impact exponent, near three for the observed range (`2608.00988v1`).
3. Order-flow long memory is micro-founded and quantitatively parametrised: about a quarter of traders are order splitters but they issue about 80% of market orders, and the macroscopic memory exponent equals the microscopic metaorder exponent minus one (`2301.13505v2`).
4. Fat tails are largely a conditioning artifact: ordered by an implied-volatility stress proxy, 83% of random equity windows look non-normal against 17% of stress-ordered windows (`1310.4538v2`).
5. Most classical stylized facts survive 150 years and a dozen markets, but the absence of return autocorrelation does not hold for cryptocurrencies (`2504.08611v1`).
6. Tail and kurtosis estimates are not properties of an asset but of a window: excess kurtosis for the DAX and the S&P over overlapping histories differs by more than a factor of four (`1912.02684v1`).
7. Conventional long-memory tests over-reject when multifractality is present; with a joint test, eleven of twelve currency pairs show no long memory at all (`1601.00903v1`).
8. For interbank node sizes and degrees, log-normal and stretched-exponential distributions beat the power law over the full range, and the power law only wins on the truncated tail (`1409.3738v2`).
9. Persistent attention series do not make tradable cross-correlations: Google search volume for Dow components is strongly persistent, with Hurst exponents between 0.8 and 1.1, yet its correlation with traded volume and volatility is weak, scale-dependent and non-universal (`1502.00225v1`).
10. Long memory is regime-dependent: European corporate-bond Hurst exponents rose after 2008 while equity Hurst exponents fell below one half (`1605.06700v1`).
11. Cryptocurrencies do not share one stochastic process: generalized Hurst exponents span 0.36 to 0.73 across 84 coins, with large coins behaving like fractional Brownian motion and small coins strongly multifractal (`2003.09720v2`).
12. Standard entropy pooling can fail outright under fat-tailed priors; the workable replacement is polynomial divergence, equivalent to relative Tsallis or Renyi entropy (`1203.0643v1`).

## 1. Which stylized facts survive 150 years, and which one breaks

`2504.08611v1` tests the eleven Cont stylized facts on roughly 150 years of monthly data from 1871, ten years of daily futures and rates, minute futures from 2012 to 2022, and minute Bitcoin and Ethereum from 2017 to 2022 (p.5). It supports seven of the eleven outright (p.3). The fact that fails is the first one: log-return autocorrelation falls inside the 95% band within roughly 20 minutes for traditional markets, but not for cryptocurrencies, which retain slower decay (p.6). The autocorrelation of absolute returns decays as a power law with fitted exponents 0.38 for monthly, 0.45 for daily and 0.33 for minute traditional data, against 0.28 for minute crypto (p.7). Aggregational Gaussianity holds under an Anderson-Darling test, but the statistic falls from 4064.6 at a two-minute horizon to 0.68 at 1024 minutes, and from 16580.3 to 16.6 for crypto, which the authors note may simply reflect that fewer tail events are observed over longer windows (pp.34, 27). Contemporaneous volume-volatility correlation is positive everywhere but weak and heterogeneous: Pearson 0.093 for Bitcoin and 0.29 for Ethereum (p.31), and 0.059 for Nikkei futures against 0.47 for crude oil (p.32). The practical reading is that "the stylized facts" are a useful checklist and a poor calibration target: seven of eleven is the honest score for a generic model, and the exceptions cluster exactly where an operator trades a young, continuously-open venue.

## 2. The measurement is as fragile as the fact

`1912.02684v1` recomputes excess kurtosis, Hill tail estimates and autocorrelations on DAX, Dow Jones and S&P data and shows that the numbers move with the sample, not just the asset: excess kurtosis is 7.49 for the DAX over 1959-2018, 19.95 for the Dow over 1896-2018 and 32.50 for the S&P over 1789-2018, a spread of more than a factor of four between the DAX and the S&P on the same measure (p.5). The five-percent Hill estimates for the same three series are 2.86, 2.62 and 2.38 (p.5), and the absolute-return autocorrelation at lag ten is 0.206, 0.322 and 0.335 over a common 2008-2018 window (p.6). The authors also report only order 1e-2 differences between Stooq and Yahoo for the same series, so the dispersion is mostly horizon and index, not vendor (p.5). `1601.00903v1` shows a sharper version of the same problem: when multifractality is present, the conventional test of no long memory is oversized, rejecting at 16.7% instead of 10% for a series with true exponent 0.5 and multifractal parameter 1.04 at 2500 observations (p.9). Applied to 5000 daily returns for twelve currencies against the dollar from 1993 to 2012, the joint test rejects the null of no long memory and no multifractality for eleven of twelve - but the rejection comes entirely from multifractality, with no evidence of long memory in returns (p.10). Any "the market has memory" claim built on a standalone Hurst estimate should be treated as unproven until a joint test is run.

`1912.02684v1` also notes that at least thirty stylized facts have been documented in the literature (p.4) and frames agent-based market models by three universal building blocks: agent design, a price-adjustment mechanism, and an environment that couples agents beyond the price (p.9). That taxonomy is a reminder that matching the stylized facts is a weak validation criterion when the fact set is open-ended and its individual members are as sample-sensitive as this section shows.

## 3. Tails are a mixture, not a property of the increment

`1310.4538v2` proposes that drift and volatility are functions of an observable market-stress level, proxied by an implied-volatility index, so that the returns are locally normal but the time series samples many different normal distributions (p.3). On S&P 500 and ten-year bond data from 1988 to 2012 (p.4), a Shapiro-Wilk test flags 83% of randomly ordered equity windows as non-normal at the 95% level, against 17% when the same returns are ordered by stress; for bonds the numbers are 44% and 22% (p.6). The practical payoff is in the tail: the model assigns probability 8e-4 to a one-day decline of at least 9%, roughly once in five years, where a naive normal Value-at-Risk gives 2e-14; for the October 1987 fall of 22.9% the two estimates are 9e-8 and 4e-82, a gap of 74 orders of magnitude (pp.9-10). Stock-bond correlation also flips sign with stress - negative when equities are stressed, positive when they are calm (p.11) - so diversification is regime-dependent. This is the clearest statement in the slice that a heavy-tail exponent is not an intrinsic constant but the shadow of an unmodelled conditioning variable.

## 4. Power law, or something that fits the whole range

`1409.3738v2` beats the reflex of labelling every heavy tail a power law. It fits five candidate distributions to twelve network measures of the Russian interbank market from August 1998 to October 2004 (p.1). Every distribution is heavy-tailed and the fat tail averages about 20% of the observations (p.10), with a truncated power law the best tail fit; its exponent is consistently near two, ranging from about 1.49 to 2.20 across measures (p.10). But the truncated power law is rarely significantly better than the log-normal or stretched exponential, and over the full range the log-normal wins for assets, capital, loan size and exposures while the stretched exponential wins for degrees (pp.1, 13). Network clustering averages 0.198 and the average shortest path is about 3, with both moving sharply around the two crises (p.6). The operational point is that choosing the tail model is a model-selection decision with a visible cost: a power law that merely ties with a log-normal over the bulk should not be carried into a risk limit as though it had been established.

The same discipline appears in `cond-mat/0311585v2`, a reanalysis of a published result. The frequency of recession durations across seventeen economies from 1870 to 1994 fits an exponential with RMS error 4.67 far better than a power law with RMS error 11.86 (p.2), and the power law only becomes the better description once the one-year recessions - 61% of the data - are dropped (p.3). A power-law claim that depends on deleting most of the sample is not a statement about the process; it is a statement about where the tail was cut.

## 5. Cryptocurrencies are several stochastic models, not one

`1708.04532v1` finds that daily Bitcoin returns from 2011 to 2017 have a standard deviation of 6.24% against 0.57% for the euro and the pound, with negative skew and excess kurtosis of 25.57 (p.8). Detrended fluctuation analysis with a sliding 500-point window shows the Hurst exponent persistently above 0.5 until about 2014 and then stabilising near 0.5, while the euro and pound stay inside 0.45-0.55 throughout (pp.8, 13). At intraday sampling the kurtosis remains extreme and falls only slowly, from 107.5 at five-hour sampling to 53.0 at twelve-hour sampling (p.11). `2003.09720v2` generalises this to 84 cryptocurrencies over 790 daily observations from January 2018 to March 2020 (p.5) and finds the market is not one process: the generalized Hurst exponent at order one averages 0.515 but spans 0.356 to 0.733 (p.5), the largest coins sit between 0.5 and 0.6 and are consistent with fractional Brownian motion, while the smallest coins show a multifractal spectrum width rising from 0.4640 in the first volume quartile to 1.0162 in the fourth (p.8). Shuffling removes some but not all multifractality, and the residual is outside the shuffled confidence interval for 29 of 84 coins, concentrated in the smallest quartile where 57% reject (p.8). The conclusion the authors draw is the one that matters for cross-asset platforms: one model does not fit all, so a single crypto calibration is an averaging error, not a simplification (p.10).

`1708.04532v1` also samples Bitcoin intraday across 5 to 12 hours per bar from March 2013 to August 2016, from a raw set of 9540332 transactions (p.7). The Hurst profile is essentially the same at every sampling interval, and the same post-2014 stabilisation towards 0.5 appears there, with the authors unable to tie it to any fundamental driver (pp.11, 13). For a venue-agnostic platform this is the important asymmetry: a crypto calibration can become wrong on a multi-year timescale rather than decaying gracefully.

## 6. Predictable order flow, unpredictable price: the square-root reconciliation

`2301.13505v2` gives the microscopic measurement. Using nine years of Tokyo Stock Exchange data with account identifiers, from January 2012 to December 2020 (p.2), it clusters traders into order-splitting and random types and finds that typically a quarter of all traders are splitters but that they issue about 80% of all market orders (p.3). The metaorder-length complementary CDF obeys a power law whose exponent is typically between one and two - about 1.62 for Toyota in 2020 - and the measured macroscopic sign-autocorrelation exponent matches the prediction gamma = alpha - 1 across markets (pp.3, 4). The prefactor carries information too: an estimator built from it recovers the number of splitting traders, though only as a lower bound (p.4). `2608.00988v1` then explains why this predictability does not show up in prices. Adding a nonlinear impact law of exponent delta to the same order-flow model, the mean squared displacement grows linearly in time when the metaorder exponent exceeds twice the impact exponent, and only superdiffusively otherwise, and the return autocorrelation decays with exponent alpha - 2 delta + 1 (pp.10, 11). Since delta at most 0.5 covers every observed alpha between one and two, the square-root law is exactly what suppresses the impact of institutional metaorders and preserves diffusion (p.2). The same framework reproduces the inverse-cubic law with exponent equal to alpha divided by delta, near the observed value of three (pp.14-15). For anyone building an impact model or a synthetic price generator, this is the load-bearing result: get the concavity wrong and the simulation becomes exploitable by construction.

`2301.13505v2` also builds an estimator for the number of order-splitting traders from the observable autocorrelation prefactor: it correlates with the true count but systematically underestimates it, giving a lower bound (p.4). The authors argue that the pair of microscopic parameters - the metaorder tail exponent and the splitter count - measures a latent illiquidity that spread, depth and average impact do not capture, because large investors are waiting for liquidity to replenish during their own split executions (p.4). That is a prototype for a venue-characteristic metric rather than a trading signal.

`2608.00988v1` further shows the square-root model needs no extra machinery to also produce the inverse-cubic law and volatility clustering: adding only an exponential inter-metaorder resting time yields a price-change tail exponent equal to alpha divided by delta and a volatility autocorrelation decaying with an exponent between zero and one (pp.14-15). The volatility-clustering result is numerical, not proved, and the paper does not claim otherwise.

## 7. Long memory is state-dependent and micro-founded

`1605.06700v1` measures rolling Hurst exponents for corporate-bond and equity indices in six EU countries from 1999 to 2015, with a 500-point sliding window (p.6). Corporate-bond returns are not thin-tailed: excess kurtosis reaches 551.7 for Belgium, 162.5 for Finland and 120.7 for Italy (p.7). Over the whole period the mean bond Hurst exponent is 0.5573 for Italy, 0.5462 for Austria and 0.4652 for Luxembourg (p.8), generally above 0.5. Splitting at the Lehman bankruptcy, bond market memory increases sharply - Italy from 0.5153 to 0.6468, France from 0.5342 to 0.5991 - while equity memory falls below one half everywhere, Austria from 0.5546 to 0.4956 (p.9). The authors attribute this to a flight to quality and liquidity that concentrates trading in fewer instruments during stress (p.11). Read against section 3, this is a second reason not to treat a Hurst exponent as a constant: it is a state variable, and a strategy that assumes a fixed degree of trendiness across regimes is mis-specified in exactly the periods when it will be traded hardest.

## 8. Two measurement notes for alt data and for sizing

`1502.00225v1` measures daily Google search series for the component stocks of the Dow Jones Industrial Average from 2004 to 2013, reconstructed to 2516 observations (p.7). The search series are strongly persistent, with detrended-fluctuation Hurst exponents between 0.8 and 1.1 for every reliable stock (pp.1, 8). The temptation is to read that persistence as a signal, but the cross-correlations say otherwise: most detrended cross-correlation coefficients fall between -0.2 and 0.2, they are larger for traded volume than for volatility, they concentrate at short and medium scales, and the sign varies by stock, so the author concludes there is no universal relationship (p.9). The largest average coefficient reported is about 0.23, for Walt Disney traded volume (p.18), which is not a deployable edge before costs. For an alt-data feed the lesson is to test significance per instrument and per scale rather than to assume a persistent source is an informative one.

`1203.0643v1` is a theoretical caution that pairs with the empirical ones. Finding the probability measure closest to a prior while satisfying a moment or marginal view - the machinery behind entropy pooling and option calibration - has a closed-form exponential-twist solution under relative entropy (Kullback-Leibler divergence), but that solution may not exist when the underlying variable is fat-tailed, as it is for a Pareto or log-normal prior (p.8). Replacing relative entropy with a polynomial divergence, equivalent to relative Tsallis or Renyi entropy, restores existence and uniqueness under stated conditions (p.9), and a view that a portfolio return is fat-tailed then propagates that tail to every correlated asset (p.19). The engineering consequence is that a scenario-weighting or view-updating layer built on KL divergence can silently return no solution, or a solution concentrated on a truncation point, under precisely the distributions this slice keeps finding.

## Caveats

The read set is mixed in kind and several members cannot support a trading conclusion. `1203.0643v1` is a pure existence and uniqueness result with no market data; `1502.00225v1` correlates a reconstructed Google Trends sample from 2004 to 2013 with volume and volatility; `cond-mat/0311585v2` reanalyses a single small frequency table for macro recession durations; `1409.3738v2` studies a bilateral interbank exposure network, an institutional structure rather than a tradable instrument; `1310.4538v2` covers only US equity and Treasury data from 1988 to 2012 and depends on an implied-volatility stress proxy; `2301.13505v2` and `2608.00988v1` rest on one exchange (the Tokyo Stock Exchange) and on a theoretical proof of diffusion rather than an out-of-sample test. The cryptocurrency evidence is pre-2020 and spans two short, structurally different windows, so its own authors warn that the market changes character over time. The slice also disagrees with itself in instructive ways: `1912.02684v1` and `2504.08611v1` report heavy unconditional tails but also aggregational convergence to normality and clear heterogeneity across indices, while `1310.4538v2` argues the tails are a conditioning artifact and `1601.00903v1` argues many apparent long-memory findings are multifractality mislabelled by an oversized test. No paper here establishes a universal tail exponent, a stable Hurst exponent across regimes, or a tradable edge; the recurring result is that the distributional parameters are state- and window-dependent, and that the one structural constant - the concavity of impact - is the thing worth encoding explicitly.

## Papers read in depth

- `2504.08611v1` - International Financial Markets Through 150 Years: Evaluating Stylized Facts (2025). Tests eleven classical facts over 150 years and a dozen markets; supports seven of eleven and finds crypto breaks the return-autocorrelation fact; its own caveat is that aggregational Gaussianity may be a sampling artifact.
- `1912.02684v1` - Stylized Facts and Agent-Based Modeling (2019). Recomputes kurtosis, Hill and autocorrelation for DAX, Dow and S&P and shows extreme horizon/index sensitivity; a review, so its value is the dispersion, not any single number.
- `2301.13505v2` - Can we infer microscopic financial information from the long memory in market-order flow? (2023). First quantitative validation of the Lillo-Mike-Farmer prediction gamma = alpha - 1 on nine years of TSE account-level data; single exchange.
- `2608.00988v1` - Exactly solvable model for the diffusive price-dynamics paradox under long-range correlated market-order flow (2026). Proves square-root impact is sufficient for diffusion and reproduces the inverse-cubic law; a model result with no out-of-sample test.
- `1708.04532v1` - Some stylized facts of the Bitcoin market (2017). Rolling Hurst exponents for Bitcoin, euro and pound show Bitcoin's persistence fading to 0.5 by 2014; short and dated sample.
- `2003.09720v2` - One model is not enough: heterogeneity in cryptocurrencies' multifractal profiles (2020). Across 84 coins, large ones look fractional-Brownian and small ones multifractal; pre-2020 daily data only.
- `1601.00903v1` - Long memory and multifractality: A joint test (2016). Shows conventional long-memory tests over-reject under multifractality, then finds no return long memory in eleven of twelve currency pairs; the joint-test machinery is the reusable part.
- `1605.06700v1` - The impact of the financial crisis on the long-range memory of European corporate bond and stock markets (2016). Rolling Hurst exponents show bond memory rising and equity memory falling after 2008; six EU indices only.
- `1409.3738v2` - Beyond the Power Law: Uncovering Stylized Facts in Interbank Networks (2014). A distribution horse race on the Russian interbank network; log-normal and stretched exponential beat the power law over the full range.
- `cond-mat/0311585v2` - The duration of recessions follows an exponential not a power law (2003). Refits a published power law and shows an exponential wins until most of the sample is discarded; macro, from a single small frequency table.
- `1502.00225v1` - Power-law correlations in finance-related Google searches, and their cross-correlations with volatility and traded volume (2015). Shows search series are strongly persistent but only weakly and non-universally correlated with volume and volatility; a reconstructed 2004-2013 daily sample only.
- `1310.4538v2` - The Origin of Fat Tails (2013). Attributes fat tails to stress-dependent parameters and quantifies the tail-probability gap against a normal; US equity and bond data only.
- `1203.0643v1` - Incorporating fat tails in financial models using entropic divergence measures (2012). Shows relative-entropy view-updating can be infeasible under fat tails and fixes it with polynomial divergence; no empirical validation.
