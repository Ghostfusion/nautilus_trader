# Execution, impact and the order book: what a general-finance harvest adds to the microstructure core

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers the execution, market-impact and limit-order-book slice of a local harvest of the arXiv `q-fin.GN` (General Finance) category: 24 records spanning 2009-2026, 6 with a journal reference, of which all 24 were screened and 12 read in depth. The harvest itself holds 3095 records from 1997-2026. The companion `strategies/books/` corpus was written from a different harvest, arXiv `q-fin.TR`, which is where the core microstructure theory lives; briefs `01`-`03` there already own the Almgren-Chriss tradeoff, the square-root impact law and the queueing view of the book. This brief is therefore written for what that corpus does not cover: DeFi liquidations, corporate buyback execution, order-splitting measured from account data, generative order-book simulators, cross-asset Hawkes calibration and realtime impact detection. It is for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from the classic statement of price impact, through the measurement of who actually moves the book, to the systems that are built on top of it: a market maker trained inside a Hawkes limit-order-book simulator, a multivariate quadratic Hawkes calibration across futures and stocks, a prediction-market order book measured against on-chain ground truth, and the two flows where execution cost is the entire economics, DeFi liquidations and corporate buybacks. The single most consequential finding is a measurement warning: on Polymarket, trade direction inferred from the public order-book feed agrees with on-chain ground truth only about 59% of the time (`2604.24366v2`, p.1), which is close enough to the 50% chance baseline to flip the sign of the effective spread on two-thirds of comparable markets (p.13). Every direction-dependent microstructure measure in this slice inherits that problem when it is computed from a matching-engine feed that does not expose the aggressor.

## The short answer

1. Trade-direction inference from a public order-book feed is noise-dominated: on Polymarket, feed-inferred aggressor sign matches on-chain ground truth only 59.2% volume-weighted (panel mean 0.615, 95% CI [0.58, 0.65]), and effective half-spread sign flips on 67% of comparable markets (`2604.24366v2`, p.1, p.13).
2. Order splitting is the measured microscopic origin of order-flow long memory: on nine years of Tokyo Stock Exchange account data, splitting traders are about 25% of accounts but submit about 80% of market orders, and the metaorder exponent alpha and the sign-autocorrelation exponent gamma obey gamma = alpha - 1 (`2308.01112v1`, p.1, p.10).
3. DeFi liquidation is a same-block bot race: 60% of Compound's liquidated collateral cleared in the block it became liquidable, rising to 70% in 2020 from 26% in 2019 (`2009.13235v6`, p.14).
4. DeFi liquidation design over-sells collateral: 28,138 liquidations moved 807.46M USD and 73.97% paid an above-average gas fee, with close factors of 50-100% liquidating more than the debt requires (`2106.06389v2`, p.6, p.7).
5. Liquidation risk is knife-edge: a 3% DAI de-peg would have made over 10M USD liquidable on Compound, and a 43% ETH decline 1.07B USD on MakerDAO (`2009.13235v6`, p.13; `2106.06389v2`, p.9).
6. Buyback execution is the most expensive benchmarked flow here: one UK FTSE 250 buyback paid roughly 8.5% in fees on a GBP 184m programme, and a 125-day accelerated repurchase at 35% volatility puts about $215m of an $870m programme at one-sigma price risk (`2307.09617v1`, p.13, p.6).
7. The book is deeper than level 1 and depth matters: adding order-flow imbalance at ten levels improves out-of-sample RMSE by 65-75% on large-tick and 15-30% on small-tick Nasdaq names (`1907.06230v2`, p.2).
8. Filtering ephemeral orders does not sharpen book-based imbalance, but filtering the parent orders of trades does: a modification-time filter removed 168,058 order ids yet about 36M ticks on one BANKNIFTY session, and only trade-based filtered imbalance showed stronger Hawkes cross-excitation to returns (`2507.22712v2`, p.1, p.12).
9. Cross-asset feedback is measurable and near-critical: calibrated multivariate quadratic Hawkes kernels on six futures have diagonal Hawkes norms above 0.6 with largest pairwise eigenvalues 0.73-0.90, while cross-effects are an order of magnitude smaller than self-effects (`2509.21244v1`, p.11, p.1).
10. A Hawkes LOB simulator makes an RL market maker look strong (mean PnL 13.76 and Sharpe 1.62 against 8.39 and 1.20 for a symmetric benchmark) but only against synthetic data, and Sharpe falls from 1.38 to 0.86 as arrival intensities are perturbed (`2207.09951v1`, p.5).
11. Impact is concave and transient, not linear and permanent: the exponent psi is about 0.1-0.3 for single trades and rises toward 1 over thousands of trades, with transient decay beta = (1 - gamma)/2 and gamma about 0.5 for stocks (`0903.2428v1`, p.5, p.7).
12. Generative simulators are a privacy and scenario tool, not a validation substitute: a corporate taxonomy places calibrated agent-based simulation at privacy level 5 and uncalibrated simulation at level 6 (`2401.00081v2`, p.16, p.17).

## 1. Liquidation, on-chain: DeFi and the block clock

`2009.13235v6` is the first in-depth empirical study of liquidations on a protocol for loanable funds, covering Compound from 7 May 2019 to 6 September 2020 (p.10). The market is extremely concentrated: the top supplier holds 27.4% and the top ten 49% of supplied funds, while the top borrower holds 37.1% and the top ten 59.9% of borrows (p.11). Leverage is manufactured by looping: 2,141 accounts ran leveraging spirals for over 600M USD, roughly half of all funds supplied (p.12). The launch of the COMP governance token on 15 June 2020 changed risk appetite, and after it a 3% decline in the DAI price would have turned over 10M USD of collateral liquidable (p.13). Liquidation itself is a race: 60% of the total 35M USD of liquidated collateral was taken in the same block it became liquidable, 85% within two blocks and 95% within sixteen (p.14), and same-block liquidation rose from 26% in 2019 to 70% in 2020 (p.14). The authors even identify an account that sat roughly 20 USD from the threshold for one block with 3M USD of ETH exposed (p.14).

`2106.06389v2` widens the measurement to Aave, Compound, MakerDAO and dYdX, which together held over 85% of the Ethereum lending market at a total value locked of 39.88B USD (p.1). Across the full history of the four protocols it counts 28,138 liquidations moving 807.46M USD, run by 2,011 unique liquidator addresses (p.6). The economics are winner-take-most: average profit was 31.62K USD per liquidator, but the most active address ran 2,482 liquidations for 741.75K USD and the most profitable made 5.84M USD in only 112 liquidations (p.6). Competition is visible in fees, with 73.97% of liquidations paying an above-average gas price (p.7), and 623 flash loans totalling 483.83M USD were used to fund liquidations without holding inventory (p.9). The paper's central design criticism is over-liquidation: because Aave and Compound allow a 50% close factor and dYdX 100%, a debt that could be rescued by selling less collateral is instead sold at the full allowed amount, which the authors show favours liquidators over borrowers (p.6, p.8). It also documents bad debt (Aave V2 had accumulated 87.4K USD by 30 April 2021, p.6) and a liquidation sensitivity of 1.07B USD on MakerDAO for a 43% ETH decline (p.9).

## 2. Order splitting and the microscopic origin of long memory

`2308.01112v1` supplies the missing account-level evidence for the 2005 Lillo-Mike-Farmer model. Using the Tokyo Stock Exchange's virtual-server identifiers over nine years (2012-2020) and reconstructing trading desks, it classifies traders by a binomial test on run lengths into random and splitting traders (p.3, p.10). Splitting traders are typically about 25% of accounts but contribute about 80% of market orders (p.10-11), and their metaorder lengths follow a power law P(L) proportional to L^(-alpha-1) with alpha in (1, 2); about 90% of stocks have alpha below 2 (p.11). The paper's headline is a scatterplot that directly verifies the LMF prediction gamma = alpha - 1 across stocks and years (p.1), where gamma is the exponent of the sign autocorrelation C(tau) proportional to tau^(-gamma). The measurement is expensive: the authors estimate that at least 0.5 million transactions are needed for even one datapoint of gamma because of slow estimator convergence under long memory (p.2). The practical reading for a platform is that order-flow persistence is not a nuisance parameter but the footprint of a strategy population, and the exchange's own latency improvements (arrowhead reaction time fell from 2 ms to 0.2 ms over the window, p.3) are part of what makes it measurable.

## 3. Order-flow imbalance, depth and filtration

`1907.06230v2` extends the scalar order-flow imbalance of Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov to a vector over the first M price levels, using the full 2016 LOBSTER event tape for six Nasdaq stocks (p.10). With M = 10 and a 10-second window, out-of-sample RMSE improves monotonically with each additional level: by about 65-75% for large-tick names and 15-30% for small-tick names relative to level-1 OFI (p.2). The methodological point is as important as the result: ordinary least squares gives weak and unstable deep-level coefficients because the level imbalances are strongly collinear, whereas Ridge regression makes them strongly significant, and OLS also overfits in-sample (p.2). The microstructure reason is in the data: for the small-tick names (Amazon, Tesla, Netflix) more than half of all order flow occurs beyond the best quotes, while for large-tick names (Oracle, Cisco, Micron) the majority stacks at the best bid and ask (p.12). Mean mid-prices range from 699.22 USD for Amazon to 14.16 USD for Micron, so relative tick size is the organising variable (p.11).

`2507.22712v2` asks the operational question that follows: does removing ephemeral, heavily modified order flow sharpen the imbalance-return relationship? On three days of BANKNIFTY January 2023 futures it applies three lifecycle filters - order lifetime, modification count and time between modifications - to both the book and the parent orders of executed trades, and evaluates with a three-step ladder of Pearson correlation, regime regression and Hawkes cross-excitation (p.1, p.6, p.12). The answer is asymmetric. Filtering the aggregate book does not consistently improve alignment, but applying the same filters to the parent orders of trades produces a trade-based imbalance with systematically stronger Hawkes excitation norms from imbalance to return regimes (p.1). The scale is instructive: on 23 January 2023 the unfiltered book used 83,547,534 ticks, the lifetime filter removed 5,928,152 order identifiers leaving 51,023,701 ticks, and the modification-time filter removed only 168,058 identifiers yet about 36M ticks (p.12). A tiny subpopulation of orders generates a disproportionate share of message traffic without ever trading.

## 4. Price impact: concave, transient, and the spread identity

`0903.2428v1` is the compact statement of what impact is and is not. It separates three explanations - informed forecasting, private information revelation, and pure order-flow fluctuation - and notes that all three produce the same positive volume-price correlation, so the mechanism cannot be read off the correlation alone (p.1-2). Empirically the volume dependence is concave, R proportional to v^psi with psi about 0.1-0.3 for individual trades, rising toward 1 as the aggregation interval grows to thousands of trades (p.5); for metaorders the square-root law R = A sigma sqrt(v/V) is the standard form (p.6), and cross-sectionally impact rescales as M^(-0.3) v^0.3 F(v/M) (p.6). Impact cannot be permanent because order-flow signs are strongly autocorrelated over days; reconciliation requires a transient kernel G(l) decaying as l^(-beta) with beta = (1 - gamma)/2, giving beta about 0.25 for gamma about 0.5 (p.7). Gatheral's condition that price manipulation is impossible when beta + psi >= 1 is the constraint a simulator must respect (p.7). The most useful identity for a platform is that spread and impact are two sides of one compensation: the market maker's spread is S = 2 lambda v^psi, so a simulation that calibrates impact and then hard-codes an unrelated spread is internally inconsistent (p.9). The author notes the correlation between price change and signed volume exceeds 0.5 at daily horizons (p.5).

## 5. Hawkes models: from simulator to calibration

`2207.09951v1` trains a deep RL market maker inside a weakly consistent limit-order-book simulator driven by a multivariate Hawkes process with exponential kernels, using eight event types and Ogata thinning, with the market maker's own quotes feeding back into the arrival intensities (p.3). Training is Soft Actor-Critic for 10^6 steps over 10^3 Monte Carlo episodes (p.5). Against a symmetric best-quote benchmark and a linear-inventory benchmark the learned controller reports mean PnL 13.7567 and Sharpe 1.6193 versus 8.3913/1.1970 and 7.6772/1.4184, with a mean absolute position of 0.4232 against 1.4659 and 0.5478 (p.5). It tolerates limit-order fees up to about 0.6% while the linear benchmark turns negative at 0.4% and the symmetric one at 0.2% (p.5-6), and it trades less as fees rise, from 24.54 to 7.85 mean transactions (p.6). The limit of the evidence is the same as its strength: everything is synthetic, the environment is a reduced-form book rather than a full one, and when background intensities are perturbed by Gaussian noise of variance 0.1/0.2/0.3 the Sharpe ratio falls to 1.3766/1.0175/0.8638 (p.5). This is a controlled experiment in a simulator, not a market result.

`2509.21244v1` is the calibration counterpart, fitting a multivariate quadratic Hawkes (equivalently quadratic ARCH) model non-parametrically by the method of moments on 1-minute bins from 10:00 to 15:00, for six futures and 317 S&P 500 stocks over 2013-2023 (p.3). The method is a four-step procedure that fits self kernels first and then cross-effects, because cross-effects are an order of magnitude smaller and must be separated from noise (p.1, p.6-7). It identifies cross-Zumbach effects (past trends of one asset driving the volatility of another) for the first time, a covariance-to-volatility feedback, and a cross-leverage effect (p.1). The system is close to critical: diagonal Hawkes norms are all above 0.6 and the largest eigenvalues of the pairwise kernel matrices lie between 0.73 and 0.90 (p.11). The e-mini/tbond return correlation in the sample is about -0.15, which is what makes the cross-leverage signs interpretable (p.8).

## 6. Generative and synthetic order-book data

`2401.00081v2` is a corporate overview of synthetic data in finance and its most transferable contribution is a six-level privacy hierarchy that doubles as a fidelity taxonomy (p.11-17). Level 1 obscures personally identifying fields, level 2 adds noise, level 3 uses generative models such as GANs and copulas, level 4 adds explicit attack testing, level 5 is calibrated simulation (rules plus statistical calibration, with agent-based market simulators such as ABIDES as the example) and level 6 is uncalibrated simulation used for stress and what-if testing (p.16, p.17, p.6). For a trading platform the useful part is the evaluation framing: fidelity metrics (KS, Jensen-Shannon, stylized-fact similarity), utility metrics including train-on-synthetic-test-on-real, and privacy metrics are three different axes, and a generator can score well on one and badly on another (p.6-7). The paper's own tabular case study reports downstream fraud-detection AUROC of 98.52% for a TVAE generator, essentially matching the real-data classifier, which is an upper bound on what fidelity buys rather than evidence about markets (p.22). The level-5/level-6 distinction is the one to import: a calibrated simulator is an estimator of the real process and must be validated against it; an uncalibrated one is a test harness and must never be cited as evidence.

## 7. Corporate buyback execution: a benchmarked, cost-dominated flow

`2307.09617v1` is the slice's purest execution-economics paper and it is a practitioner white paper rather than a peer-reviewed measurement, which the caveats below respect. It documents that S&P 500 companies spent 3.8 trillion USD on repurchases over five years (p.2), and that roughly 20% of all buybacks are executed through products benchmarked to what the authors call the bogus benchmark, the arithmetic average of daily VWAPs rather than a volume-weighted one (p.12). The benchmark matters because the executing broker often controls the measurement window: the authors compare it to a coin-toss game where the broker may stop after anywhere from 100 to 150 flips, turning an honest 50/50 into an overwhelming edge (p.11). The cost is not hypothetical. In one UK FTSE 250 case a GBP 184m gross programme carried about GBP 16.6m of costs, of which roughly GBP 15.7m was fee after stripping the 0.5% stamp duty, an effective 8.5% (p.13). Price risk is the other half of the tradeoff: a 125-day accelerated repurchase at 35% annual volatility implies a one-standard-deviation price move of about 25%, so an average $870m programme carries about $215m of one-sigma VaR at initiation (p.6), and 68% of accelerated repurchases are sold without price caps or collars (p.10). The authors estimate that in 2022 about $70bn of shareholder value was managed under this benchmark structure, and $276bn over five years (p.12). Ordinary execution of the same strategies costs about 5-8 bps (p.12).

## 8. Clocks, timestamps and the cost of getting them wrong

`1312.2004v1` is an idiosyncratic paper, but it contributes one hard operational fact and one classification. It defines the a-b-c increments: waiting times between transactions within a session, price increments within a session, and price changes between sessions (p.16). Its CME Globex example is a single E-mini S&P 500 contract across 57 sessions from 1 March to 22 May 2013, 21,364,635 records and 835,401,602 bytes, averaging 374,818 records per session (p.15). The mean arrival rate is only 4.5 records per second, but a single second at 11:00:00 on the corn contract carried 806 transactions, 1,166 contracts and a price range of [630.00, 638.75], which is 437.50 USD per contract before fees of about 10.66 USD per round trip (p.15). The load-bearing point for a data engine is that the exchange reports time to one-second accuracy, and this truncation does not merely add noise: it manufactures zero-length waiting times and reshapes the empirical distribution of a-increments in exactly the liquid periods where a model would be calibrated (p.21). Any waiting-time or event-time model fitted on one-second data is fitting the exchange's clock, not the market's.

## Caveats

Half the slice is thin on data or narrow in scope. `0903.2428v1` is a review and position paper with no new dataset; `2401.00081v2` is a corporate overview whose numbers are vendor and internal case studies; `2307.09617v1` is a white paper by outsiders with two anonymised UK examples and no access to the private contracts it criticises, so its 8.5% fee and $276bn estimates are inferences, not measurements. `2207.09951v1` is entirely synthetic - its RL edge exists only inside its own Hawkes simulator, which is a reduced-form book. `2009.13235v6` covers one protocol (Compound) to September 2020, `2106.06389v2` covers four protocols to April 2021, and both are pre-2022 DeFi, so nothing here speaks to the current liquidation landscape. `2604.24366v2` is a single venue over 52 days and its own two-window robustness check shows the effective-spread sign-flip rate swinging from 67% to 50%, so the direction-inference result is window-specific even though the methodological conclusion is not. `1907.06230v2` is one venue in one year, `2507.22712v2` is three days of one futures contract, `2308.01112v1` is one market, and `1312.2004v1` is one contract family on one exchange. `2509.21244v1` calibrates intraday only and explicitly notes that the leverage effect lives at daily scale, so its intraday leverage kernels are weak by construction. The slice also disagrees with itself on magnitude: `0903.2428v1` puts the single-trade impact exponent at 0.1-0.3 while the metaorder square-root law uses 0.5, and the Hawkes papers disagree on whether cross-effects are negligible (`2509.21244v1` finds them an order of magnitude below self-effects but significant, while `2207.09951v1` models them as the main driver). No paper here measures live strategy P&L net of fees, and no paper establishes a unified cross-venue, cross-asset impact model; the strongest claims in the slice are about measurement and design, not about profitability.
