# Return predictability and trading strategies: the cross-sectional findings are mostly real, so decay and leakage are the binding constraints

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers one slice of the local arXiv harvest of the `q-fin.GN` (General Finance) category: the 122 records whose titles or abstracts concern return predictability and trading strategies, spanning 2001 to 2026, 22 of which carry a journal reference. It reads twelve of those papers in depth and asks what is actually predictable in returns, how much a predictability finding is worth once multiple testing is counted, whether a claim survives out of sample, and how the newest family of forecasters, large language models, contaminates its own backtests. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, who needs to know which research claims are worth encoding and which are backtest artefacts.

## What this covers

The slice runs from the oldest question in asset pricing, whether payout ratios predict returns, to the newest, whether a language model can. The arc is a reversal. The classic time-series result does not survive a broad out-of-sample panel (`2209.00121v1`), while the cross-sectional "factor zoo" that was widely suspected of being p-hacked turns out to be mostly genuine (`2206.15365v10`). That inversion is the single most consequential finding here: predictability is abundant in the cross-section, so the binding constraints move from discovery to decay, crowding, and the integrity of the evaluation. The rest of the slice shows what those constraints look like in practice: momentum design (`2101.01006v2`), drawdown- and physics-based ranking rules (`1403.8125v4`, `1208.2775v5`), indicator combinations (`2206.12282v1`), a regime-switching pairs strategy (`2309.00875v3`), news- and AI-based forecasts (`1508.04332v2`, `2608.00761v1`), the leakage that inflates LLM signals (`2309.17322v1`, `2512.23847v2`), and the endogenous decay of any signal once it is widely traded (`2605.23905v1`).

## The short answer

1. Payout-ratio predictability fails a broad out-of-sample test: of 48 country-asset combinations across 16 countries, 1870s-2020, none shows consistent in-sample, out-of-sample and utility gains, and the prediction portfolios trade several times more than the benchmark (`2209.00121v1`).
2. The cross-sectional factor zoo is probably mostly true rather than p-hacked: an easy bound puts the false-discovery rate at or below 25%, and a tighter visual bound at or below 8.5%, i.e. at least 91.5% of equal-weighted findings are real (`2206.15365v10`).
3. The defence of a signal must be weighting- and factor-specific: after value-weighting and a four-factor adjustment the false-discovery bound rises to 41.7% (`2206.15365v10`).
4. Momentum strategies are positively skewed by construction, even when the underlying returns are i.i.d., zero-mean and unskewed, and the skewness is hump-shaped in the holding horizon before decaying as M^-1/2 (`2101.01006v2`).
5. Recovery- and drawdown-based ranking rules beat cumulative return at matched horizons: recovery wins weekly contrarian portfolios in KOSPI 200 and the S&P 500 while cutting maximum drawdown roughly in half (`1403.8125v4`).
6. Physics-flavoured momentum works only through the mass proxy: volume- and turnover-weighted definitions beat unit-mass cumulative return, while inverse-volatility mass does not replicate that on the KOSPI (`1208.2775v5`).
7. Indicator-only MACD strategies win under half their trades, and genetic-algorithm parameters differ for every index and are driven by outliers; adding a price-pressure indicator is what lifts the win rate (`2206.12282v1`).
8. A hidden-Markov, regime-switching spread model produces the best pairs strategy in crude oil futures, including a Shanghai-Brent-WTI triplet whose members are not pairwise cointegrated (`2309.00875v3`).
9. A news-driven agent-based model backtests long-horizon SPY predictability and still reports a Sharpe ratio above 1 after a 0.80% p.a. cost drag, but on one market and one strategy family (`1508.04332v2`).
10. AI-derived currency fundamentals show a cross-sectional Sharpe ratio above 0.7 p.a. with an alpha worth 74% of the average return, and four separate leakage tests fail to explain it away (`2608.00761v1`).
11. LLM lookahead contamination is measurable without access to the training corpus: the interaction between a forecast and a date-only recall probability is significant in sample and statistically zero after the model's cutoff (`2512.23847v2`).
12. Anti-leakage interventions cut both ways: anonymising firm names raises in-sample performance because the model's general knowledge distracts as much as it leaks (`2309.17322v1`), and the model's own half-life is compressed by crowding (`2605.23905v1`).

## 1. The textbook predictability does not survive the panel

`2209.00121v1` builds annual data for 16 developed countries across long-term government bonds, equities and housing from the 1870s to 2020, using coupon-price, dividend-price and rent-price ratios as predictors. In sample, payout prices predict excess returns in only two bond markets and five equity markets, while rent-price predicts eight housing markets (p.9). Out of sample the record collapses: none of the 16 bond markets is predictable, dividend price predicts only the UK equity market, and only the French and German housing markets are predictable (p.10). Utility gains agree: positive certainty-equivalent-return gains appear in two bond markets, three equity markets and four housing markets, and two of the four housing gains are near zero (p.11).

The decisive detail is turnover. The portfolios built on payout-ratio forecasts trade several times more than the prevailing-average benchmark: in the Italian equity market the dividend-price portfolio turns over about four times as much despite a non-positive CER gain, and the French housing portfolio about four times as much (p.11). Because the paper rebalances annually, the housing transaction costs alone, commission near 6% in the US and around 7.7% of value in 13 of the 16 countries, swamp the best 0.61% CER gain (p.3, p.11).

The paper's constructive half is the Cochrane decomposition: if returns are not predictable then payout growth must be, and it finds dividend growth predictable in 14 countries and rent growth in five, including the US, with a VAR simulation that does not reject the joint null of no return predictability and predictable dividend growth (p.12, p.13). That is the mechanism an operator should internalise: a persistent valuation ratio is not evidence that the price will move.

## 2. The factor zoo is mostly real, and the measurement decides

`2206.15365v10` asks how many published cross-sectional predictors are false. Its "Easy Bound" needs only the share of signals with |t| > 2 and gives FDR <= 5% divided by that share; because data-mining studies find at least 20% of randomly chosen accounting ratios clear |t| > 2, the bound is 25% in eight of the nine studies examined (p.3, p.4). A tighter "Visual Bound" uses the observed frequency of small t-statistics and, on 29,000 data-mined signals of which 9,700 clear |t| > 2 and 6,300 sit below 0.5, gives FDR <= 8.5%, i.e. at least 91.5% true (p.9, p.10).

The reconciliation matters as much as the level. Studies reporting a false-discovery rate above 45% are reinterpreted: Harvey, Liu and Zhu's own dataset implies a bound of at most 12% under the same algebra, and the gap is an interpretation of "insignificant" as "false" (p.7, p.14). Where the measurement changes, the bound moves: equal-weighted raw returns give 7.3-9.6%, but value-weighted returns with a four-factor (FF3 plus momentum) adjustment give FDR <= 41.7% (p.13). The paper is explicit that large, liquid stocks show weaker predictability and that liquidity and momentum may account for that 42% (p.13).

The practical corollary is about portfolio construction, not statistics. Ten thousand significant signals are not ten thousand independent bets: pairwise correlations are mostly below 0.25 in absolute value, roughly 50 principal components explain 75% of variance, and combining data-mined predictors yields out-of-sample Sharpe ratios near 1.5 rather than the 100-fold boost an independence assumption would suggest (p.12).

## 3. Momentum is a design problem, not a forecast problem

`2101.01006v2` separates the first moment of a trend-following strategy from its third. Even when the risk-adjusted returns are i.i.d. with zero mean and zero skewness, the trading returns of a linear momentum strategy are positively skewed, because the position is largest after the strategy has already made money and the high-variance mixture components carry a positive mean (p.1, p.2). Skewness is therefore a property of the position rule, not of the market.

The term-structure result is the actionable one: the skewness of the M-period trading return rises to a maximum at a period proportional to the trend filter's response time and then decays as M^-1/2 (p.3). A strategy's reported skewness therefore depends on the horizon at which it is measured. The position is scaled inversely to volatility, phi_n / sigma_n, so the risk of the book is held roughly constant (p.4).

Two design warnings follow. A binary plus-or-minus-one transformation of the momentum factor is not optimal and can remove the positive skewness or make it negative (p.4). And the option-like reading is exact: the trading return decomposes into quadratic bets with a few large positive-convexity, long-dated eigenvalues and many small negative-convexity, short-dated ones, which is why trend-following does well when long-horizon volatility is high relative to short-horizon volatility and suffers when it is not (p.15, p.16). The Cornish-Fisher relation between the probability of exceeding the mean and the coefficient of skewness makes the point sharp: a positively skewed strategy can win less than half its trades and still be worth running (p.3).

## 4. Ranking rules that beat cumulative return

`1403.8125v4` replaces the cumulative-return sort with sorts on maximum drawdown and the recovery after it, decomposed as cumulative return equals pre-peak return minus drawdown plus recovery over the three sub-periods (p.5). On weekly six-week/six-week contrarian portfolios in KOSPI 200 from January 2003 to December 2012 the recovery portfolio returns 0.146% per week against 0.073% for the benchmark, with standard deviation 1.757% against 2.842% (p.8). On the S&P 500 weekly contrarian the recovery rule returns 0.0872% against 0.0420% and cuts maximum drawdown from 72.56% to 43.99% (p.18).

The time-scale split is the finding: drawdown-based rules win at monthly momentum horizons, where the CMR portfolio on sector ETFs returns 0.1724% per month against 0.1174% for cumulative return (p.16), while recovery-based rules win at weekly contrarian horizons (p.8, p.18). The loser baskets carry the improvement in both directions (p.8, p.15).

`1208.2775v5` does the same exercise with a physics analogy, defining price momentum as mass times velocity in log-price space and giving eleven candidate definitions, of which cumulative return is the special case with unit mass (p.8, p.9). On the KOSPI 200 the best weekly contrarian criterion returns 0.2607% per week against 0.0685% for cumulative return with a lower standard deviation (p.14), and on the S&P 500 the inverse-volatility criterion is best (p.16). The mass proxy matters: volume- and turnover-based definitions outperform, whereas the inverse-volatility mass does not reproduce the effect on the KOSPI and can turn negative (p.14). Transaction costs are not applied in the comparison, so these are gross rankings (p.12).

## 5. Indicator strategies and the price-pressure gap

`2206.12282v1` backtests MACD (12, 26, 9) rules on Dow Jones, Nasdaq and S&P 500 constituents from 01/01/2015 to 28/08/2021 (p.1). The plain MACD rules win under half their trades: the signal-crossover rule wins 0.40-0.49, the zero-crossover rule 0.37, and the histogram rule has the highest win rate but a profit-and-loss ratio below 1 in every universe, with single losses exceeding 80% of the principal in the S&P 500 (p.15, p.16).

Optimisation does not repair it. A genetic algorithm run on DIA, QQQ and SPY returns different parameter sets for each index, (9, 27, 40), (18, 35, 39) and (17, 31, 23), so there is no single optimum (p.20). The apparent improvement is outlier-driven: two trades exceed twenty times the initial investment, and once the outliers are removed the average return is close to the traditional parameter (p.21). The paper's own conclusion is that the genetic-algorithm solution is a local optimum for one index and one period (p.29).

What does move the needle is adding a price-pressure indicator. MACD plus RSI wins 0.84, 0.86 and 0.78 across the three universes with the best risk-adjusted performance, and MACD plus MFI wins 0.69, 0.70 and 0.67 (p.23, p.25). But the same strategies rank in the last third on accumulated profit, because the filter misses trends (p.29). The derived volume-price indicator, VPVMA, adds trading volume and daily volatility to the MACD construction and lands between the two: better win rate and Sharpe than plain MACD, with accumulated profit above the RSI-filtered version (p.31, p.33).

## 6. Regime-switching statistical arbitrage in crude oil

`2309.00875v3` treats pairs trading as cointegration plus a mean-reverting spread and models the spread as an AR(1) whose parameters switch with a hidden Markov chain, estimated online by a filter-based EM algorithm (p.4, p.6). The data are daily and weekly continuous Brent, WTI and Shanghai crude oil futures, from the Shanghai contract's launch on 26 March 2018 to 30 June 2023, giving 1373 daily and 273 weekly observations, with a training sample ending 1 July 2022 and a one-year test sample (p.13, p.14).

The Johansen trace test finds exactly one cointegrating relationship among the three prices (p.15), and the error-correction coefficients show the adjustment runs through the Shanghai contract: its coefficient is positive and significant at 1% while Brent and WTI do not adjust, a unidirectional long-run causality (p.16). That is the economic reason the recently introduced contract is the profitable leg.

Performance is measured with transaction costs estimated as average daily half-spreads: Brent 5.80 bps, WTI 20.24 bps, Shanghai 53.71 bps (p.12). At the baseline bandwidth the forward-looking prediction-interval strategy returns 15.18% annualised with a Sharpe ratio of 1.18 and a maximum drawdown of -5.73%, against -28.47% and -0.74 for a crude-oil ETF and 11.74% and 0.67 for the S&P 500 (p.21, p.23, p.25). Across bandwidth levels the model-based prediction-interval and predicted-increment strategies dominate the backward-looking probability-interval and realised-increment rules, and the White Reality Check repeatedly selects the prediction-interval strategy (p.21, p.22). The magnitudes are consistent with the 6-8% annual excess returns previously documented for commodity-futures statistical arbitrage (p.22).

## 7. News, language models, and the leakage that flatters them

`1508.04332v2` couples a news-driven agent-based model with empirical news flow and backtests four purely news-based SPY strategies over 1995-2015, reporting out-of-sample results from 2003 (p.34). Mean returns are 16.6-19.0% per annum with volatility 13.9-15.5% and Sharpe ratios 0.97-1.23, against 0.39 for SPY and 0.62 for a price-based momentum-reversal benchmark, with average holding periods of 8.6-44.6 business days (p.35). The single strategy later run live shows 25.1% annual volatility, a 5% monthly value-at-risk of -4.7% and a Sharpe ratio of 1.07 after an 0.80% per annum cost drag, against 0.31 for SPY (p.37). The model's claim is structural: the market is quasi-efficient intraday and inefficient beyond one day, because a news-price feedback loop is negligible at intraday scale (p.2, p.41).

The LLM papers make that claim expensive to test. `2309.17322v1` compares GPT-3.5 sentiment strategies on original and anonymised headlines over an in-sample window ending September 2021 and an out-of-sample window to December 2022 (p.4). In sample the anonymised headlines do better, 30.97 versus 25.08 basis points per day on the scraped set and 81.64 versus 77.27 on the Thomson Reuters set, with p-values 0.066 and 0.017 (p.8). The original prompt is wrong less often, 17.6% against 38.95%, but its errors are far larger: it loses 242 basis points when the anonymised prompt stays neutral against 97 basis points in the mirror case (p.10). The strategy also trades systematically larger companies, 35.37 billion against 17.91 billion USD in sample (p.12).

`2512.23847v2` turns that worry into a diagnostic. It defines Lookahead Propensity as the probability the model assigns to "up" or "down" on a date-only recall query that contains no headline at all (p.3, p.10). LAP is materially positive throughout the in-sample period and collapses to essentially zero immediately after the December 2023 training cutoff (p.3, p.11); the Kodak query for 29 July 2020 returns P(up) of about 0.9999 with no headline supplied (p.3, p.9). In the headline-to-return task the LLM signal predicts a 0.21% higher next-day return per one-step move, and a one-standard-deviation increase in LAP raises that marginal effect by 0.067 percentage points, about 32% of the standalone effect; the recall-direction signal alone has a t-statistic of 3.53 (p.4). The interaction is statistically zero after the cutoff, and in an earnings-call-to-capex task the pattern repeats with an interaction t-statistic of 2.01 and a 12% amplification (p.4, p.5).

`2608.00761v1` is the counterweight. Using GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 to read 174,820 economic data releases across 544 indicators from 1996 to 2024 for nine G-10 currencies, it builds an AIFX index and reports a cross-sectional long-short Sharpe ratio above 0.7 per annum and an alpha worth 74% of the average return at a 48-month lookback after controlling for dollar, dollar-carry, carry, momentum and value (p.2, p.8, p.13). Four leakage tests are run: the model guesses the release year with 5.68% precision and 4.17% recall, so it cannot recall dates (p.19); a difference-in-differences test across the GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o cutoffs finds no significant divergence (p.20, p.21); it cannot reproduce realised macro-return correlations (p.22); and a pure-hindsight control portfolio with a Sharpe ratio of 0.91 does not subsume the AIFX strategy, whose alpha stays significant with a negative beta (p.22, p.23). The predictive content is concentrated in inflation, employment and broad activity data, and in positive rather than negative news, which the paper links to a stronger immediate reaction to bad news (p.25, p.27).

## 8. Crowding and the endogenous half-life of a signal

`2605.23905v1` is the slice's forward-looking frame, and it is a model rather than a measurement. It writes the alpha half-life as h(phi) = ln 2 / [theta + delta(phi)] with delta(phi) = N phi rho a / lambda(phi), so that half-life is convex-decreasing in AI adoption phi; at phi = 0.7 and rho = 0.6 the calibration implies an 18-month half-life for a medium-frequency factor against 5-7 years pre-AI (p.1, p.5). Three channels are combined: signal crowding when systems share training data and architectures, performative signal erosion when trading degrades the signal itself, and a Red Queen equilibrium in which aggregate AI investment is positive but net alpha is identically zero (p.3, p.5).

The supporting evidence is calibrated simulation, not observation: SEC Form 13F filings, 99.5 million holdings over 2013-2024, are used to calibrate a portfolio-convergence model that reports a 42% rise in simulated pairwise cosine similarity over the sample, and the 2010 Flash Crash is reproduced as a fragility illustration (p.1, p.6). The context it frames is the useful part: 91% of asset-management institutions report using or planning to use AI, and algorithmic trading is estimated at 60-80% of US equity volume (p.3). Whether or not the half-life formula is right, the direction agrees with the rest of the slice: a signal that is published and widely implementable is a signal whose excess return is being competed away.

## Caveats

The evidence is uneven. `2605.23905v1` has no data at all: its empirical section is a calibrated simulation matched to Form 13F filings, so the 42% convergence figure and the 18-month half-life are model outputs, not observations. `2206.15365v10` derives bounds from other papers' summary statistics rather than a new dataset, so its conclusions are about interpretation as much as about evidence. Several papers are single-market and single-period: `2206.12282v1` covers US index constituents from 2015 to 2021 and explicitly excludes shorting; `2309.00875v3` covers three crude oil futures from 2018 to 2023 with a one-year test window; `1403.8125v4` and `1208.2775v5` share overlapping samples (KOSPI 200 from 2003 to 2012, S&P 500 from 1993 to 2012, sector ETFs from 1999 to 2012) and both rank gross of transaction costs; `1508.04332v2` is one market and one model; and `2309.17322v1` and `2512.23847v2` use US equity headlines only. The slice disagrees with itself in two places. `2209.00121v1` and `2206.15365v10` look like opposites but measure different objects, time-series payout-ratio timing versus cross-sectional characteristic sorts. `2608.00761v1` finds an LLM FX signal that survives four leakage tests, while `2512.23847v2` and `2309.17322v1` find measurable LLM contamination in equity and capex tasks; leakage is task-specific, so neither result transfers for free. No paper here establishes that any signal survives realistic costs, capacity limits and live multi-venue execution.

## Papers read in depth

- `2209.00121v1` - 150 Years of Return Predictability Around the World: A Holistic View Across Assets (2022). Shows payout-ratio return timing failing across 48 country-asset combinations; limitation: annual data, and housing costs are discussed rather than modelled trade by trade.
- `2206.15365v10` - Most claimed statistical findings in cross-sectional return predictability are likely true (2022). Bounds the false-discovery rate at 8.5-25% and reconciles the literature; limitation: bounds are built from published summary statistics, not a new panel.
- `2101.01006v2` - Design and analysis of momentum trading strategies (2021). Derives the skewness term structure of linear and nonlinear momentum rules; limitation: a synthesis of theory with a light empirical check, not a portfolio backtest.
- `2309.17322v1` - Assessing Look-Ahead Bias in Stock Return Predictions Generated By GPT Sentiment Analysis (2023). Anonymisation shows distraction outweighs leakage in sample; limitation: one model version, headline-level signals, no transaction costs.
- `2309.00875v3` - A hidden Markov model for statistical arbitrage in international crude oil futures markets (2023). Regime-switching cointegration with online EM beats fixed bands; limitation: three contracts and a one-year out-of-sample window.
- `2206.12282v1` - A comparative study of the MACD-base trading strategies: evidence from the US stock market (2022). Documents sub-50% win rates and GA overfitting; limitation: no shorting, a bull-market sample, and a student-thesis backtesting setup.
- `1508.04332v2` - Forecasting stock market returns over multiple time horizons (2015). A news-driven agent-based model with a live SPY strategy; limitation: one market, one instrument family, and the live track is pro-forma.
- `2608.00761v1` - AI and Exchange Rate Predictability (2026). An LLM-built fundamentals index with a Sharpe ratio above 0.7 and four leakage tests; limitation: nine currencies and a data sample built from one provider's calendar and consensus forecasts.
- `2605.23905v1` - AI-Driven Alpha Decay (2026). A model of crowding, performative erosion and a Red Queen equilibrium; limitation: empirical validation is entirely simulated.
- `1403.8125v4` - Maximum drawdown, recovery, and momentum (2014). Drawdown and recovery ranking rules beat cumulative return; limitation: equity universes to 2012, gross of costs.
- `1208.2775v5` - Physical approach to price momentum and its application to momentum strategy (2012). Eleven momentum definitions with a mass proxy; limitation: same overlapping samples as the drawdown paper, and transaction costs are not applied.
- `2512.23847v2` - Detecting Lookahead Bias in LLM Forecasts (2025). A portable leakage diagnostic via date-only recall probability; limitation: two applications, one model family, and no trading-cost analysis.
