# Banking, credit and funding liquidity: funding is the residual of payment flows, and leverage rules can amplify the cycle

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads twelve papers from the arXiv q-fin.GN harvest on banking, credit and funding liquidity, and states what their evidence changes about how an event-driven trading platform should model funding, margin and credit. The slice is 223 records spanning 2000-2026, 59 of them carrying a journal reference; forty were screened and twelve were read in full. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, whose engine holds cash and margin accounts, simulates settlement and funding, and evaluates strategies whose returns depend on how the balance sheet behind the order flow behaves when it is stressed.

## What this covers

The slice runs from payment-flow accounting to balance-sheet fragility, and it is unusually consistent about where funding comes from. `1202.3182v1` and `1702.08774v1` show that overnight funding is not an independent market but the residual of payment and settlement flows. `2506.03344v1` shows that the 2023 deposit run was predictable from funding composition and was not hedged. `1906.08617v1`, `1407.5429v1` and `0901.2377v3` show that credit relationships have mesoscale structure that survives only if maturity and counterparty type are kept. `1403.1637v1`, `1507.04136v1`, `1204.1583v1` and `0904.1426v4` show that the capital rule is itself part of the price mechanism. `1309.2130v4` and `2211.09176v3` show where the balance sheet and the credit risk are least measured. The single most consequential finding is in `1202.3182v1`: loan and nonloan imbalances correlate about -0.9 on most days (p.8), so the funding rate is a settlement residual, and any simulation that feeds funding in as an exogenous rate series is modelling the wrong object.

## The short answer

1. Overnight lending is the residual of payment flow imbalance, not an independent market: loan and nonloan imbalances correlate -0.93, -0.88, -0.95 and -0.36 across four days (`1202.3182v1` p.8).
2. Loans are a tiny part of the flow system but carry real money: under 1% of transactions by number and up to 9% by value, with 897 overnight loans in four days at a mean rate within 0.01% of the 6.25% target (`1202.3182v1` p.5).
3. The payment network is stable and the loan network is not: about 80% of nonloan flows persist day to day and carry more than 96% of value, while only about 50% of loan flows persist and they carry about 65% (`1202.3182v1` p.9, p.10).
4. Banks did not hedge deposit run risk before 2023: uninsured deposits fell 1.9 percentage points on average, from 27% to 25%, and the fall was largest where initial uninsured and brokered funding was highest (`2506.03344v1` p.5, p.8).
5. Only about 1,200 of 4,551 banks report interest-rate derivative positions at all, and option use has no significant relation to the change in uninsured deposits (`2506.03344v1` p.5, p.7).
6. Collapsing loan maturities destroys the structure that matters: the three sub-30-day layers hold 97% of issued loans, yet the statistically optimal granularity always sits between collapsing and separating the layers (`1906.08617v1` p.5, p.4).
7. Leverage rules are part of the price mechanism: a 16% capital floor forces selling into a decline and can turn an absorbable shock into a banking catastrophe (`1403.1637v1` p.8, p.2).
8. The best leverage policy is not a constant: a procyclical Basel-style rule is optimal when noise is high and the bank is small, and constant leverage is closer to optimal otherwise (`1507.04136v1` p.7, p.11).
9. Whether credit is bounded depends on the reserve-base definition: once interbank claims are admitted to the base, the potential supply of bank credit becomes unbound (`1702.08774v1` p.24).
10. When interbank coordination degrades, the system does not reprice, it moves to permanent central-bank recourse (`1702.08774v1` p.26, p.27).
11. Consumer credit risk is not stationary over the life of a loan: default hazards converge across risk bands after about one year and fully by 42-48 months (`2211.09176v3` p.4).
12. The largest balance sheets are the least covered: financial firms are about 30% of Global 2000 firms but 87% of its assets, and the missing top-tail mass is the shadow-banking estimate (`1309.2130v4` p.1, p.5).

## 1. Funding is a residual of settlement, not a price series

`1202.3182v1` reconstructs every interbank transaction settled on an RTGS basis in Australia's RITS system over five consecutive weekdays from 19 February 2007, with 55 participating banks and 2,970 possible directed flows (p.3, p.9). Overnight loans are extracted by matching reversing transaction pairs above A$200,000 whose implied rate is within 0.5% of the known 6.25% target; the authors estimate contamination below 2%, at most five misidentifications per day (p.4). They find 897 overnight loans over four days (p.5); the loans are less than 1% of transactions by number and up to 9% by value (p.5), and the mean implied rate is within one basis point of the target with a standard deviation of about 0.07% (p.5). The decisive result is the imbalance relation: across Monday to Thursday the Pearson correlation between loan and nonloan imbalances is -0.93, -0.88, -0.95 and -0.36, and the absolute loan imbalance correlates with total nonloan value at 0.74, 0.75, 0.66 and 0.77 (p.8). Payment flows create the imbalance; the money market clears it.

The size of the funding flow is small relative to the flow it clears. Overnight loans are 9.12%, 4.45%, 6.84% and 7.04% of daily transaction value across the four days, and two-day loans number 27, 67 and 24 with total values of A$1.3, A$2.2 and A$1.4 billion, or 1.5%, 1.0% and 0.9% of the day's value (p.5, p.6). Net nonloan flows average about 470 per day, of which roughly 110 consist of a single transaction and 50 of two transactions (p.11). The counterparties are concentrated: the big four banks held 65% of total resident assets in February 2007, each above A$225 billion against A$93 billion for the next largest retail bank, and the big four plus one investment bank held 56% of cash and liquid assets (p.6, p.7). The evidence is one quiescent week in one country and the extraction rule assumes the target rate is known, so the correlation is a mechanism description, not an estimated causal coefficient. `1702.08774v1` makes the same point structurally: each bank's balance sheet has five paired items (currency reserves against base-money deposits, retail loans against loan deposits, interbank lending against interbank borrowing, equity reserve against equity provision, central-bank assistance against guarantee) (p.6), and interbank settlement is what rebalances the treasury after payment and lending activity (p.11, p.17).

## 2. Deposit composition decides who runs

`2506.03344v1` uses FDIC Call Reports for 4,551 to 4,553 banks and asks whether banks positioned their balance sheets ahead of the 2022-2023 hiking cycle. Uninsured deposits, computed as (DEP-DEPINS)/DEP, fell on average by 1.9 percentage points from Q4 2021 to Q3 2023, with the average bank going from 27% uninsured to 25% (p.5, p.6). The cross-sectional regression coefficient on the initial uninsured share is -0.218 with a standard error of 0.012 in the base specification and -0.312 in the specification with derivatives, so the banks that ran hardest were the banks that started most exposed; the brokered-deposit coefficient is -0.108 and -0.241 (p.8). Option positions bought and sold have coefficients of 0.009 and 0.005 with standard errors of 0.049 and 0.046, statistically indistinguishable from zero, and the change in equity is likewise insignificant (p.7, p.8). Futures and forwards carry a coefficient of -0.026 with a standard error of 0.002, so larger notional positions are associated with larger declines, while swaps carry 0.009 with a standard error of 0.004 (p.8).

The dispersion is the story. The change in uninsured deposits has a standard deviation of 0.076, a minimum of -0.544 and a maximum of 0.680, so some banks lost more than half of their uninsured funding while others gained (p.6). The equity ratio averages 0.115 with a standard deviation of 0.067, brokered deposits average 0.015 but reach 0.605, and interest-rate swap notional averages 0.121 of assets while reaching 58.289 (p.6). The sample constraint is itself the finding: deposit data exist for about 4,550 banks but interest-rate derivative data for only about 1,200, and adding the derivative variables cuts the regression sample to 1,123 observations while lifting R-squared from 0.196 to 0.313 (p.5, p.8). The paper has only notional derivative positions, not net long or short exposure, so it cannot identify convex-payoff hedging; it is a single-period cross-section, not a causal design.

## 3. Maturity and counterparty type are the structure

`1906.08617v1` uses the complete panel of Russian domestic unsecured interbank loan contracts, 2.4 million loans aggregated into 57 monthly networks from January 2000 to October 2004, with each loan tagged into one of eight Central Bank of Russia maturity classes (p.2). The three short layers below 30 days contain 97% of all issued loans (p.5), which is exactly the argument for collapsing the network. The layered stochastic block model rejects that argument: complete aggregation is never the optimal granularity, and the optimum always lies between full separation and full aggregation (p.4). The number of statistically distinct lending patterns rises with market development, with the boundary between the early phase and the emerging-maturity phase at about month 35 (p.4), and the long layers carry the financing function while the short layers carry intermediation (p.5). The dataset is an issuance network rather than the exposure network used elsewhere, covers one country and one four-year window, and the model omits balance sheets and interest rates.

The two Japanese bank-firm studies agree on the shape of the structure from the other direction. `1407.5429v1` analyses 32 yearly bipartite networks from 1980 to 2011, and finds that the number of banks falls from 225 to 166 while firms rise from 1,414 to 3,034 and then fall to 2,706, with link density dropping from 0.0867 to 0.0398 (p.3). The largest community shrinks from 23 banks and 557 firms in 1980 to 6 banks and 905 firms in 2005 (p.7), the detected partitions agree across runs at a mean adjusted Rand index of about 0.55 for 1980-1995 rising to about 0.8 for 2000-2011 (p.3), and from 2000 a single star-like cluster of about 300 firms financed by "unknown-other financial institutions" carries roughly 20-30% of total credit (p.6). The links are unweighted, so volumes are ignored. `0901.2377v3` adds the concentration result over 1980-2005: private financial institutions accumulated loan losses of more than 80 trillion yen, nearly 15% of GDP, three major banks failed and two were nationalized in 1997 (p.2); in 2005 the 1.25% of domestic firms classified as large took about half of all loans (p.2); bank lending and bank degree have a rank correlation of 0.825 at 16 sigma, firm borrowing and firm degree 0.450 at 28.3 sigma, and lending scales with degree as a power law with exponent 0.69 plus or minus 0.03 (p.6). Its panel covers 229 net bank institutions and 2,330 firms (p.4, p.5), and it excludes interbank credit by construction (p.5), so it measures one side of the network only.

## 4. Leverage rules amplify the cycle

`1403.1637v1` models two consolidated sectors, banks that are risk neutral subject to a probability-of-insolvency constraint and risk-averse outside investors, trading a fixed supply of risky assets under common beliefs. The benchmark example sets the insolvency probability to 1%, the investors' currency share to 10%, the CRRA coefficient to 15 and the clearing price to $0.87, with banks holding 439 bonds, $168 of reserves, $62 of equity and $486 of deposits, and investors holding 611 bonds, $1,072 of equity, $54 of currency and $486 of deposits (p.8). The asymmetry is the result: a positive belief shock has a unique clearing price, while a negative shock can have several because bank demand rises as the price falls, and a strong enough negative shock leaves only the insolvent equilibrium (p.2, p.3). Because the adjustment is nonlinear and path dependent, successive positive and negative shocks of equal size do not cancel, and a catastrophe can occur when beliefs simply return to their starting point (p.3). A capital requirement set at 16% of assets can force additional selling in a declining market and produce a crisis for shocks that would otherwise be absorbed (p.2, p.8). The model has one risky asset, homogeneous sectors, fixed supply and risk-free bank liabilities, so it cannot be read as a calibrated statement about crisis frequency.

`1507.04136v1` generalises the policy question with an agent-based model of a leveraged bank, an unleveraged fundamentalist fund and a passive outside lender, where the leverage target is a power function of perceived risk, the target equalling the sum of current and baseline variance raised to a cyclicality parameter b in the interval [-0.5, 0.5]; negative b is procyclical and positive b countercyclical (p.11, p.12). Its central claim is that the optimum is not a constant: a procyclical Basel II-style policy is optimal when exogenous noise is high, volatility is strongly clustered, the bank is small and leverage is low, while in the opposite regime constant leverage is closer to optimal (p.7). The supporting empirical figure is explicitly labelled anecdotal, showing US broker-dealer leverage tracking the index up and the VIX down and institutions deleveraging by a factor of two inside one quarter (p.4). The model is roughly calibrated, holds fixed portfolio weights, has no default and uses a single risky asset.

## 5. What actually limits bank lending

`0904.1426v4` works through the arithmetic. In the textbook fractional-reserve model with a 10% reserve requirement, the money supply expands ten times the initial deposit and the loan supply nine times (p.3). In the United States the reserve requirement applies only to Net Transaction Accounts, at 10%, and not to non-personal time deposits or Euro currency liabilities (p.4). Capital rules then bind instead: a well-capitalised bank must hold regulatory capital of at least 10% of risk-adjusted loans, and because mortgages carry a 50% risk weight a bank can lend twice as much against the same capital in mortgages as in other loans (p.4); under Basel the minimum ratio of Tier 1 plus Tier 2 capital to loans is 8%, and subordinated term debt must have an original maturity over five years, is amortised at 20% per year in its last five years, and is capped at 50% of Tier 1 (p.10). The paper's two-bank worked example then shows the loophole: with a 10% reserve requirement and a capital multiplier of 10, a bank that securitises a loan and sells it to a depositor of another bank can repeat the cycle indefinitely without breaking either constraint (p.7, p.8).

`1204.1583v1` gives the bookkeeping that makes this legible, as a purely descriptive specification with no data. The worked examples assume a 2% reserve requirement on European accounts with notice periods up to two years, and start from two banks each holding 10,000 of loans, 800 of cash, 200 of reserves, 10,000 of deposits and 1,000 of capital, against a central bank of 400 (p.3). Reserve accounts at the central bank are liabilities of the central bank (p.4); a same-bank transfer is a debit to one account and a credit to another with no change to the bank's aggregate liability (p.5); a cross-bank transfer of 20 clears through reserve accounts (p.6); lending is constrained by the risk-weighted capital limit and by the reserve requirement on the newly created deposits, illustrated by a 500 loan that moves 10 from cash into reserves (p.7, p.8). Reserve requirements apply only to some account types, so they throttle deposit expansion rather than cap it (p.8). A repayment of 100 splits into 40 of principal, which removes the deposit the loan created, and 60 of interest, which is money-supply neutral (p.10); and a loan written off against income does not reduce the capital base, so the bank can keep lending on the same capital subject to liquidity (p.10).

`1702.08774v1` formalises the boundary case. With a narrow reserve base of currency only, the model reproduces the textbook bound; with a broad base that includes interbank lending, interbank credit becomes an additional money aggregate and the potential offer of bank credit becomes unbound (p.13, p.24). In simulation with ten banks, a thousand customers, base money of 1 billion, total capital of 100 million, a 10% required reserve ratio, a punitive central-bank spread of 0.03 and coordination quality of 0, 0.4 and 0.8 (p.22), perfect pooling makes central-bank recourse virtually non-existent, while distressed pooling makes it the most frequent and most intense outcome, with the system needing assistance almost permanently (p.26, p.27). Under lower coordination bank equity stays thinner and profits are lower, so banks cannot rebuild equity from retained earnings (p.27). The model assumes no customer or interbank default and no dividends, so the magnitudes are illustrative.

## 6. The parts of the balance sheet that are not measured

`1309.2130v4` starts from a distributional anomaly in the Forbes Global 2000 and turns it into a measurement. Financial firms are about 30% of the listed firms and about 30% of sales, profits and market value over 2003-2012, yet they hold 70% of total assets in the 2004 list, rising to 87% in 2013 (p.1). The largest firm in 2013 is Fannie Mae at $3.2 trillion and the twelfth is Royal Bank of Scotland at $2.13 trillion, comparable to UK GDP of $2.4 trillion (p.1). Zipf's law holds only from the twentieth-largest company downward, and if it held at the top Fannie Mae would be ten times RBS, at $21.3 trillion (p.2). The authors define a shadow-banking index as the summed gap between the fitted power-law size and the actual size over the 1,000 largest firms, and argue that it is an upper bound while the Financial Stability Board's entity-based estimate is a lower bound; the FSB series runs from $26 trillion in 2002 to $62 trillion in 2007 and $67 trillion in 2011 (p.5). Calibrating a proportional-random-growth model with a leakage parameter gives a peak before the crisis and a flow of about $3.5 trillion a year into the shadow system (p.7).

The paper is also a case study in how coverage distorts a cross-section. The number of financial firms in the list ranges from 501 in the 2013 list to 597 in the 2008 list (p.9), while total financial-firm assets rise from $48 trillion in 2004 to $138 trillion in 2013, twice world GDP, against non-financial assets of about $20 trillion in both years (p.9). Its own comparison to an external estimate is instructive: Standard and Poor's put outstanding Chinese shadow-banking credit at $3.8 trillion at the end of 2012, equal to 34% of on-balance-sheet loans and 44% of GDP and 5.6 times the Financial Stability Board's 2011 estimate for the country (p.8). The index depends on the fitted exponent in the middle of the distribution and on the assumption that proportional random growth would hold absent the anomaly, and it cannot say which entities or activities the missing mass belongs to.

## 7. Credit risk changes over the life of a position

`2211.09176v3` takes loan-level performance data from SEC EDGAR for four 2017-vintage consumer auto ABS trusts, starting from 55,000, 67,797, 80,636 and 72,515 loans (p.9) and filtering to 72- and 73-month used-car loans with no co-borrower, stated-not-verified income, no subvention and less than 18 months of age at first report, plus an integrity check that removes 2,630 loans or 4.3% of the filtered population (p.10). Risk bands are defined by original APR, with super-prime at 0-5%, prime at 5-10%, near-prime at 10-15%, subprime at 15-20% and deep subprime at 20% or more (p.11). The filtered 58,118 loans are 37% deep subprime and 37% subprime, with 4% super-prime, and 37% defaulted overall, ranging from 52% of deep subprime to 4% of super-prime; 23% are censored and 40% repaid (p.13, p.14). The largest single trust contributes 28,920 loans or 50% of the sample and the smallest 2,171 or 4% (p.13, p.14).

Estimating a discrete-time competing-risks cause-specific hazard, the authors find that conditional default risk converges across bands beginning after about one year, and that loans as different as subprime and super-prime behave alike after 42-48 months of payments (p.4). The consumer-finance implication is that borrowers who improve are slow to refinance, leaving $11-61 per month, or $193-1,616 in total, for the riskiest borrowers and $13-56 per month, or $160-2,327, for near-prime and prime borrowers (p.6). The loans are dispersed across states and manufacturers, with the top five state concentrations at Texas 13%, Florida 12%, California 9%, Georgia 7% and North Carolina 4%, and the top five makes at Nissan 13%, Chevrolet 10%, Ford 7%, Toyota 7% and Hyundai 7% (p.12). The convergence is measured on one asset class, two vintages and a period containing the 2020 shock, and the sample is deliberately a tail, so the point estimates are not transferable without re-estimation.

## Caveats

Four of the twelve papers contain no data at all: `1403.1637v1`, `1507.04136v1` and `1702.08774v1` are models whose numbers are illustrative parameters, and `1204.1583v1` is a descriptive bookkeeping specification with worked ledgers. The empirical papers are narrow by construction: `1202.3182v1` is one quiescent week in February 2007 in one country and relies on knowing the target rate; `1906.08617v1` is one country over four years and uses issuance rather than exposure networks; `0901.2377v3` and `1407.5429v1` are the same Japanese bank-firm dataset over 1980-2005 and 1980-2011 and both exclude interbank credit; `2506.03344v1` is a single hiking cycle; `2211.09176v3` is one asset class in two vintages. The slice also disagrees with itself. `1403.1637v1` finds that a capital requirement can be destabilising because it forces selling, while `1507.04136v1` finds an interior optimum in which procyclical control is best only for small banks in noisy markets; `0904.1426v4` and `1204.1583v1` argue that lending is not bound by reserves, while `1702.08774v1` derives an unbound case only under a broad reserve base and no defaults. Nothing here establishes that a funding structure or a capital rule improves or degrades a trading strategy's P&L, nothing measures prediction-market or crypto funding, and no paper in this read set links funding composition to order flow or to execution cost.

## Papers read in depth

- `1202.3182v1` - Loan and nonloan flows in the Australian interbank network (2012). Reconstructs an RTGS week and shows overnight lending is the residual of payment imbalance; one week, one country, target rate assumed known.
- `1702.08774v1` - Interbank Credit and the Money Manufacturing Process (2017). Agent-based model showing interbank credit adds a money aggregate and can make credit supply unbound; no empirical calibration and no defaults.
- `2506.03344v1` - Hedging Deposit Run Risk Prior to the 2023 Regional Banking Crisis (2025). Cross-section of 4,551 banks showing run risk tracked funding composition and was unhedged; notional-only derivatives and one period.
- `1906.08617v1` - Loan maturity aggregation in interbank lending networks obscures mesoscale structure and economic functions (2019). Layered block model on 2.4 million Russian loans; aggregation always rejected; issuance network, one country.
- `1407.5429v1` - Bank-firm credit network in Japan. An analysis of a bipartite network (2014). Tracks 32 yearly communities and their over-expressed attributes; unweighted links and stochastic partitions.
- `0901.2377v3` - Structure and temporal change of the credit network between banks and large firms in Japan (2009). Eigenvalue fragility scores on a weighted bipartite graph; interbank credit excluded and surviving firms only.
- `1403.1637v1` - Inside Money, Procyclical Leverage, and Banking Catastrophes (2014). Shows asymmetric response to shocks and that a capital floor can force selling; stylised two-sector model with illustrative parameters.
- `1507.04136v1` - Taming the Basel Leverage Cycle (2015). Agent-based leverage-cycle model with a cyclicality parameter; the optimal policy depends on bank size and noise; roughly calibrated, single asset.
- `1204.1583v1` - Description of the Operational Mechanics of a Basel Regulated Banking System (2012). Worked double-entry specification of lending, repayment, defaults and reserves; no data and regional conventions vary.
- `0904.1426v4` - What are the limits on Commercial Bank Lending? (2009). Arithmetic of reserve and capital limits plus a securitisation loophole; descriptive only, no estimated coefficients.
- `1309.2130v4` - The Interrupted Power Law and The Size of Shadow Banking (2013). Derives a shadow-banking index from missing top-tail mass; relies on a fitted exponent and cannot identify entities.
- `2211.09176v3` - On the Convergence of Credit Risk in Current Consumer Automobile Loans (2022). Competing-risks hazards show credit risk converges with loan age; one asset class, two vintages, left-truncated sample.
