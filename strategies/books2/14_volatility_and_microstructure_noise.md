# Volatility, jumps and microstructure noise: hybrid volatility forecasts beat risk models, and most jumps are not news

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers a slice of the arXiv q-fin.GN (General Finance) harvest that speaks to volatility measurement and forecasting outside the market-microstructure literature: 86 records spanning 1998-2026, 19 of them with a journal reference, from which 40 candidates were screened and 12 papers read in depth. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets. It deliberately avoids the ground the companion trading-corpus brief already covers (the size of microstructure noise, the Epps effect, rough volatility) and instead asks four general-finance questions: whether a better volatility point forecast buys a better risk forecast, what implied volatility adds over realized measures, how volatility models depend on regime and market structure, and how jumps and extreme moves can be measured.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from pure forecasting horse races to the economics of implied volatility and the anatomy of price jumps. The most consequential single finding is that machine-learning hybrids and sentiment features improve point volatility forecasts substantially while leaving the tail-risk forecasts they are supposed to serve no better, or worse: a GARCH-GRU hybrid cuts the S&P 500 volatility MSE from 0.0575 to 0.0159 yet fails the Expected Shortfall backtest, and the best Value-at-Risk models are the plain GARCH variants it was meant to replace `2310.01063v1` (pp.13-15).

## The short answer

1. A GARCH-GRU hybrid cuts the S&P 500 volatility MSE from 0.0575 to 0.0159 and the Bitcoin MSE from 5.8356 to 0.3818, but the resulting Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall models are not the hybrid ones and the hybrid Expected Shortfall tests fail `2310.01063v1` (pp.13-18).
2. Sentiment toward macroeconomic announcements improves volatility forecasts by 14.99% on average on the top-10% highest-volatility days but reduces accuracy by 1.38% on the other 90% of days `2503.19767v1` (pp.28-29).
3. A graph-spectral HAR that embeds directional volatility spillover beats plain HAR, VHAR, HAR-KS and a GNN-HAR for more than half of 24 global indices at daily, weekly and monthly horizons `2410.22706v3` (p.20).
4. Which spillover network you build decides whether it tracks crises: a Pearson-correlation graph stays calm through the 2008 and COVID-19 spikes, while a Diebold-Yilmaz magnetic-Laplacian graph rises with them `2410.22706v3` (p.3).
5. Volatility persistence is not constant: a time-varying persistence model beats the time-varying HAR at 22 days with relative RMSE 0.807 for crude oil, 0.923 for natural gas and 0.880 for gasoline `2402.01354v2` (p.20).
6. Implied volatility is informationally inefficient: after a negative return jump the at-the-money implied volatility rises an additional 0.218% over the next 60 minutes, gradually rather than at once `1810.12200v1` (p.3).
7. Equity index option risk premia contain unspanned jump and local-time risk: average weekly straddle returns are -10% and 3% out-of-the-money call returns are -59%, with upside and downside jump-crossing premia statistically at par `2303.16371v1` (pp.15-16).
8. Most price jumps are not news: only about 4.3% of 37,452 jumps in 301 US stocks fall within three minutes of a news item, and the co-jump size distribution follows a power law with exponent about 0.95 `2404.16467v1` (pp.3,8).
9. Volatility clustering and a power-law return tail close to exponent 3 can be produced by a purely linear model of news-driven investors plus trend-following speculators, with no nonlinearity `2309.04876v1` (pp.4,9).
10. Introducing CSI 300 index futures coincided with lower spot volatility and weaker asymmetry: the daily standard deviation falls from 2.4603 to 1.4171 and the TGARCH bad-news coefficient from 0.08833 to 0.07492 `2109.15060v1` (pp.10,16).
11. In German electricity, a matrix-HAR realized-covariation forecast improves the R-squared of spread risk premium prediction from 0.244 to 0.472 relative to a backward-looking rolling variance `2606.05991v1` (p.15).
12. A restricted multivariate GARCH with only six parameters produces covariances competitive with DCC and DECO, and shows the leverage effect is a filtering artifact rather than a driver `1609.07051v6` (pp.14,16).

## 1. Forecast accuracy versus risk accuracy

`2310.01063v1` combines four GARCH specifications (GARCH, EGARCH, GJR-GARCH, APARCH) with a three-layer GRU network and tests the result on S&P 500, Bitcoin and gold daily returns over 2707 observations each (p.11, Table 1). The target of the neural component is the Garman-Klass-Yang-Zhang range volatility, not a risk measure (p.7), and this choice matters. On the S&P 500 the hybrid cuts MSE from 0.0575 to 0.0159 for the skewed-t GARCH and from 0.1350 to 0.0152 for the skewed-t EGARCH, with Diebold-Mariano p-values below 0.05 throughout (p.13, Table 2). On Bitcoin the pure GARCH MSE of 5.6795 falls to 0.4042 and the APARCH-GRU reaches 0.3818 against 5.835 for the plain APARCH (p.16, Table 5; p.18, Table 7). The gain, however, stops at the point forecast. For the S&P 500 the models with the best Value-at-Risk coverage are a GJR-GARCH-GRU at the 5% level and a plain APARCH with skewed-t at the 1% level, and both hybrid GJR models fail the Expected Shortfall backtest (p.15, Table 4); the paper's own conclusion is that no specification wins everywhere and that the hybrid advantage does not translate into risk (p.22). The magnitude of the improvement is real; its relevance to position sizing is not established.

`2503.19767v1` makes the same distinction from the other direction. Using attention and sentiment indices for ten scheduled US macroeconomic variables, built from Google Trends, Wikipedia, newspapers and Twitter, the complete-subset sentiment model beats the HAR benchmark in 98.76% of 404 stocks with an average MSE improvement of 12.74% (p.25, Table 5). Splitting the sample by realized volatility shows where the gain lives: on the top-10% volatility days the sentiment model's improvement rises to 14.99% and the attention model's to 9.64%, but on the other 90% of days the same sentiment model reduces accuracy by 1.38% and the attention model by 2.15% (pp.28-29, Tables 8-9). Attention to FOMC meetings is the single most important variable across sources (p.30, Table 10). The lesson for a live system is that these features pay only in the regime where they are needed, and cost elsewhere.

`2410.22706v3` attacks forecast accuracy through the cross-section rather than the feature set. On 24 stock market indices with about 3500 common trading days from May 2002 to June 2022, at 5-minute realized volatility, 70% in-sample and 30% out-of-sample (p.17), the GSP-HAR model is selected by the Model Confidence Set for more than 20 of the 24 indices and achieves the lowest MSE and MAE for more than half of them at horizons of 1, 5 and 22 days (pp.19-20, Tables 1-3). The headline improvement is modest per index but broad, and it depends on the network construction: a graph-signal-energy diagnostic shows the Pearson-correlation network has low energy during the European debt crisis and COVID-19 while the directional magnetic-Laplacian network raises energy in exactly those periods (p.3). A mis-specified graph can therefore forecast reasonably while representing the wrong object.

`2402.01354v2` shows that even a well-chosen linear memory structure is not enough if persistence itself drifts. Modeling the volatility of crude oil, natural gas and gasoline futures with a time-varying extended Wold decomposition, the model's advantage over a time-varying HAR grows with the horizon: relative RMSE at 22 days is 0.807 for crude oil, 0.923 for natural gas and 0.880 for gasoline (p.20, Table 1), and the advantage weakens in the calm 1993-1999 sample where transitory shocks dominate (p.24, Table 2). `2606.05991v1` reaches a similar conclusion for electricity: the best matrix-HAR realized-covariation forecasts include a quarterly horizon that standard daily-weekly-monthly HAR omits, reach a Mincer-Zarnowitz R-squared of 40-45% (p.8), and use renewable generation and price level as state variables (p.11, Table 1).

## 2. The cross-section: spillover, factors and betas

`2410.22706v3` is the clearest statement in the slice that volatility is a cross-sectional object. The Diebold-Yilmaz framework supplies a directed spillover network, the magnetic Laplacian preserves direction, and the graph Fourier transform moves the whole system into a spectral domain where a HAR filter is applied to the real and imaginary parts separately before an inverse transform (pp.13-15). The result is that a global network, not a set of pairwise correlations, carries the incremental information. The diagnostic role of graph-signal energy is the part most likely to transfer: it is model-agnostic and can be computed before any forecast is made.

`1609.07051v6` provides the parametric counterpart for a single market. On 356 S&P constituents over 1995-2013 it restricts the conditional covariance to one market eigenvalue plus N-1 degenerate equal factors, which makes the inverse analytic and leaves only six GARCH parameters to estimate (p.1). The empirical covariance matrix has a largest eigenvalue of 104 against an order-one bulk (p.5), which is what makes the restriction defensible. The covariances are competitive: RMSE against the empirical covariances is 2.1390 for the restricted model, against 1.4018 for DCC, 3.0633 for DECO and 0.9513 for OG-GARCH (p.14, Table 2), at a small fraction of the parameter count. Because the factors are time-varying, the model also delivers daily betas, and the sector risk measure computed from them dates the shift of market leadership from information technology to financials to 2006 (p.16).

`2606.05991v1` shows what the cross-section costs in a market with a different structure. Electricity prices are averages over delivery periods, so the object being modeled is an infinite-dimensional covariance operator observed through daily auctions; the realized covariation identifies a smoothed effective variance rather than the integrated variance, with mean-reversion accounting for more than 40% of total variation (p.2). Modeling the diagonal (variances) and the correlation matrix separately, and pooling the diagonal regression across hours because a single level factor drives about 60% of weekly integrated variance (p.5), is what makes the estimation tractable. Off-diagonal calibration is tested on four net-zero-volume spread portfolios, where the models produce variance ratios statistically indistinguishable from one (p.13, Table 2) and 95% prediction intervals with 94.7% realized coverage (p.14, Table 3).

## 3. Implied versus realized: information and risk premia

`1810.12200v1` tests whether option markets incorporate jump news at once, using minute-by-minute S&P 500 index option data over 2006-2010 and a Lee-Mykland jump test (p.2). They do not. After a negative return jump, at-the-money implied volatility rises an additional 0.218% over the following 60 minutes beyond the simultaneous move (p.3), and the drift is persistent and directed; after a positive jump the initial reaction largely reverts within the hour. The asymmetry is also across moneyness: implied volatility from out-of-the-money calls converges within the jump window, while at-the-money and out-of-the-money-put implied volatilities keep drifting (pp.13-14, Tables 3-4). The economic interpretation is that the smile is updated asymmetrically, and the paper is careful that once actual bid-ask spreads are imposed the mid-price statistical arbitrage disappears (p.3). The evidence is single-market and single-period, but it is a clean rejection of instantaneous adjustment.

`2405.11730v1` extends the same theme to the shape of the surface and to a different market. Using 630,000 posts from a Chinese stock forum over 2014-2023 and SSE 50 ETF options from February 2015 to December 2023 (pp.1,4), it decomposes sentiment into high- and low-frequency components and finds that high-frequency sentiment correlates more strongly with at-the-money implied volatility while low-frequency sentiment correlates more strongly with deep out-of-the-money implied volatility (pp.9-11, Tables 1-2). Adding the sentiment components to a VAR improves next-day surface prediction, with the BERT-BigBird sentiment model reaching mean percentage errors of 20.83%, 16.19% and 28.09% for one-, three- and twelve-month expiries against 22.21% for the no-sentiment model (p.16, Table 5). The gains are modest and the market is retail-dominated, but the frequency decomposition is a reusable idea.

`2303.16371v1` supplies the economics behind negative option premia. Using S&P 500 weekly options and farther-dated contracts over 415 weekly cycles from January 2011 to December 2018 (p.13), it shows that average straddle excess returns are -10% unconditionally, 3% out-of-the-money call excess returns are -59%, and 3% out-of-the-money put excess returns are -61% (p.15). Bootstrap confidence intervals put the upside and downside jump-crossing risk premia statistically at par (p.16), and 88-day options imply negative local-time risk premia, that is, a dislike of unspanned volatility risk (p.17). The decomposition into jumps crossing the strike and local time is model-free and rests on Tanaka's formula, so the conclusion is not a parametric artifact. It says that deep out-of-the-money option premia are not simply the other side of a positive equity premium.

## 4. Regime dependence and structural change

`2109.15060v1` is a natural experiment in market structure. Before the CSI 300 index futures launched on 16 April 2010 the daily return standard deviation was 2.4603; in the following three years it was 1.4171 (p.10, Table 4.1). A GARCH(1,1) estimated on either side reports an ARCH coefficient that falls from 0.05704 to -0.021965 and a GARCH coefficient that falls from 0.937384 to -0.500689 (p.15), and a TGARCH(1,1) reports the bad-news coefficient falling from 0.08833 to 0.07492 and the total bad-news effect from 0.091522 to 0.03568 (p.16). Granger causality between spot and futures returns is bidirectional at lags 3 to 10, with futures leading spot at lags 1 and 2 (p.16). The paper's negative coefficient values are unusual and should be treated as a specification warning rather than a structural estimate, but the direction of the regime change is consistent with the descriptive statistics. The operational point is that a volatility model fitted across a listing event is fitted across two regimes.

`2402.01354v2` gives the same message without a market-structure shock. Its persistence decomposition shows the oil volatility of 2014-2022 driven by 64- to 128-day shocks that generate more than 50% of the variance around COVID-19, then by 2- to 4-day transitory shocks accounting for about 35-40% after February 2022, while the calm 1993-1999 period is transitory-driven throughout (pp.16-17, pp.22-23). `1609.07051v6` recovers the market transition of 2006 from daily betas (p.16), and `2606.05991v1` builds a regime variable from the trace of the realized covariation into the variance regression (p.7). In all three cases the regime variable is estimated from the data rather than imposed, which is the practical requirement if a live system is to detect a change instead of being told about it.

## 5. Measuring jumps and extreme moves

`2404.16467v1` builds a wavelet-scattering embedding of each jump's return profile, 42 coefficients from 119 one-minute rescaled returns in a two-hour window, and clusters them without labels (p.4). Applied to 301 US stocks from January 2015 to December 2022, within the 10:30-15:00 window, it identifies 37,452 jumps, of which 16,127 belong to 2534 co-jumps and 21,325 are single (p.3). Only about 4.3% of jumps fall within three minutes of a news item (p.3), and 60% of jumps have a positive mean-reversion score, meaning the price partly reverses the jump (p.6). The co-jump size distribution is a power law with exponent about 0.95 and a cutoff near 100 stocks (p.8), which the authors read as a critical branching or contagion signature; 20% of co-jumps involve only symmetric or anticipatory profiles, and 76% of those are two-stock events (p.9). The largest co-jump in the sample, 248 stocks in one minute, is dated 24 September 2019 (p.3). The method is unsupervised and needs no news feed, which makes it usable where news labels are incomplete.

`2303.16371v1` measures the priced side of the same phenomenon: jumps crossing the strike and local time are the components that must be negative for out-of-the-money calls and straddles to earn negative premia (pp.9-10), and the weekly data isolate the jump-crossing channel from the local-time channel (p.13). `1810.12200v1` measures the option-market response to a detected jump directly and finds the response lagged and asymmetric (pp.3,13-14). `2503.19767v1` contributes the standard realized-variance jump decomposition using median realized volatility and a signed jump (p.14). The three together give a consistent picture: jumps are common, mostly endogenous, and priced in a way that a symmetric realized-variance model does not capture.

## 6. What makes volatility cluster: linear microfoundations

`2309.04876v1` argues that the two canonical regularities, a power-law return tail and clustered volatility, need no nonlinear agent machinery. Two trader types with linear demand, a news-driven random walk in investor valuations and a first-order autoregressive speculative anticipated return with coefficient 0.99 produce both a power-law tail with exponent near 3 and long-range volatility clustering (pp.4,9), in simulations of 10,000 periods (p.11, Table 1). The paper is explicit that the purely news-driven model generates clustering but is non-stationary, and that the purely speculative model generates the tail but not the clustering; the mixture is what does both (pp.7-9). The claim is theoretical and simulation-only, with no empirical calibration, so it is a mechanism argument rather than a measurement. Its value for this platform is as a caution: matching clustered volatility in a simulator is weak evidence that the simulator's mechanism is right, because a two-equation linear model already matches it.

`2109.15060v1` adds the empirical counterpart: a GARCH(1,1) on the CSI 300 gives ARCH plus GARCH coefficients summing to 0.9939, close enough to one to signal near-integrated persistence (p.14), and that persistence weakens after index futures begin trading. `2402.01354v2` shows the persistence is heterogeneous rather than a single near-unit-root parameter (pp.16-17). The practical reading is that the GARCH persistence parameter is a reduced-form summary of a changing mixture of shock durations, and treating it as structural will mislead a forecast exactly when the mixture changes.

## Caveats

No paper in this read set establishes that any of these forecast improvements transfers to this platform's venues or instruments. The hybrid GARCH-GRU result is on three assets, two of which are not equities, and its own authors decline to claim that the point-forecast gain reaches risk. The sentiment results are single-market (US equities or a Chinese retail forum) and single-period, and the Chinese forum work rests on a market whose participant mix is unusual. The jump classification is one US equity sample over 2015-2022 with a fixed intraday window and a 250-stock exclusion rule, so the 4.3% news-relation figure is a lower bound on exogenous jumps, not an estimate of reflexivity. The dark-matter evidence is index options over one eight-year window, and its bootstrap intervals are wide. The electricity work is one generation zone, and the CSI 300 result spans a listing event that is not repeatable. The slice also disagrees with itself in one place: the general-finance material treats volatility as a persistent, forecastable object with stable enough dynamics to model, while the time-varying-persistence and regime results say the persistence parameter is itself unstable. No paper here establishes the economic size of any trading edge net of costs, and none of these documents is trading advice.
