# Regimes, breakpoints and state switching: the only regime model that survived out of sample was the one filtered online

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads eight papers from the arXiv `q-fin.GN` harvest: the eighteen records whose titles or abstracts concern regimes, structural breaks, change-points or state switching, published between 2006 and 2026, three of them with a journal reference. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, who needs to know how a regime or change-point detector is validated, how it behaves in real time rather than in hindsight, and what breaks when the detector is used inside a strategy. The slice is small and methodologically uneven, so the brief is deliberately concrete: it separates what a detector can be shown to do on a full sample from what it can be shown to do as bars arrive.

## What this covers

The slice moves in one direction. It opens with retrospective dating of breaks in historical and macroeconomic series (`0710.0745v1`, `0905.3873v1`, `physics/0607197v1`), moves to hidden-state models that describe volatility and return regimes in sample (`2011.03741v2`, `1410.6005v1`), and closes with two systems that actually put a state model in front of a portfolio (`2309.00875v3`, `2407.19858v7`), plus one risk application where the regime is only a conditioning device (`2604.21734v1`). The single most consequential finding is that the only paper here that both fitted a regime model and traded it out of sample won with an online filter that re-estimates as data arrives, not with a state label read off a full-sample fit: the filter-based prediction-interval rule returned 15.18% annualized with a Sharpe of 1.18, while a purely backward-looking increment rule on the same spread lost money (`2309.00875v3`, p.21).

## The short answer

1. A regime model earns its keep only when it is filtered and re-estimated online; the one paper that did this out of sample returned 15.18% annualized (SR 1.18) against -2.44% (SR -0.15) for a backward-looking increment rule (`2309.00875v3`, p.21).
2. Change-point detection is retrospective by construction: the search assumes the whole series is observed and the authors state it "has not a predictive goal" (`0710.0745v1`, p.7).
3. Break dates come as intervals, not triggers: Mexico's four integration breaks are dated 1992:12, 1994:12, 2001:5 and 2005:12 with 95% windows about a year wide (`0905.3873v1`, p.6).
4. A hidden state is mostly a volatility state: the four-state Bitcoin model splits into a high-volatility bear state (daily sd 5.91%), two bull states (2.49% and 1.69%) and a calm state (0.53%) (`2011.03741v2`, p.11).
5. The margin over a random walk is thin: the best crypto model averages CRPS 1.78 on Bitcoin against 1.98 for the random-walk benchmark over thirty one-step forecasts (`2011.03741v2`, p.8).
6. Regime conditioning flips a textbook relationship: the risk-return trade-off is positive and significant only in low-volatility states, and non-significant or negative in high-volatility states (`1410.6005v1`, p.10).
7. Persistence is state-dependent, so a pooled estimate is biased: lagged-variance coefficients exceed unity in high-volatility states while the low-volatility state has the lower value (`1410.6005v1`, p.12).
8. Regime duration must match sampling frequency: a two-state model persists across quarters but alternates almost every week at weekly frequency on the same loss series (`2604.21734v1`, p.14).
9. A macro covariate helps only where a regime exists: adding VSTOXX cut the weekly two-state exceedance MSE from 1.55 to 0.923 for internal fraud, and did nothing for physical damage or system failures (`2604.21734v1`, p.15-16).
10. The number of states is not identified by the data: a three-regime Markov-switching model for a 19th-century spread was rejected for instability, and the paper calls the choice of regime count "quite subjective" (`0710.0745v1`, p.6).
11. Costs decide whether a regime signal is tradeable: the estimated round-trip cost is 53.71 bps for Shanghai crude futures against 5.80 bps for Brent (`2309.00875v3`, p.12).
12. A regime-aware trading system still reports a negative information ratio against its benchmark: the energy HMM plus neural network returned 82.76% with a Sharpe of 0.77 but an information ratio of -0.1 (`2407.19858v7`, p.16-18).

## 1. The models in this slice, and the one that traded

Five distinct model families appear. The earliest is a two-regime Markov-switching autoregression fitted to the spread between the highest and lowest gold-silver price across London, Paris and Hamburg, combined with a self-organising map for periodisation (`0710.0745v1`, p.5-6). A hidden Markov model with two to five states and exogenous predictors is fitted to Bitcoin, Ether and Ripple returns (`2011.03741v2`, p.5). A regime-switching GARCH multi-factor model with state-dependent prices of risk is fitted to US monthly equity returns (`1410.6005v1`, p.6-7). A bivariate hidden Markov model with a macro covariate is fitted to operational-loss data (`2604.21734v1`, p.8). And a mean-reverting autoregressive process modulated by a hidden Markov chain is fitted to a three-contract crude-oil cointegration spread (`2309.00875v3`, p.4).

Only two of these are connected to a portfolio, and only one is evaluated out of sample. The crude-oil paper trains on data through 30 June 2022 and tests on the following year, 261 daily observations (`2309.00875v3`, p.13). It selects two states for the latent chain by minimising mean forecast error, and finds both regimes highly persistent, with the second regime's self-transition probability close to one (`2309.00875v3`, p.18-19). The energy paper runs a five-state Gaussian HMM alongside a five-input neural network, requiring a three-year warm-up before it trades (`2407.19858v7`, p.6, p.10). Everything else in the slice is an in-sample description with no trading rule attached.

## 2. Change-point detection is retrospective by construction

Three papers search for breaks in a fixed sample, and the difference between that and a live detector is stated, not implied. The monetary-history paper is explicit: the whole series is assumed observed and change-points are computed a posteriori, so the method "has not a predictive goal" (`0710.0745v1`, p.7). It finds seven change-points in the mean of the spread and four in both mean and variance, and reads them as three shock episodes - a speculative one beginning in week 21 of 1824, an institutional one from week 45 of 1839, and a technological one from week 46 of 1850 - each absorbed after one to four years (`0710.0745v1`, p.7-9). The economics are convincing; the timing is not available to a trader watching the series arrive.

The Mexico paper makes the same trade explicit in its method: it first estimates a time-varying degree of integration from a conditional CAPM with segmentation, then runs a Bai-Perron mean-shift search over the resulting series with a minimum segment length (`0905.3873v1`, p.5). It reports four break dates with confidence intervals that cover roughly a year before and after each date, and the confidence interval is the honest output: a break is located, not signalled (`0905.3873v1`, p.6). The integration degree itself averages 57% over January 1988 to February 2008 and is significantly time-varying, with a Wald statistic of 176.10 on three degrees of freedom and a p-value of 0.000 (`0905.3873v1`, p.6).

The lead-lag paper is the most careful about causality. Its thermal optimal path method searches a distance matrix for the best-matching path between two series, and the generalisation here is a mixed distance that takes the minimum of the co-monotonic and anti-monotonic distances so that a sign change in the correlation does not break the path search (`physics/0607197v1`, p.4). On a synthetic series with a lag of 10 in the first third, a lag of 5 with a sign flip in the second third and a lead of 5 in the last third, the mixed distance recovers the path where the single-sign distances each fail on one segment (`physics/0607197v1`, p.7-8). The reconstructed sign is then smoothed with a Savitzky-Golay filter using only points to the left of each time step, which is the one place in this slice where a filter is constructed to be causal on purpose (`physics/0607197v1`, p.9).

## 3. A hidden state is a volatility state, not a direction

When the states are finally described, they are almost always volatility bands. The four-state Bitcoin model assigns 35% of time to a state with average return 0.46% and standard deviation 5.91%, 30% to a state with 0.49% and 2.49%, 24% to 0.47% and 1.69%, and 11% to 0.02% and 0.53% (`2011.03741v2`, p.11). The two "bull" states have nearly identical means and differ in kurtosis, which is a warning that state labels can be splitting second moments rather than first moments. For Ether the process alternates between a high-volatility state (sd 8.90%) and a low-volatility state (2.61%) for about 95% of the sample, and Ripple spends 80% of its time in two states whose standard deviations are 4.46% and 1.77% (`2011.03741v2`, p.11).

The regime-switching GARCH paper reaches the same conclusion from the other direction. Its two states are identified by their variances, with median scaled market variance 8.5023 in state 1 against 19.8916 in state 2, and the authors use that to label state 1 low-volatility and state 2 high-volatility (`1410.6005v1`, p.12). A detector built on these states is therefore a volatility detector, and any strategy that treats the state as a directional forecast is using it for something the fit does not support. The crude-oil paper's two regimes differ in long-run mean and volatility while having comparable mean-reversion speeds, which is consistent: the state changes the level and variance of the spread, not its speed of adjustment (`2309.00875v3`, p.18).

## 4. Real time versus hindsight: filter, smooth, or cheat

The operational-risk paper states the distinction cleanly: the smoothed posterior uses observations from `n+1` to `N` as well as the past, while the filtered posterior uses only past and present, and the two must not be confused (`2604.21734v1`, p.6). This is the single most reusable sentence in the slice. A regime plot drawn from smoothed probabilities will look decisive and will not be reproducible live, and the paper uses smoothed marginals only for parameter estimation and decoding, never for the one-step prediction (`2604.21734v1`, p.6, p.9).

The crude-oil paper is the counter-example that shows what causal discipline buys. It uses a filter-based EM algorithm whose recursions update without re-running the whole pass when a new observation arrives, and it re-estimates parameters every ten observations rather than every step (`2309.00875v3`, p.6-8). It documents that the filtered parameter estimates are unstable at the start of the sample and move around the onset of the war in Ukraine, coinciding with a volatility spike (`2309.00875v3`, p.18). That instability is the real-time cost of the approach, and it is disclosed rather than hidden. The reward is that the resulting prediction-interval strategy is the best of five over the test year, and the White Reality Check repeatedly identifies it as the best performer in the set (`2309.00875v3`, p.21-22).

The energy paper sits between the two. It retrains on a fixed schedule and uses the forward algorithm to predict the next state, which is a filtered object, but the underlying model is fitted on a three-year window with no reported out-of-sample re-estimation schedule (`2407.19858v7`, p.10-11). Its reported numbers - 82.76% total return, Sharpe 0.77, maximum drawdown 17.1%, turnover 3.29% and capacity of $10M - come from a single backtest over January 2019 to January 2022, so they describe one path, not a distribution (`2407.19858v7`, p.16-18).

## 5. Regime conditioning flips the sign of a relationship

The most useful structural result in the slice is that pooling regimes can invert a sign. On 724 monthly US excess market returns from March 1953 to June 2013, the linear multi-factor model finds a positive but non-significant market price of risk, the classic "no trade-off" result (`1410.6005v1`, p.9). Splitting into two volatility states recovers a positive and significant price of risk in the low-volatility state across every proxy for the hedging component, while the high-volatility state shows no clear pattern and is negative at extreme volatility (`1410.6005v1`, p.10). The low-volatility state occupies 71% to 77% of the sample, so a quarter of the data was enough to blur the pooled estimate (`1410.6005v1`, p.12).

The crypto paper finds the same structure in the cross-section of predictors. Most predictors are not significant in the linear mean equation, yet removing the insignificant ones degrades out-of-sample accuracy, and the state-dependent coefficients change sign across states (`2011.03741v2`, p.11). Only VIX and the US Treasury yield have predictive power on all three coins, and the predictors that move the transition probabilities differ by coin, so a single conditioning variable set does not transfer (`2011.03741v2`, p.11). The practical reading is that a regime variable is not a feature to add to a linear model; it is a variable that changes the coefficients of the other features, and the coefficients must be estimated per state.

## 6. Validation: what was tested, and what was not

The crude-oil paper is the only one in the slice that validates at the level a trading system needs. It reports annualized return, a Sharpe ratio with a Ledoit-Wolf test against zero using a fixed-size circular block bootstrap with 2,000 replications, the count of completed trades, trading-window returns with a t-test, the fraction of positive trading windows, and a White Reality Check against a zero-return benchmark (`2309.00875v3`, p.21). It then repeats the whole exercise with randomised initialisations 100 times and reports the dispersion of return and Sharpe, finding t-ratios of 4.8 and 5.0 for the prediction interval strategy (`2309.00875v3`, p.19, p.23). It also reports bootstrapped tail risk: the strategy's maximum drawdown is -5.73% against -39.47% for the crude-oil ETF over the same window (`2309.00875v3`, p.23).

The crypto paper validates the density forecast, not a strategy: it compares continuous ranked probability scores and mean squared forecast errors over thirty one-step-ahead predictions and finds that every hidden Markov model beats the random walk, kitchen-sink and AR(5) benchmarks (`2011.03741v2`, p.7-8). It also reports in-sample coverage, where the four-state model leaves 5% of Bitcoin returns outside its 1% credibility region against 75% for the random walk, which is a large gap but is an in-sample statistic (`2011.03741v2`, p.9). The operational-risk paper validates by exceedance backtesting of the 90th percentile, expecting about 10% exceptions and comparing mean squared exceedance error across model configurations (`2604.21734v1`, p.10, p.15). None of the other four papers reports an out-of-sample trading test at all.

## 7. Frequency and state count: two knobs that decide the answer

Two choices dominate the outcome and neither is settled by the data. The first is frequency. On the same internal-fraud loss series, the two-state model persists across consecutive quarters but alternates almost week to week at weekly frequency, because a homogeneous HMM absorbs high-frequency noise as state switching (`2604.21734v1`, p.14). Adding the VSTOXX covariate makes the weekly states more persistent, which the authors read as the covariate supplying the structure the noise was destroying (`2604.21734v1`, p.14-15). The crude-oil paper makes the opposite frequency choice for a stated reason: it runs the cointegration analysis weekly because daily data are noisier, but runs the filter and the trading on daily data because it wants short-term variation and daily trading (`2309.00875v3`, p.13, p.17). Frequency is a modelling decision that must match the horizon of the regime, not the horizon of the execution.

The second is the number of states. The crypto paper chooses four after testing two through five and finding that more than five states fit worse out of sample (`2011.03741v2`, p.2). The operational-risk paper finds that two- and three-state models identify the main regimes while four states overfit at quarterly and monthly frequency, with the four-state quarterly internal-fraud MSE of 2.59 not clearly better than the two-state 4.27 once the extra switching is counted (`2604.21734v1`, p.15). The monetary-history paper tried three regimes and could not find a stable specification, and states that the criteria for choosing the number of regimes are "quite subjective from a statistical point of view" (`0710.0745v1`, p.6). The only defensible selection rule in the slice is out-of-sample score, as the crypto and crude-oil papers use (`2011.03741v2`, p.8; `2309.00875v3`, p.17).

## Caveats

Three papers in this slice contain no trading data at all: the monetary-history study is a 19th-century commodity-spread exercise (`0710.0745v1`), the lead-lag paper is a methodological contribution validated on synthetic series plus one macroeconomic application (`physics/0607197v1`), and the Mexico paper is a single-market, single-period study of one integration series (`0905.3873v1`). The two papers that do trade are single-market and single-period: crude-oil futures over one test year (`2309.00875v3`) and US energy equities over one COVID-era backtest (`2407.19858v7`), so neither establishes that a regime model works generally, only that it worked once. The operational-risk paper is a bank-loss study, not a trading study, and its labels are regulatory event types, so its finding that a covariate helps only where a regime exists transfers as a validation principle rather than as a result. The slice disagrees with itself on the number of states, on the frequency at which regimes are visible, and on whether a macro covariate helps: `2604.21734v1` finds it helps for fraud but not for physical damage or system failures (p.16). No paper here establishes that a detected regime is predictable in advance, that a change-point can be called before it completes, or that regime conditioning survives realistic costs outside the one crude-oil configuration. The crypto and equity results are in-sample descriptions of state structure, and the energy paper's 82.76% return carries an information ratio of -0.1 against the S&P 500, which is the cleanest available statement that a good-looking return is not evidence of skill.

## Papers read in depth

- `2309.00875v3` - A hidden Markov model for statistical arbitrage in international crude oil futures markets (2023). Filter-based EM on a three-contract cointegration spread; the only out-of-sample trading test in the slice, but limited to one year and three futures contracts.
- `2011.03741v2` - Exploring the Predictability of Cryptocurrencies via Bayesian Hidden Markov Models (2020). Four-state non-homogeneous HMM beats single-regime benchmarks on density forecasts; states are volatility bands and the margin over a random walk is small.
- `1410.6005v1` - The non-linear trade-off between return and risk: a regime-switching multi-factor framework (2014). Shows the risk-return trade-off exists only in low-volatility states; US monthly data only, and no trading rule.
- `2604.21734v1` - Modeling dependency between operational risk losses and macroeconomic variables using Hidden Markov Models (2026). Clearest statement of the filtered-versus-smoothed distinction; bank-loss data, not market data, and Gaussian emissions that need outlier filtering.
- `0710.0745v1` - Mixing Kohonen Algorithm, Markov Switching Model and Detection of Multiple Change-Points (2007). Retrospective break dating with explicit acknowledgement that the method is not predictive; 19th-century monetary data, no modern market evidence.
- `0905.3873v1` - Structural Breaks in the Mexico's Integration into the World Stock Market (2009). Four break dates with confidence intervals from a Bai-Perron search; one market, one period, and no live detector.
- `physics/0607197v1` - Lead-lag cross-sectional structure and detection of correlated-anticorrelated regime shifts (2006). A causal sign-detection method that beats rolling correlation by about two years; validated mainly on synthetic data.
- `2407.19858v7` - AI-Powered Energy Algorithmic Trading: Integrating Hidden Markov Models with Neural Networks (2024). Five-state HMM plus a neural network on energy equities; one backtest, negative information ratio, and no out-of-sample re-estimation schedule.
