# Overfitting, reproducibility and research integrity: most published predictors replicate, and one bad data row can still erase a strategy

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers the nine-record slice of the arXiv q-fin.GN harvest that speaks to overfitting, replication and research integrity: records published from 2011 to 2025, only three of which carry a journal reference. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, whose real question is not whether overfitting exists but how a strategy search should be run, recorded and believed.

## What this covers

The slice is thin and its arc is unusual. Only three of the nine shortlisted records address study replication or research integrity directly; four use "replication" to mean hedging or portfolio replication, and the rest are robustness simulations of networks that share the vocabulary without sharing the subject. The arc runs from 2011 network-robustness work, through a 2014 numerical-method paper and a 2022 correction study, to a 2025 measurement of generative-model reproducibility. The single most consequential finding is that the largest and best-documented body of evidence here says the published literature is more trustworthy than the folklore: meta-studies of 207 cross-sectional return predictors find that almost all findings replicate and that publication-bias corrections account for only 10 to 15 percent of in-sample mean returns (`2209.13623v3`, p.1, p.4). The operator's dominant exposure is therefore the specification search, the metric choice and the input data -- not a significance threshold.

## The short answer

1. Cross-sectional return predictors mostly replicate, persist out-of-sample, and carry t-statistics far above 2.0, so the practical risk in a search is the analyst's own choices rather than a threshold (`2209.13623v3`).
2. Publication-bias corrections are small and agree across three independent teams: shrinkage of 10 to 15 percent of in-sample returns, with false discovery rates under 10 percent (`2209.13623v3`).
3. The multiple-testing hurdle is a priced policy: 1.96 implies an 8.8 percent false discovery rate, 2.3 gives 5 percent, and the recommended 3.0 leaves 81 percent of rejected findings true (`2209.13623v3`).
4. Mining 240 accounting variables produces 18,113 strategies, of which 30.17 percent clear t=2.0 and 8.40 percent clear t=4.0, thousands of times the null rate (`2209.13623v3`).
5. A single erroneous data row turned two published betting returns of 17.29 and 28.82 percent into losses of 7.36 and 6.31 percent (`2306.01740v4`).
6. The replication that found the error was exact: same data and algorithm reproduced the original coefficients and the identical sequence of bets (`2306.01740v4`).
7. After correction one strategy survived at 12.44 percent, then earned nothing on three further years of cleaned data, and its predictors lost significance (p-values 0.837 and 0.471) (`2306.01740v4`).
8. A generative model is not deterministic even at temperature zero: across 50 runs, earnings point estimates varied by 2.05 percent for the strongest model and 9.89 percent for the weakest (`2503.16974v4`).
9. Aggregating 3 to 5 runs removes most of that variance and leaves downstream inference valid in 97.35 percent of 10 million simulated regressions (`2503.16974v4`).
10. A robustness conclusion is conditional on the null model: none of five null models reproduced all the empirical higher-order statistics, and one measure sat outside every model's 95 percent interval in all nine years (`1104.4249v2`).
11. Which node metric ranks the removal determines the answer: eight of nine metrics agree on the ordering, and the clustering coefficient reverses it (`2302.13695v1`).
12. A regression surrogate is valid only inside the state space it was fitted on, because regressions can be arbitrarily bad outside their data (`1405.0508v2`).

## 1. What replication actually shows in asset pricing

The strongest evidence in this slice is a review of meta-studies of the cross-section of stock returns (`2209.13623v3`). The paper decomposes every reported effect into a true effect plus author error plus sampling error (p.2), and then asks which term publication selects for. Four stylized facts emerge, each documented by at least three independent meta-studies: almost all findings can be replicated, predictability persists out-of-sample, empirical t-statistics are far above 2.0, and predictors are weakly correlated (p.1, p.4). The replication dataset contains 207 cross-sectional predictors drawn from 140 papers, with an intended coverage of 319 characteristics from 153 papers (p.4). Replicated t-statistics line up along the 45 degree line with the hand-collected originals (p.5). McLean and Pontiff's replication of 97 published predictors found returns fall by only 26 percent out-of-sample, so 74 percent survives, and in the first three years after the original samples end the mean is still 74 basis points per month when the in-sample mean is normalized to 100 (p.6). Far from the original samples the decline is larger, about 50 percent (p.7), but monthly predictor returns have a mean autocorrelation of only 7 percent at one month, falling to zero at three months (p.21), so publication selection cannot explain a decline that takes a decade to appear. One apparent contradiction is reconciled by reading the definitions: a widely cited study reporting that "most anomalies fail to replicate" had only about 26 percent of its strategies shown to be clearly significant in the original papers, so it was testing a different population (p.5). The limit is that this is one literature, US equity cross-sectional predictors, one meta-dataset, and a review rather than a new experiment.

Two of the four facts are easy to misread, and the paper spends most of its length on the misreading. The 26 percent out-of-sample decline bounds the effect of sampling error, not the effect of data snooping, because publication selection should act within months; the mean autocorrelation of 7 percent at one month (p.21) rules out a decade-long decay as a selection artefact. The paper also separates phrases that are routinely conflated: "failed replication" is not "insignificant ad-hoc trading strategy", and "data snooping" is not "liquidity effects" (p.1). The operational consequence is that a decayed strategy is evidence about changing expected returns, not automatically evidence that the original finding was false.

## 2. Multiple testing and the price of a wide search

Same paper, harder numbers. Published t-statistics are not near the threshold: of the 207 predictors, 183 exceed 2.0, 74 exceed 4.0, and 26 exceed 6.0 (p.7, p.8). Under the null of no predictability it would take about 1/0.000063 = 16,000 draws to produce one t-statistic above 4.0 and about 50 billion draws to produce one above 6.0 (p.7). The counter-experiment is pure data mining: sorting stocks on simple functions of 240 accounting variables yields 18,113 strategies of which 30.17 percent clear t=2.0 and 8.40 percent clear t=4.0, against null rates of 4.55 percent and 0.0063 percent (p.8); a later study mines 2 million (p.18). The operational point is that the hurdle is a policy choice with a price. The classical 1.96 hurdle implies a false discovery rate of 8.8 percent; raising it to 2.3 gives 5 percent; the preferred 1 percent requires 3.0 (p.19, p.20). At that 3.0 hurdle, 81 percent of the rejected findings are true predictors with an average expected return 1.6 standard errors from zero (p.20), which is what "increased conservatism" looks like in practice. Estimated corrections are small and consistent across three independent teams: shrinkage of 8 to 17 percent in one study, 13 percent with a false discovery rate of 6.3 percent in another, and 10 to 15 percent overall (p.1, p.16, p.17). The implied variance of expected returns is about 3 in standard-error units, and with an average standard error near 20 basis points per month that means true effects of about 60 basis points per month are common (p.13). Predictors are also not redundant: almost all pairwise correlations are below 0.5 and it takes 60 principal components to span 90 percent of variance (p.9).

The paper is careful about what the large hurdles are actually buying. The preferred 1 percent false discovery rate is five times stricter than the conventional 5 percent, and reaching it requires a hurdle of 3.0 (p.20); family-wise methods such as Holm at 5 percent are stricter still and would imply that every significant predictor is true with 95 percent probability (p.20). The standard error on these hurdles is itself about 1.0, so the classical 1.96 need not be raised at all within estimation uncertainty (p.19). A search that reports one t-statistic and no trial count is reporting a quantity whose threshold is undefined.

## 3. One bad data row

The correction study (`2306.01740v4`) is the cleanest demonstration in the slice of where a published trading result actually breaks. It replicates a published betting strategy whose reported returns on investment reached 29.38 percent, against 3.56, 3.8 and 4.35 percent reported elsewhere in the tennis-betting literature (p.2). The replication is exact: the in-sample model coefficients are identical to the published ones (p.6) and the sequence of bets placed is identical (p.8). The failure is data quality. A single bet on Hercog, in the 2019 Miami Open, is listed at Bet365 odds of 5.50 while the best odds in a sample of 40 to 60 bookmakers are 2.10 and the market average is 1.85; Kelly sizing put 39.3 percent of the bankroll on it, and it won (p.9, p.10). Hercog's odds are the 14th longest of 2,453 rows that year, and all 13 longer ones are rank outsiders beating a favourite (p.10). Removing that one row turns a return on investment of 17.29 percent into -7.36 percent and 28.82 percent into -6.31 percent (p.10). One strategy survives correction: restricting to competitive matches with average implied probability in [0.4, 0.6] leaves 12.44 percent, down from 29.38, with a bootstrap probability of 0.002 that random betting would match it (p.11, p.12). Extending to a cleaned dataset of 35,274 player-matches, 17,751 matches and 584 players to August 2023 (p.13) produces no further profit after February 2020 (p.13), and the two predictors are no longer significant over February 2020 to August 2023, with p-values of 0.837 for the buzz factor and 0.471 for rank distance across 13,913 player-matches (p.14). The paper's own warning is that betting returns are not a valid measure of forecasting accuracy (p.15).

Two smaller discrepancies are worth recording because they show how narrow "replication" is as a word. The best-odds strategy reproduces to 3.05 percent in both papers, but the replication uses 5,189 odds against the original 5,188, traced to a single match where one side's best odds were missing (p.22). And in the competitive-match strategy, a floating-point boundary error at p = 0.8 caused the original code to overlook 30 potential bets, so the replication places more bets and lands at 18.13 percent against the published 20.11 percent (p.11). Neither changes a conclusion, but both would matter to anyone who claimed to have reproduced a strategy to the decimal.

## 4. Non-determinism in generative tooling

The 2025 reproducibility study (`2503.16974v4`) is the only record here that measures a modern tool's output distribution directly. It runs five finance and accounting tasks 50 times each over 50 days, from 27 December 2024 to 15 February 2025, at temperature zero, with a different seed per run, generating more than 3.4 million outputs from about 750,000 API calls and more than 80 million pairwise comparisons (p.3, p.13, p.15). Simple tasks are nearly deterministic: binary classification reaches Fleiss' Kappa above 0.93 with identical classifications across all 50 runs for more than 92 percent of sentences (p.4), sentiment analysis exceeds 0.94 (p.4), and perfect agreement is 96.18 percent for binary classification but only 55.66 percent for a five-class task (p.15). Multi-class classification sits at 0.86 to 0.91 (p.4). Generative tasks keep their meaning and lose their form: summary semantic similarity has a mean cosine of 0.98 while word counts differ by about 8.5 percent mean absolute relative difference (p.16), and text generation ranges from 8.18 to 14.91 percent length variation depending on the model (p.16). Numerical prediction is where non-determinism bites, with point-estimate differences of 2.05 percent for the strongest model and 6.18 and 9.89 percent for the weaker two (p.17). Two results matter operationally. Aggregating 3 to 5 runs lifts majority-class consensus from 86 to 94 percent and from 82 to 91 percent, and cuts text-length variation from 8.5 percent to about 3 percent (p.5). And in a simulation of 10 million regressions, downstream inference was correct 97.35 percent of the time, with Type I error at 1.28 percent and Type II at 1.38 percent (p.5). The authors define "G-hacking" as generating many outputs and reporting the favourable ones, and conclude the risk is relatively low for these tasks (p.6). The limit is that this covers three OpenAI models, zero-shot prompting and one 50-day window.

The comparison against humans is the most counter-intuitive result in the paper. Language models were more consistent than expert human annotators in more than 50 percent of cases, humans were more consistent in fewer than 9 percent, and the models agreed at 96.8 to 98.3 percent even on sentences where the human experts significantly disagreed (p.4). Consistency is not accuracy, and the paper is explicit that the two are separate; but it does mean that a low-agreement human label set is not a gold standard against which a deterministic pipeline should be judged.

## 5. Conclusions conditional on the null model

The 2011 network paper (`1104.4249v2`) makes the dependence of a robustness claim on the comparison set explicit. It builds the international financial network from the IMF Coordinated Portfolio Investment Survey for 2001 to 2009, up to 73 reporting countries against 237, thresholded at 500,000 USD, and restricts to a core of at least 64 countries holding at least 97.4 percent of external assets (p.3). It then compares the empirical network against five null models built from progressively more first-order structure (p.3, p.5). With 10,000 generated networks per model and 95 percent confidence intervals, none of the five reproduces all of the higher-order statistics, and one measure -- the probability that country i reaches k conditional on i reaching j and j reaching k -- sits above every null model's interval in all nine years and both specifications (p.9). The robustness result itself is the familiar robust-yet-fragile pattern: random removal leaves the average shortest path length essentially unchanged while targeted removal of the highest-degree nodes raises it immediately (p.6). The contagion simulations are explicitly preliminary. A single PIGS default at thresholds of 0.1 produces at most one further default, while Greece and Ireland together produce six rounds (p.10), and across 576 specifications the number of threshold pairs producing a worst-case default of more than 55 percent of the network doubles in 2006 and 2007 (p.12). The limits are severe: aggregated country-level data, no loss given default beyond a threshold rule, and the authors call the cascade modelling a first step.

Two thresholding rules are used to turn a weighted asset matrix into a binary network, one based on above-average exposure and one on a 4.17 percent GDP-normalized threshold (p.3), and the best-matching null model is not the same for both: the log-normal model matches the first closely while the rewiring model matches the second (p.6). That is a concrete instance of the general point that a robustness verdict is a statement about a network construction plus a null family, and changing either can change which model looks right.

## 6. Conclusions conditional on the metric

The pesticide-trade paper (`2302.13695v1`) is the slice's example of a conclusion that flips with the analyst's choice of metric. It builds directed trade networks for five pesticide categories from UN Comtrade over 2007 to 2018 (p.2), ranks economies by nine node metrics, and removes them in descending, random and ascending order (p.2, p.6). For eight of the nine metrics the ordering of damage is stable -- descending removal hurts most, ascending least (p.9) -- but the clustering coefficient reverses it, with random removal causing the largest efficiency loss (p.8, p.9). The direction of the shock matters too: metrics associated with importing economies (authority, in-closeness, in-degree) have a larger impact on network efficiency than the export-side metrics (hub, out-closeness, out-degree) (p.9). Network efficiency rose from 2007 to 2010, was stable to 2015, and fell after (p.4), with a sharp drop in 2015 that the authors attribute to an 8.5 percent fall in market sales (p.3, p.10). The paper closes with a data availability statement that the associated data will not be deposited (p.11). That is not a criticism of this paper specifically -- it is the ordinary state of the literature -- but it is the concrete form the reproducibility gap takes in this slice.

The ordering of the six networks by robustness is itself a result worth holding loosely: the aggregated network is the most robust and the insecticide network the second most robust, and the robustness curves for the other eight metrics trend upward while the clustering-coefficient curve trends down (p.10). The single most fragile year is 2015, and the authors attribute the drop to the 8.5 percent fall in pesticide sales rather than to a structural change in the network (p.3, p.10).

## 7. Surrogates are valid only where they were fitted

The 2014 MVA paper (`1405.0508v2`) is included because it states most clearly a failure mode that also applies to any regression or machine-learning surrogate used inside a search. The paper computes a margin valuation adjustment by replacing full portfolio revaluation with a Longstaff-Schwartz regression used as a compression device (p.1, p.7). The authors document two reasons the regression cannot be fitted naively: at time zero the portfolio has exactly one value so regression is impossible, and for later times the region of state space explored by the simulation is much smaller than the region explored once value-at-risk shocks are applied (p.8). Their own sentence is the general lesson -- it is simple to construct regressions that are arbitrarily bad outside the range of their data -- so the design goal is to expand the calibration state space so the regression interpolates rather than extrapolates (p.9). The numbers: 1,024 paths and relative shocks up to 30 percent (p.8, p.9); 99 percent one-sided value-at-risk over 10-day moves with a 5-year window giving 1,294 shocks defined at 18 maturities (p.11); errors on outcome metrics of around 20 basis points of notional or less (p.11); and a speedup reaching 100x for a 10,000-swap portfolio (p.12). The result it is used for is that the margin adjustment is close to 50 percent of the funding adjustment, for example 53 versus 115 basis points for a 90 percent payer portfolio (p.13). The limit is that this is a single asset class (USD interest rate swaps) and a single numerical experiment, so it is evidence about the failure mode, not about adjustment magnitudes elsewhere.

The parsimony argument is what makes the method usable: the regression uses 2m+1 basis functions, m swaps and m annuities, chosen once and reused at every stopping date, with 41 basis functions in the reported runs (p.12). The augmentation is complete at time zero by construction, because there are more simulation paths than value-at-risk shocks, so the calibration range covers every shock the value-at-risk computation will apply (p.9). The residual error of about 20 basis points of notional is then a statement about interpolation inside that range, and says nothing about behaviour outside it.

## 8. What a research protocol must record

The slice does not contain a research protocol, but it does show which omission each failure traces back to, and those omissions define the minimum a protocol has to close. The correction study (`2306.01740v4`) failed on a data-quality rule that was not applied before the model saw the row (p.10). The multiple-testing problem (`2209.13623v3`) traces to a trial count that was never reported alongside the selected t-statistic (p.8). The generative-model problem (`2503.16974v4`) traces to a run configuration and a seed policy that were not recorded (p.13). The surrogate problem (`1405.0508v2`) traces to a calibration region that was not stated with the result (p.9).

A protocol that closes these must therefore record, at minimum: the identity and digest of the dataset, including membership and the point-in-time aperture; the full candidate list and the number of trials, not only the winner; the significance policy and the false-discovery rate it implies; the in-sample, out-of-sample and held-out periods, named by date; the data-cleaning rules and their measured effect on the result; the model, temperature and seed policy for any generative step; the aggregation rule across repeated runs; the comparison set or null model; and the metric used to rank candidates.

The last item is the one most likely to be skipped, and section 6 shows why it cannot be. In `2302.13695v1` the same network is robust or fragile depending on which of nine metrics ranked the removals, and one of the nine reverses the ordering outright (p.9). A protocol that records the metric makes that reversal visible; one that does not turns an analyst's arbitrary choice into a reported fact.

## Caveats

Two of the six papers read here carry no data at all: `2209.13623v3` is a review of meta-studies and `1405.0508v2` is a derivation with one numerical experiment. `2302.13695v1` explicitly declines to deposit its data, so its numbers cannot be re-derived from the paper. Every empirical paper in the slice is single-domain and mostly single-period: US equity cross-sectional predictors, WTA tennis betting from 2015 to 2023, five OpenAI models over 50 days, one IMF survey for 2001 to 2009, and one commodity network for 2007 to 2018. The slice disagrees with itself in a useful way. `2209.13623v3` and `2503.16974v4` both conclude that the feared failure is smaller than the folklore says -- publication bias corrects only 10 to 15 percent, and generative non-determinism leaves downstream inference valid 97.35 percent of the time -- while `2306.01740v4` shows that a single dirty row destroyed most of a published result. The reconciliation is that the first two measure aggregate bias across many findings while the third is a single high-leverage observation. No paper here establishes a research protocol, measures overfitting inside a live strategy search, or tests a trading strategy's replication; the two direct integrity papers study asset-pricing anomalies and language-model annotation, and the trading evidence is a sports-betting correction study. Nothing here supports a profitability claim, and the robustness papers are about networks of countries and commodities, not order flow.

## Papers read in depth

- `2209.13623v3` - Publication Bias in Asset Pricing Research (2022). Reviews the meta-study evidence on publication bias, multiple testing and replication in the cross-section of returns; the quantitative backbone of this brief, limited to US equity predictors and a review format.
- `2503.16974v4` - Assessing Consistency and Reproducibility in the Outputs of Large Language Models (2025). Measures output consistency across 50 runs and five finance tasks; the only direct measurement of tool non-determinism here, limited to three OpenAI models and a 50-day window.
- `2306.01740v4` - Not feeling the buzz: Correction study of mispricing and inefficiency in online sportsbooks (2023). An exact replication that exposes a single erroneous odds row and then loses significance out-of-sample; one betting market and one correction.
- `1104.4249v2` - Robustness and Contagion in the International Financial Network (2011). Compares an empirical financial network against five null models and finds none accounts for all higher-order statistics; preliminary, aggregated country data.
- `2302.13695v1` - Impact of shocks to economies on the efficiency and robustness of the international pesticide trade networks (2023). Shows the robustness answer depends on which of nine node metrics ranks the removal; no data deposited, single commodity domain.
- `1405.0508v2` - MVA: Initial Margin Valuation Adjustment by Replication and Regression (2014). States the interpolate-don't-extrapolate rule for regression surrogates with a numerical validation; one asset class and one experiment.
