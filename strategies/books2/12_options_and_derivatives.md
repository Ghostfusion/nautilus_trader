# Options and derivative instruments: implied volatility is a variance quantile and parity enforcement still costs

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers the general-finance half of the options and derivatives slice of a local arXiv `q-fin.GN` harvest: what option prices imply about information, how variance and volatility instruments are calibrated and quoted, how structured and off-index products are priced, where funding, collateral and margin change the value of a contract, and what the data say about who uses derivatives and why. It reads twelve papers from the 96-record slice spanning 2008-2026, of which 17 carry a journal reference. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, and it deliberately stays on the pricing, calibration and cost side rather than repeating the trading-corpus brief on hedging as a metaorder and joint quoting.

## What this covers

The slice's arc runs from the price-to-factor map itself, through the fitting of a whole volatility surface, into the instruments that trade variance and the structured products that wrap it, and finally to the collateral and funding plumbing that decides what a derivative is worth to a particular balance sheet. The single most consequential finding is that a clean price-space identity can hide a systematic carry cost: put-call parity residuals against traded futures are compressed to near zero in both SPX and RUT options, yet option-implied discount factors sit about 37 bp above the OIS benchmark, 98.4% of observations positive, and the wedge is explained by a volatility-times-sqrt(maturity) path-risk term plus bid-ask and financial-condition variables (`2604.19604v6`). The rest of the slice supplies the pricing machinery, the empirical premiums and the collateral rules that surround that fact.

## The short answer

1. Black-Scholes implied volatility is an exact quantile of a strike-specific generalized inverse Gaussian law, not an algorithmic root-solve, and variance is the natural coordinate of the inversion (`2604.24480v4`).
2. On a 100,000-case random grid the quantile formula recovers implied volatility to machine precision (median 1.39e-16) about three times faster than the Let's Be Rational reference (59.49 ns vs 180.03 ns per evaluation) (`2604.24480v4`).
3. After a single return jump the S&P 500 option market keeps repricing: a negative jump adds 0.218% to at-the-money implied volatility over the next 60 minutes (`1810.12200v1`).
4. That post-jump drift is asymmetric - negative-jump movements persist or grow, positive-jump movements vanish within an hour, and out-of-the-money calls adjust immediately (`1810.12200v1`).
5. Fitting the Black-Scholes implied-volatility surface does not pin the variance term structure; calibrating with an explicit penalty on variance-swap or VIX deviations is needed (`2509.08096v1`).
6. With jumps the variance-swap multiplier departs from two and is driven by the third moment, so variance-swap minus squared VIX is about minus lambda times E[J^3]/3 (`2509.08096v1`).
7. Out-of-the-money calls and straddles carry negative risk premiums that require unspanned "dark matter" - local time plus jumps crossing the strike - to explain (`2303.16371v1`).
8. An option on an illiquid asset should be priced by utility indifference with a dynamic proxy hedge plus a static proxy-option hedge, and its value is convex in the hedge size (`1205.3507v1`).
9. Carbon allowance options need at least two variance factors plus jumps; a two-factor stochastic volatility model with jumps fits implied-vol RMSE of about 11 to 20 vol points (`2501.17490v2`).
10. Superhedging produces a piecewise-linear CALL function, so reported smoothness is a modelling choice, and roughly 30% of quoted strikes are priced inefficiently (`1502.03978v1`).
11. Put-call parity residuals can be near zero in price space while a systematic 37 bp positive carry gap remains in carry space (`2604.19604v6`).
12. An uncollateralized derivative's value is agent-specific: DVA is a funding benefit and the residual FVA is governed by the hedger's bond-CDS basis (`1403.1086v2`).

## 1. Implied volatility is a variance quantile

The Black-Scholes formula is explicit from volatility to price but market practice needs the inverse map, and for fifty years that inverse was handled by approximation, bounds or iteration. The paper shows the inverse is a distributional transform. With normalized call price c = C/D/F, log-moneyness k = log(K/F) and total implied variance w = sigma^2 T, the normalized call price is a probability on the variance scale and implied variance is a quantile of a generalized inverse Gaussian law, w = F_GIG^{-1}(c; 1/2, 1/4, k^2) for the out-of-the-money branch k > 0 (Eq. 11, p.5) (`2604.24480v4`). Equivalently the call price equals P(Z_k <= w) where Z_k is the variance budget a drifted Brownian motion needs to reach level one (Eq. 15, p.7), giving implied volatility a first-passage-time reading. The representation reorganises the whole surface: the variance-vega is the GIG density itself (Eq. 16, p.8); calendar no-arbitrage becomes a probability band and a monotonicity condition on total implied variance (Eq. 21, p.9); butterfly no-arbitrage becomes a sign condition on the same Greek hierarchy combined with the slope and curvature of the variance smile (Eqs. 22-23, p.9). The map is not D-finite, with w(c) ~ k^2/(2 log(1/c)) as c -> 0 (Proposition 1 and Eq. 41, pp.20-21), which explains why closed forms were elusive. The evidence limit is that the numerical claim is a single benchmark: one 100,000-case random grid, total volatility in [0.01, 2.00], ten timing runs of one million evaluations each, median absolute recovery error 1.39e-16 against 1.67e-16 for Let's Be Rational, at 59.49 ns against 180.03 ns (Table 1, p.12). A tail grid of 246 cases is essentially tied, 2.22e-16 against 2.20e-16 median absolute and 2.43e-14 against 2.32e-14 maximum relative (p.12).

## 2. The smile updates asymmetrically after return jumps

Whether an index option market is informationally efficient can be tested by treating a return jump as a news event and watching whether implied volatility moves at once or drifts. Using minute-by-minute S&P 500 index options from the start of 2006 to the end of 2010, with jumps detected by the Lee-Mykland non-parametric test at 1% on one-minute windows, and 83% of jumps occurring before 10:30 (p.12), the authors reject the one-shot adjustment (`1810.12200v1`). The total reaction is in the expected direction - positive jumps push implied volatility down, negative jumps push it up - but there is a documented post-drift: at-the-money implied volatility increases by an additional 0.218% after a negative jump over the next 60 minutes, worth about 1% or more in call dollar terms (p.3). The post-movement is directed, gradual and persistent for negative jumps and largely vanishes within an hour for positive jumps (p.15). Crucially the smile does not move as one block: implied volatility from at-the-money options and out-of-the-money puts drifts after the jump window, while out-of-the-money calls (equivalently in-the-money puts) converge immediately, so the smile is adjusted asymmetrically (p.2). The principal components confirm the split, with the first three components explaining 91.16%, 88.51% and 88.86% of smile variation at three, six and nine months, and the first component loading on out-of-the-money calls (Table 2, p.10). Regression coefficient signs are stable in the expected direction - at five minutes the three-month at-the-money estimates are -0.440 for positive jumps and +0.471 for negative jumps (Table 3, p.14), and after excluding the jump-window movement they remain -0.096 and +0.136 (Table 4, p.16). The economic limit is stated by the authors: statistical arbitrage at mid-prices would be possible, but under actual bid-ask spreads in the option market the results do not imply abnormal option returns (p.3).

## 3. Fitting the surface is not fitting the variance term structure

Practitioners calibrate option models by minimising squared errors in Black-Scholes implied volatility, and the paper shows this single objective can be badly incomplete. A model whose implied-volatility surface matches the market can still imply a variance term structure far from the one the option prices contain (p.1), which matters for anyone pricing variance swaps, VIX derivatives or forward variance. The fix is to augment the standard objective with a penalty on the deviation between model-implied and market-implied variance, using a hyperparameter alpha in [0,1] to trade off the volatility surface against the variance term structure (Eq. 2, p.4) (`2509.08096v1`). The theoretical content is the jump caveat: when the underlying can jump, the variance swap is priced as a model-dependent multiplier Q times the log contract, and Q departs from two whenever the jump distribution is skewed, with variance-swap minus squared VIX about minus lambda times E[J^3]/3 (Eqs. 19-21, pp.13-14). The empirical work is on a Bates model fit to a panel of S&P 500 index options covering 1996-2023 (p.1), and a simple jump-diffusion exposition uses sigma = 0.16, lambda = 1, J = -0.05, T = 1/12, where the objective is nearly flat near the true parameters and the VIX locus reduces a two-parameter search to a univariate line search (pp.18-19). The limit is that the paper is a calibration methodology with an illustrative simulation and one index panel, not a live trading test.

## 4. Equity option risk premiums contain unspanned dark matter

Using Tanaka's formula for semimartingales, the call risk premium can be decomposed into a "dark matter" risk premium - local time plus jumps crossing the strike - and an upside equity risk premium (Eq. 16, p.10). The theorem is sharp: the call risk premium at moneyness k > 1 can be negative only if the dark matter risk premium is negative, and the straddle risk premium is negative only if the dark matter premium at the money is negative (Theorem 1, p.10) (`2303.16371v1`). The empirics use index options over expiration cycles: weeklys averaging eight days, plus 28-day and 88-day contracts, with 415 weekly cycles from 13 January 2011 to 20 December 2018 (p.13). The results are consistent and large: weekly straddle excess returns are negative in 14 of 15 partitioned estimates, with an unconditional -10%, and the 3% out-of-the-money call excess return is -59% unconditionally against -61% for the same-delta 3% out-of-the-money put (pp.15-16). Crash-neutral straddle returns are close to zero, which the authors read as the jumps- crossing-the-strike premium on the short put roughly balancing the long straddle position (p.16). For farther-dated options the evidence points to a negative local time premium, with 5% OTM 28-day calls negative in 21 of 30 entries and 88-day 12% (6 delta) OTM calls negative and significant in 10 of 15 (p.17), and unconditional call premiums becoming more negative deeper out of the money (p.17). The limit is that this is short-dated S&P 500 index option data over one decade, and the object is a risk premium, not a pricing formula.

## 5. Structured and off-index products need their own models

Two papers show how far the machinery must travel to price non-index exposures. Carbon allowance options on ICE futures are priced with a two-factor affine stochastic-volatility model with jumps, estimated by indirect inference on realised volatility decomposed into continuous and jump parts, with a three-dimensional pricing kernel compensating equity and two variance risks. The data are 155,238 five-minute observations across 1,283 trading days and 520 options (263 puts, 257 calls) from December 2015 to December 2020 (pp.9-13) (`2501.17490v2`). The market is volatile: mean realised variance is 9.6% (mean annualized volatility 49.5%, p.11), five-minute log-returns have standard deviation 0.28%, skew -0.95 and kurtosis 64.87 (Table 1, p.11), and the option sample averages 60% implied volatility with mean moneyness 1.24 for calls and 0.76 for puts (pp.13,21). The authors find a single variance factor is not enough to reproduce the HAR features and that two factors are needed, a fast mean-reverting high-vol-of-vol component plus a slow one with a half-life near 20 days (p.17); calibrated risk premia are phi = -7.35e-3, psi1 = 2.81e-3 and psi2 = 1.50e-2 (Table 7, p.22). Pricing accuracy is modest and comparable to a discrete-time benchmark: implied-vol RMSE of 19.80, 11.96 and 11.33 across the three moneyness bands for the two-factor model against 19.25, 11.17 and 10.12 for the benchmark (Table 8, p.24). The second paper treats the illiquid case directly: to price a European option on an illiquid asset Z you dynamic-hedge in a correlated liquid proxy S and statically hedge in a liquid option on another proxy Y, using exponential-utility indifference pricing and an asymptotic expansion around perfect correlation between Y and Z (`1205.3507v1`). The zero-order problem reduces to a heat equation (p.13) and the first correction follows by Duhamel's principle (p.15); the resulting option price is convex in the number of static hedge units (p.22), and the closed-form (in quadratures) solution is fast - about 0.68 s against 17 s for a finite-difference solve at three years and moderate risk aversion (pp.16-17). Both papers are single-market or simulation-based.

## 6. Clean prices can still hide a cost

Option quoting and arbitrage screening both depend on knowing what the market's prices actually encode. One paper rebuilds the CALL function from the superhedging principle rather than from absence of arbitrage, so it tolerates bid-ask spreads, shorting restrictions and margin constraints, and shows that the projection yields a piecewise-linear superhedging price which coincides with the first stage of the Dykstra-style shape-restricted estimator (`1502.03978v1`, p.2). Smoothness therefore has to be added deliberately: in a Black-Scholes-with-smile simulation (S = 1.365, 25 strikes from 1000 to 1700, volatility falling linearly from 40% to 20%, prices from 378 down to 0.9, p.13) a noisy sample has an average of 34.74% inefficient prices, up to 56% (Table 1, p.14), and the error is asymmetric because positive shocks make a quote inefficient and invisible while negative shocks are absorbed, producing a mild underpricing of deep in-the-money options (p.14). On real S&P 500 quotes from 21 October 2010, roughly 30% of quoted strikes were inefficient at the three sampled times (pp.16-17). The second paper turns the same kind of discipline on the parity identity: extracting option-implied discount factors from minute-level NBBO data on SPX and RUT options from January 2016 to October 2025, it finds quoted put-call parity residuals against traded futures are tightly compressed around zero (median cell-level R^2 of 0.9999999 for SPX and 0.9999995 for RUT, p.9), yet the implied-discount-factor carry gap is positive with a full-sample median of 37.50 bp, 98.4% of observations positive, and market means of 34.87 bp (SPX) and 40.12 bp (RUT) (`2604.19604v6`, p.9). The authors model it as a pre-maturity path-risk problem, since enforcing parity by +C - P - F requires daily settlement and variation margin; the expected support capital of a Brownian P&L scales with volatility times sqrt(2t/pi) (p.12). A reduced-form regression on the GBM path-risk term plus a bid-ask term and the NFCI has pooled R^2 of 0.309 and RMSE 13.57 bp, with all four coefficient signs stable in ten of ten leave-one-year-out folds (Tables 6.1 and 7.3, pp.14,19), and Engle-Granger residual-stationarity statistics of -12.009 (SPX) and -14.101 (RUT) reject the spurious-regression reading at 1% (Table 8.1, p.21). The limit is that LOYO out-of-sample mean R^2 is small, 0.049 to 0.075, with 2020 as the dominant failure regime (p.18).

## 7. Funding, collateral and margin are part of the price

An uncollateralized derivative is not worth the same to both counterparties. Starting from the double-counting debate between DVA and FVA, the paper argues that the hedger's unhedgeable jump-to- default risk nevertheless has a well-defined value, equal to a fictitious CDS written on the hedger and implicitly sold by the investor, which the hedger is willing to pay its own short-term CDS spread for because it is a funding benefit (`1403.1086v2`, pp.1,6). Carrying that CDS in the self-financing replicating portfolio (subject to the funding constraint V_t = phi_t B(t; t+dt) + psi_t B(t; T), Eq. 1, p.3) yields a price equal to the collateralized value plus a survival- contingent CVA, a contingent DVA, and an FVA proportional to the hedger's bond-CDS basis (Eq. 11, p.10). The FVA does not vanish; it is governed by the bond-CDS basis, so funding does not enter purely through credit risk (p.10). Two sophisticated counterparties agree on a price only if their bond-CDS bases are related, and a deal closes when the creditor's basis is no larger than the debtor's (pp.11-12). A neat steel-man case is a hedger with zero bond-CDS basis but a 200 bp funding spread lending to a risk-free counterparty: the funding spread does not appear in the valuation, because the recovery feedback cancels it in steady state (p.12). The limit is that the framework is a replication argument under idealized, single-factor credit-spread dynamics and deterministic rates, aimed at accounting fair value rather than at live quoting.

## 8. Bounded risk produces corner solutions and threshold collapse

A different route into margin and cost is an optimization model of whether a derivative desk or supply chain adopts smart-contract settlement under bounded, lognormal risk. The model is a convex newsvendor-style profit maximization over an adoption intensity and order quantities (p.9) (`2510.07006v1`). Its central analytical finding is that adoption is pinned at a boundary: the baseline calibration gives a unique corner solution alpha* = 0.05 that is invariant to demand variance and supplier-readiness heterogeneity, because the marginal benefit of adoption is dominated by procurement economics (p.22). Behaviour is discontinuous - a sharp threshold in the fixed-cost parameter A3 triggers a collapse of adoption (p.20) - and profitability is far more fragile than the adoption decision: bootstrap mean profit falls monotonically from 1010.0 to 900.6, 839.3 and 705.4 as demand standard deviation rises through 5, 8, 10 and 15, with a regression slope of -30.12 and Spearman correlation -1 (Table 4, p.24). Relaxing the corner lock with a lower adoption cost, weaker convexity and stronger complementarity (A3 = 500, nu = 1.5, A1 = 15) produces an interior alpha* = 0.875, profit 1089.2 and fill rate 0.896 (Table 5, p.26), and the sample-average approximation is shown to converge at the canonical O(1/sqrt(N)) rate (p.1). The limits are serious: the evidence is a synthetic model with calibrated parameters, a bounded lognormal demand assumption and an empirical check only on S&P 500 returns and a MovieLens dataset.

## 9. Derivative usage evidence: credit, retail and the shape of the market

The slice also carries descriptive and empirical work on who trades derivatives and what the trading does to related markets. A long-period study of French credit default swaps covers thirteen companies from twelve sectors over 2001 to February 2008 with about 60,000 observations (`0911.4039v1`, p.10). The market context it documents is concentrated and increasingly risky: the credit-derivatives notional grew from about $3 trillion in 2003 to $12 trillion in 2005 with CDS roughly half, fifteen institutions accounted for 86% of 2005 volumes, and between 2003 and 2005 the share of reference entities rated at least A fell from 60% to 33% while speculative-grade entities grew from 20% to 37% (pp.2,8-9). Average correlations are negative between share returns and both spreads (-0.0765 with bond spreads, -0.1124 with CDS spreads) and weakly positive between the two credit spreads (0.0641, Table 3, p.15). In the difference VAR models the credit-spread equations are far better explained than the share-return equation - R^2 of 0.1915 and 0.1044 for bond and CDS spread changes against 0.0265 for returns over 2001-2007, rising to 0.2190, 0.1399 and 0.0428 in the tense 2001-2003 sub-period (Tables 6-7, pp.18-19) - and the authors read the tighter linkage in stress as a contagion channel. A descriptive study of the Indian market shows how fast an exchange-traded derivatives segment can grow and how unevenly: turnover rose from Rs.4,038 crore in 2000-01 to Rs.3,21,58,208 crore in 2012-13, NSE turnover was 97% higher than BSE and its derivative turnover 91% higher than its own equity turnover, Nifty options were the world's second most traded option in 2012 and NSE ranked third among the top thirty derivative exchanges (`2207.13123v1`, pp.1-2,7). It also notes the 2011 switch of Indian stock options from American to European style, which removed early-assignment risk and widened strike liquidity (p.9). Both usage papers are low on causal identification - one has no instruments, the other is descriptive with partial hypotheses - so they are context, not evidence about mechanism.

## Caveats

The evidence does not establish several things. Three of the pricing papers are theory or methodology with illustrative simulations and no market calibration: the quantile inversion is benchmarked on a simulated grid only, the illiquid-option indifference method is validated against finite differences rather than traded prices, and the superhedging estimator is tested on a simulated smile plus one day of SPX quotes. Empirical coverage is narrow throughout: the jump-reaction study is S&P 500 index options over 2006-2010, the dark-matter study is SPX weeklys and farther-dated contracts over 2011-2018, the carbon study is one ICE product over one phase, the carry-gap study is SPX and RUT over 2016-2025 with small out-of-sample R^2 and a 2020 failure, and the CDS study is thirteen French firms over 2001-2008 before the modern credit market. The slice disagrees with itself on at least one point: the jump paper finds persistent post-jump implied-vol drift while the efficiency literature it engages reports immediate co-jumps, and the joint- calibration paper argues the standard implied-vol objective is incomplete whereas the carbon paper is content to compare implied-vol RMSEs across models. No paper here establishes a profitable trading rule; the carry-gap and dark-matter results are premia and cost decompositions, and every net-of-cost claim in this slice is either negative or explicitly not tested.
