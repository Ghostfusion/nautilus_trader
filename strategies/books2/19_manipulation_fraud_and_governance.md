# Manipulation, fraud and governance: short-horizon settlement manipulation, and the false-positive cost of every detector

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads ten papers from the arXiv `q-fin.GN` (General Finance) harvest - 3095 records spanning 1997-2026 - that bear on market manipulation, fraud and governance. The slice is the 47 records (2007-2026, 8 with a journal reference) whose titles or abstracts concern spoofing, layering, wash trading, pump-and-dump, settlement and closing-price manipulation, insider flow, or the governance of the venues where those occur. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets. The recurring question is what such a platform can detect from the data it already stores, what it must not do, and how each detector fails when it does.

## What this covers

The arc runs from order-book conduct (spoofing and layering), through identity-based wash trading, to the newest and sharpest case: settlement manipulation in ultra-short prediction markets, where a trader who holds a binary contract trades the underlying spot in the final seconds to move the price the contract pays on. The single most consequential finding is `2606.31675v1`: after Polymarket's five-minute Bitcoin contract launched, a push against the favoured side overturned outcomes the market had priced at 90-100% one time in three, against 1% in cycles with no push (p.7). The vulnerability is a settlement-design property, and it gets worse as the contract horizon shortens, not better.

## The short answer

1. Settlement manipulation is real, large and retail-funded: 821 of roughly 243,000 traders took $8.2M in pushed cycles, while 93% of the loss fell on retail accounts (`2606.31675v1`, p.7).
2. The design remedy is horizon and reference, not surveillance: a fifteen-minute contract attenuates the footprint, and an aggregating settlement reference raises push cost roughly in proportion to the averaging window (`2606.31675v1`, p.35).
3. Size-based anomaly screens flag the wrong orders: a Z-score detector on the LUNA tape returned orders priced $0.01 and $0.11 far from the spread, not the spoofing orders, and missed inserted synthetic spoofing entirely (`2308.08683v1`, p.16, p.19).
4. Spoofing detectors lose most of their precision when the label set includes neutral book states: F1 falls from 80.40 to 46.08 as the classes go from two to three (`2403.13429v1`, p.2).
5. Wash-trade detection by repurchase within 30 days is an identity heuristic, not proof: intermediaries are flagged though they may be innocent buyers, and a fresh wallet evades the rule (`2305.01543v1`, p.8-9).
6. Crude wash-trade treatment destroys legitimate volume: deflating the top two volume quantiles cut the daily amount to about 40% of raw and lowered a portfolio's Sharpe from 1.41 to 0.96 (`2404.07222v3`, p.2, p.20).
7. Closing-price manipulation has a measurable signature: the Apple close at exactly $500.00 had a chance probability below 1 in 1,000, and the hedging explanation required hedgers to sell while the price rose (`1402.0910v1`, p.3, p.5).
8. A detector's data layer bounds its claim: on-chain fills cannot support address-level spoofing findings without attributed quote-lifecycle records (`2605.10486v2`, p.6).
9. Insider-flow scores are anchor-sensitive: the same Iran contract scored +0.113 at the article-derived event date and -0.331 at a resolution-anchored proxy (`2605.02286v2`, p.6).
10. Position limits must aggregate linked accounts and correlated instruments; per-wallet limits need not bind when identities fragment (`2605.10486v2`, p.9).
11. Manipulation can be as simple as trading the open/close impact asymmetry: the same shares bought at open and sold at close create mark-to-market gains with no net position change (`1912.01708v1`, p.1).
12. Abnormal-account graphs carry signal: in the leaked Mt. Gox data the abnormal-account base networks correlated 0.871 with price against 0.239 for normal accounts (`1902.01941v1`, p.6).

## 1. Spoofing and layering: the mechanics and the order-book footprint

Spoofing is the placement of non-bona-fide orders on one side of the book to mislead other participants about supply or demand; the ESMA definition quoted by `2403.13429v1` adds that the manipulative orders are removed once a trade executes on the other side (p.1). Two variants matter: traditional spoofing, one genuine order plus one large spoofing order, and layered spoofing, one genuine order plus multiple large orders at different prices (`2308.08683v1`, p.12). The economic precondition is thin liquidity, because a liquid market requires spoof orders large enough to be expensive (`2308.08683v1`, p.12).

`2308.08683v1` builds a detection statistic by treating orders as particles: order submissions are incoming particles, cancellations are exiting particles, and each order's momentum is its size times its velocity toward the quoted price (p.4-6). The measure is computed separately for an active area around the spread and a passive area beyond it, because around 97% of submissions and cancellations on the LUNA/USD tape fall inside the active area and would swamp a raw count (p.7). Spoofing orders hide in the passive area, where momentum is small and a jump stands out (p.10). On the LUNA/USD tape during the May 2022 crash, the largest ten momentum deviations in the 19:00-20:00 window were [126.12, 120.76, 17.72, 7.09, 7.02, 7.02, 6.80, 6.80, 6.80, 6.61]; the top two trace to a single 535,665-unit limit buy at 0.73 placed at 19:28:34.77 and cancelled 33 seconds later (p.14). In the 18:00-19:00 window the same method returned 124 orders of an identical 29,000-unit size, most of them limit buys cancelled soon after, which the authors read as one layered campaign (p.15). The base rate that makes such campaigns cheap to run is that roughly 98.6% of open orders on the venue are eventually cancelled (p.6).

`2403.13429v1` approaches the same problem as sequence labelling. It stacks ten order-book states, each a 30-level by 2-side by 2-field matrix, and trains a causal temporal convolutional network with 128 filters and dilations 1 to 64, so that the label at time t depends only on states up to t (p.2). The training set is generated by a labelling algorithm rather than by adjudicated cases, making this weak supervision rather than supervised learning (p.2). The reported accuracy is 91% (p.2), but the per-class numbers matter more and are discussed in Section 2.

## 2. Detectors and their false positives

The most useful evidence in this slice is not that detectors work; it is how they fail. `2308.08683v1` runs the conventional Z-score screen on the same LUNA data that its momentum method flagged. The Z-score's top ten values were [378.01, 9.23, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68, 1.68], and the two flagged records were limit buys priced at $0.01 and $0.11, far outside the passive area and too distant from the spread to influence the market, with no matching cancellation (p.16). On Bitcoin the same screen's top values were [287.55, 287.55, 274.83, 274.83, ...], corresponding to a sell order more than $200 from a midprice of $41,466.87 (p.17-18). The decisive test is synthetic: when the authors insert known spoofing orders into the BTC tape, the momentum measure jumps from a baseline top value of 26.44 to 60.75 and localises them, while the Z-score distribution barely moves, from 287.55 to 286.12 (p.17, p.19). A size-only screen therefore produces false positives on large legitimate orders and false negatives on real spoofing, because the spoofing orders are not the largest ones on the tape.

`2403.13429v1` shows the same trade-off in classifier terms. With two classes (buy-side and sell-side spoofing) at a 256-dimensional representation the pair of (accuracy, F1) is (90.97, 80.40); with a third neutral class it becomes (86.02, 46.08), and at 1024 dimensions (90.18, 56.90) versus (84.36, 45.85) (p.2). Accuracy moves by a few points; F1 nearly halves. Any operational screen that reports accuracy will look far better than it behaves.

`2404.07222v3` supplies the cost side. A common wash-trade treatment reduces the third volume quantile by 50% and the fourth by 75%, which leaves the daily traded amount at about 40% of raw, implying that about 60% of recorded volume is treated as wash (p.2). That is stricter than the 46.47% benchmark the paper cites for Binance (p.2), and far below the more-than-70% and more-than-90% figures reported for unregulated and smaller venues elsewhere in the same literature review (p.7). The treatment removes legitimate high-volume trades along with manipulative ones: a liquidity-adjusted mean-variance portfolio with the treatment has a Sharpe ratio of 0.96, below both the untreated liquidity-adjusted portfolio at 1.41 and even the traditional portfolio at 1.35 (p.20). The paper's conclusion is that treatment is unnecessary for established high-capitalisation assets on mainstream exchanges (p.22).

Finally, `1912.01708v1` documents a social failure mode of detection: it argues that a persistent overnight/intraday return split across sixteen major indices is the footprint of a round-trip manipulation, and that the academic and journalistic default has been to accept innocuous explanations without testing them (p.5). That is a false-negative complaint, not a detector, and it is treated as such in the caveats.

## 3. Wash trading: the repurchase rule and its blind spots

`2305.01543v1` defines a wash sale as a seller repurchasing the same asset within 30 days, borrowing the tax rule that applies to substantially identical securities, and applies it to non-fungible tokens where each token is treated as identical only to itself (p.1). The detector builds two directed graphs per token, one over all transfers and one over sales, and flags a wash sale when a wallet that sold a token is subsequently the recipient of the same token within 30 days; a breadth-first search then labels the intermediate transactions in the cycle (p.2-3). Across seven collections the average incidence is 0.14% of transactions, 0.11% of wallets and 0.16% of tokens, with total price-manipulation, sales and repurchase profits of about $900K, $1.1M and -$1.6M (p.1). Bored Ape Yacht Club is the densest at 0.275% of transactions, 0.414% of tokens and 0.265% of wallets (p.5). Case studies show what the cycle looks like: Otherdeed token 55343 had 17 wash sales within hours that walked the price from $14 to $197 (p.7), and an Art Blocks token rose 720% in under a month (p.7). One wallet that ranked first in collection-level sales profit at $8.8M is a detected wash trader (p.8).

The limitations are stated plainly and they are the operational content. Intermediary wallets flagged by the graph search may be unsuspecting buyers who merely passed a token along, and the collusion assumption behind the price-manipulation profit measure is described as based on anecdotal manual review (p.8). A manipulator who repurchases from a newly created wallet never satisfies the rule and is missed; a manipulator who simply waits out the 30 days is missed, though a separate study cited there finds 74.3% of NFT trading cycles occur within the 30-day threshold (p.9). The rule is thus a cheap identity screen with known false positives and known evasions, not a finding of intent.

## 4. Crypto wash trading at scale, and graph evidence from an exchange

`2404.07222v3` separates liquidity into a jump component, the ratio of regular to liquidity-adjusted daily return, and a diffusion component, the ratio of regular to liquidity-adjusted daily volatility (p.8-9). Extreme values are defined as a jump beta of 4 or more and a diffusion beta of 2.5 or more (p.9). Applying the 60%-removal treatment to ten large-capitalisation assets over 1,749 trading days, the treatment eliminates essentially all days of extreme liquidity volatility, reducing the count of maximum-beta days to zero for every asset, from as many as 19 days for Monero (p.11). Bitcoin and Ether never had extreme liquidity volatility even untreated, at maximum diffusion betas of 1.15 and 1.45 (p.11). The paper's interpretation is that manipulators in large-capitalisation markets run many small momentum trades rather than a few large ones, so liquidity diffusion is the better wash-trading indicator and the combination of high jump and high diffusion is the most reliable single screen (p.13). The broader claim, that wash trading is uncommon among established crypto assets on mainstream venues, sits in tension with the prevalence figures this same paper reports from other studies, and the caveats treat that disagreement as unresolved.

`1902.01941v1` offers the opposite kind of evidence: a forensic reconstruction of the leaked Mt. Gox transaction history from April 2011 to November 2013, cleaned from about 18 million rows to 6.7 million completed transactions (p.2). Transactions priced above 1.5 times the day's high or below 0.5 times the day's low are labelled abnormal, on the reasoning that such prints are impossible for ordinary users (p.3); one 2013/08/30 print was priced at $49,338.4 per coin against a day range of $128.56 to $142.76, and another at $0.81 (p.3). Abnormal accounts are 12.5% of the 119,343 accounts but account for only 2.8% of transactions, while more than 41% of the transactions inside abnormal accounts look ordinary, which the authors read as fake volume or liquidity provision (p.3). Abnormal-account graphs have clustering coefficients of 0.30 and 0.42 against 0.03 for normal accounts, and a singular-value decomposition of daily snapshots fits price with a Pearson correlation of 0.871 for the low-price account graph against 0.239 for the normal graph (p.4, p.6). The named patterns - a self-loop where one account transacted with itself 749 times in a day, a bi-directional pair exchanging more than 150 times, and star and polygon structures - are the direct analogues of the wash-trade cycles in Section 3 (p.7-8).

## 5. Settlement manipulation in ultra-short prediction markets

`2606.31675v1` is the most operationally consequential paper in the slice. It studies Polymarket's five-minute Bitcoin up/down contract, a binary claim that pays $1 if Bitcoin is higher at the close of a five-minute window than at its open, settling against a Chainlink oracle that aggregates prices across venues (p.5). Because the Binance midquote sits about 2.5 basis points from the oracle and finishes on the same side of the strike as the resolution about 85% of the time, a push of a few basis points on Binance reliably carries the resolution (p.6, p.124). Combined monthly volume on Kalshi and Polymarket rose from under $5B in September 2025 to about $24B by April 2026, and Kalshi crypto volume passed $1B in a month for the first time in March 2026 (p.3).

The empirical design exploits a clean launch: the five-minute contract went live on 12 February 2026, so the sample splits into a pre-launch baseline, an intermediate period with fifteen-minute and four-hour contracts only, and a post-launch period (p.6, p.39). In the final ten seconds before each close, Binance absolute order flow jumps about 50% above the pre-launch level, and the spike is sharpest where a push is pivotal: in the roughly 6% of cycles whose contract price still implies a near-even outcome just before the close, the near-settlement order-flow jump is about 3.9 times that in the rest (p.6). The clearest evidence that this is not information is the reversal: within ten seconds the price reverts, by about a quarter in the near-even cycles and a tenth in the others (p.6). Cycles in the top decile of relative final-ten-second flow, about 1,600 of them, are classified as manipulated; they fall disproportionately in thin hours, 56% overnight and 44% on weekends against 40% and 27% of normal cycles (p.6). A push against the favoured side flipped the winner in 65% of near-even manipulated cycles against 41% otherwise, and even in cycles the market gave a 90-100% chance, a push reversed the outcome 34% of the time against 1% with no push (p.7). Essentially all of the signed trading falls in the final fifty seconds (p.8).

The wealth accounting is exact because Polymarket settles on-chain. Of roughly 243,000 traders, 821 are identified as manipulators by realised profit; they take $8.2M in the pushed cycles and break even elsewhere, and 93% of the loss falls on retail, who are on the losing side of 65% of manipulated cycles against 48% of normal ones (p.7). A threshold sensitivity grid reproduces the same partition across twenty threshold pairs, with manipulator profit between $4.1M and $10.0M and retail the near-exact mirror (p.128). Two facts rule out hedging as the explanation: the cycles that a push still flips are precisely the nearly decided ones where the contract barely moves with the spot, so there is nothing to hedge, and the flow arrives in a single burst at the close rather than accumulating through the cycle (p.7-8).

The remedy follows from the model. A longer horizon inserts more ordinary trading rounds before settlement, so the pre-settlement order imbalance, a random walk with dispersion growing in the horizon, is less likely to end within the narrow pivotal band, and manipulation profit tends to zero (p.33-35). In the data the fifteen-minute contract shows only an attenuated footprint (p.121-123). A second lever is the settlement reference: a single-instant print is what a final-seconds push can move, while a reference averaged over a window or struck in a closing auction forces the manipulator to move the whole aggregate (p.35-36).

## 6. Insider flow and information-leakage scores

`2605.02286v2` evaluates a deadline-resolved Information Leakage Score on Polymarket insider cases. The score is the pre-event price move divided by the total move from open to resolution, anchored at the timestamp the underlying event first became public rather than at resolution (p.3). On the largest applicable contract, "US forces enter Iran by April 30", the article-derived anchor gives a score of +0.113 while a resolution-minus-one-hour proxy gives -0.331, a shift of 0.444 across zero: the proxy looks like counter-evidence while the corrected anchor looks like mild front-loading (p.6). The short-window variants are exactly zero at 30 minutes and 2 hours, and negative at 6 and 24 hours, so there is no last-minute informed spike (p.6). The per-category exponential hazard fits give a half-life of 2.9 days for military and geopolitical markets with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov p of 0.426 on 18 markets, a preliminary corporate-disclosure fit on 5 markets, and a rejection for regulatory-decision markets at p = 0.023 because their time-to-event distribution is bimodal (p.4).

The paper is candid about the funnel. Of eighteen Iran-cluster markets attempted, sixteen had the event timestamp recovered, eleven had positive lead time, only two had order-book coverage, and one satisfied every requirement end-to-end (p.5). The operational rule proposed is to flag a market only if the score exceeds 0.25 and a short-window variant exceeds 0.10, and the one market analysed does not satisfy it (p.7). Cross-market wallet analysis found 332 wallets active in both Iran-cluster markets, but the available trade history covers only the resolution-settlement window, so the overlap signal is settlement arbitrage rather than pre-event coordination (p.1, p.8). The methodological lesson for a platform is that the anchor is a first-class input: the same tape yields opposite conclusions depending on which timestamp is used.

## 7. Closing prices, open/close impact and mark-to-market manipulation

`1402.0910v1` investigates the Apple close at exactly $500.00 on 18 January 2013, an options expiration date on which $1.5 billion of stock's worth of options hinged on a one-penny difference in the settlement price (p.1-2). The chance probability of that exact close is put below 1 in 1,000 (p.3). The paper's contribution is to test the innocent explanation - delta hedging pinning the price to the strike - against the day's own price path. In the final hour the price rose toward the strike from below, but a delta hedger's required position falls as the price approaches the strike, so hedging would have required selling, pushing the price away from the strike, not toward it (p.5). A simulation with noise consistent with January intraday volatility puts the probability of reaching the strike at less than 1 per thousand over 5,000 runs (p.9), and only one prior close at a strike occurred in the 133 weeks since weekly options were introduced in July 2010 (p.9). The paper also corrects the hedging model it uses: the standard derivation omitted the price-dependence term of the hedger's position, and including it yields much weaker pinning (p.6). The limit is that this is one stock on one day, with the mechanism argued rather than observed.

`1912.01708v1` generalises the mark-to-market mechanism. Because spreads are wide and depth thin near the open, and narrow and deep near the close, an order placed at the open moves the price more than an equally sized order placed at the close (p.1). A firm holding a large portfolio can therefore expand it slightly at the open and contract it slightly at the close, losing a little on each round trip while pushing prices in its favour and posting mark-to-market gains on the whole book; the cartoon example shows a sell at the close moving the price down by a bit less than 0.05% and the buy at the open moving it up by 0.15% (p.2). The signature is a persistent positive overnight return and negative intraday return, which the paper documents across sixteen indices over three decades, including an S&P 500 overnight cumulative return of +1232% against an intraday -14% (p.3). It also notes that the pattern is unnecessary to the strategy: buying just after the open rather than at it removes the visible signature (p.5). This is an essay rather than a measurement study, and it is treated accordingly below.

## 8. What each evidence layer can and cannot establish

`2605.10486v2` supplies the taxonomy that keeps the rest of the slice honest. It separates four conduct channels that are usually conflated: market-price manipulation, which changes a venue or index price; outcome manipulation, which changes the real-world event; resolution manipulation, which changes the mapping from evidence to payout; and informed trading, which exploits non-public information without changing either (p.3). The channels differ by causal object, by evidence and by remedy, and a single trade sequence can touch several at once (p.4). Leverage amplifies conduct only when gross advantage scales faster than influence, detection, execution and sanction costs; the paper proves that a convex execution cost removes the free amplification (p.3), that leverage does expand profitable influence when influence costs are fixed (p.4), and that convex detection or enforceable position limits can reverse that (p.5). Position limits are effective only if they aggregate linked accounts and correlated instruments, because per-wallet limits need not bind when identities can be fragmented (p.9).

The observability criteria are the practical payload. Fills support concentration, timing, size, counterparty, cross-market and realised-position analysis, but do not reveal unfilled orders or cancellations (p.6). Spoofing, quote stuffing, cancellation races and displayed-liquidity withdrawal require attributed order placement, modification and cancellation records; market-level snapshots without participant attribution cannot support an address-level finding (p.6). Outcome manipulation requires event-domain records linking trading identities to persons with influence, and resolution manipulation requires oracle proposal, challenge, bond and payout records (p.6). The design requirements that follow include retaining quote-lifecycle records with participant attribution even when matching is off-chain, separating oracle from market-operation roles, and tracing benefit channels beyond trading profit (p.8).

## Caveats

Several papers here have no usable data. `1912.01708v1` is a polemic built on index-level overnight and intraday returns from a public web source, with no trade-level evidence and no identified actor; its own author concedes the pattern is unnecessary to the strategy. `1402.0910v1` is one stock on one day, with the hedging alternative argued from a model rather than observed. `2403.13429v1` reports a preliminary experiment on five stocks and five exchanges with labels generated by an algorithm, so its 91% is the fit of a model to a heuristic, not detector performance. `2605.02286v2` is explicitly a single-case proof of concept: of eighteen markets attempted, one satisfied every requirement. The slice is also mostly single-market or single-period: `2308.08683v1` covers two crypto pairs over hours of one crash, `1902.01941v1` covers one defunct exchange over 2011-2013, `2305.01543v1` covers seven NFT collections to mid-2022, and `2606.31675v1` covers one contract on one platform over two months. The slice disagrees with itself on prevalence: `2404.07222v3` concludes that wash trading is uncommon among large-capitalisation crypto assets on mainstream exchanges, while the same paper reports other studies finding 46.47% to more than 90% of reported volume, and `2305.01543v1` finds about 0.14% of NFT transactions. No paper here establishes a validated production detector with measured false-positive and false-negative rates against adjudicated cases, and none weighs detection cost against enforcement benefit. Nothing here is legal advice, and the governance sources are a dated snapshot.

## Papers read in depth

- `2308.08683v1` - Detecting Financial Market Manipulation with Statistical Physics Tools (2023). Models Level 3 orders as particles and detects spoofing and layering via passive-area momentum jumps; validated partly on synthetic insertions and limited to two crypto pairs.
- `2403.13429v1` - Detecting and Triaging Spoofing using Temporal Convolutional Networks (2024). Weakly supervised sequence labelling of order-book states with expert ranking; preliminary, with a labelling algorithm rather than adjudicated labels.
- `2305.01543v1` - NFT Wash Trading Detection (2023). A 30-day repurchase rule with graph cycles and BFS intermediaries across seven collections; the collusion assumption behind its profit measures is anecdotal.
- `2404.07222v3` - Liquidity Jump, Liquidity Diffusion, and Treatment on Wash Trading of Crypto Assets (2024). Splits liquidity into jump and diffusion and measures what wash-trade treatment removes; ten assets on one venue.
- `1902.01941v1` - Market Manipulation of Bitcoin: Evidence from Mining the Mt. Gox Transaction Network (2019). Graph and SVD forensics on leaked exchange data; one exchange, one period, leaked data.
- `2606.31675v1` - Settlement Manipulation in Prediction Markets (2026). Model plus on-chain evidence of settlement-time spot pushes in a five-minute binary contract; one contract, one platform, two months.
- `2605.02286v2` - Empirical Evaluation of Deadline-Resolved Information Leakage on Documented Polymarket Insider Cases (2026). Anchor sensitivity of an information-leakage score; a single in-scope market.
- `1402.0910v1` - The $500.00 AAPL close: Manipulation or hedging? A quantitative analysis (2014). Tests the hedging explanation for an exact-strike close; one stock, one day, model-argued.
- `1912.01708v1` - Celebrating Three Decades of Worldwide Stock Market Manipulation (2019). Describes open/close impact asymmetry as a mark-to-market manipulation channel; an essay on index returns with no trade-level data.
- `2605.10486v2` - Manipulation, Informed Trading, and Regulation in Leveraged Event-Linked Markets (2026). A four-channel conduct taxonomy and evidence-layer criteria for surveillance; no new empirical estimation.

## Where to next in the corpus
