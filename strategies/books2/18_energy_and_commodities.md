# Energy and commodity markets: storage is an option and the grid writes the strike

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads the electricity-and-storage cluster of the arXiv `q-fin.GN` harvest, together with the commodity-futures and gas papers that sit beside it. The slice holds 53 records from 2006 to 2026, 14 of them with a journal reference. It is written for an operator of a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform that already spans equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, and is deciding whether power and energy commodities belong in the same engine, the same data model, the same risk limits and the same settlement assumptions.

## What this covers

The slice has one arc: electricity is the only major asset class here whose deliverable cannot be freely stored, whose price is routinely negative, and whose trading day is a chain of auctions and gates rather than a continuous session, so a platform that treats power as "a futures contract with an unusual tick" will misprice both the instrument and its risk. The single most consequential finding is that battery energy arbitrage does not clear its own wear cost in most European day-ahead markets - at an observed wear cost near 100 EUR/MWh (`2112.09816v2`, p.11) - while remunerated fast frequency services are already accretive: the Danish FCR-N service was theoretically profitable in almost every hour of January-September 2021 even at that wear cost (`2112.09816v2`, p.19).

## The short answer

1. Battery energy arbitrage does not pay its own wear cost in most European day-ahead markets at an observed wear cost near 100 EUR/MWh, while remunerated frequency containment already does (`2112.09816v2`, p.11, p.19).
2. The refusal is often structural, not marginal: with wear cost cut to zero, day-ahead arbitrage in Spain was theoretically profitable on only about 310 days of 2019 (`2007.00486v2`, p.17).
3. Storage is an option, not a project: payoff is max(S + B - O, 0), with the service or energy spread as the payoff and the wear cost as the strike, so a net-present-value framing understates operational flexibility (`2112.09816v2`, p.9).
4. Storage is price-making: more independent investors cut each investor's profit but raise total installed capacity, and a small efficiency edge captures most of the market profit - a single Type-1 investor held about 70% of the profit share (`2201.02290v2`, p.4, p.5).
5. Power prices are a panel, not a series: each day is a curve of 24 (or 96) delivery periods, and about 40% of observed price variation is deterministic mean-reversion rather than innovation (`2605.13320v1`, p.2, p.13).
6. Electricity volatility does not exhibit the equity leverage effect: the apparent asymmetric response disappears once price level and mean-reversion are conditioned on (`2605.13320v1`, p.1).
7. The volatility panel is forecastable and the forecast is worth money: a matrix-HAR with a quarterly component lifted spread-risk-premium explanatory power from 24.4% to about 47% (`2606.05991v1`, p.15).
8. Renewables lower the price and compress the upper tail: in six US ISOs the merit-order effect is largest at the 0.90th quantile, and temporal volatility fell in five of the six (`2112.11338v3`, p.14, p.18).
9. Hydropower is the stabiliser in the mix: it cuts extreme price and volatility more than it cuts the mean, while wind raises volatility beyond the 70th percentile (`2203.02089v1`, p.9, p.11).
10. Delivery is a chain of gates, not an order: forward, day-ahead, intraday and balancing horizons settle residual exposure, and an executable action must pass venue, product and regulatory predicates before release (`2609.29108v2`, p.4, p.5, p.6).
11. Commodity futures curves are a separate tradeable object: Nelson-Siegel level, slope and curvature explain 96.5% of curve variation and slope continuation is the profitable leg (`2308.00383v1`, p.11, p.13).
12. Gas and power must be modelled jointly: removing the cross-commodity correlation moved a power-plant portfolio's 95% VaR from 1081 to 2213 over a three-year horizon (`0910.0236v3`, p.16).

## 1. Price formation in power and the negative-price tail

Power clears by merit order, so zero-marginal-cost renewables displace expensive generation and pull the clearing price down. The effect is not uniform: across six US ISOs an increase in variable renewable energy (VRE) penetration lowered the detrended system price in all of them, with the largest reduction at the 0.90th quantile; in PJM a move from 0-1% VRE cut the median detrended price by $1.6 and the 0.90th quantile by $10, while a later 5-6% move cut them by $0.2 and $0.8 (`2112.11338v3`, p.14). The mechanism is the same in hydro systems: a 1% increase in hydropower share reduced the detrended system price by $0.56/MWh at the mean and by $1.36/MWh at the 90th percentile (`2203.02089v1`, p.8, p.9).

Negative prices are the tail of that same mechanism, not a data error. They arise from a generation-demand imbalance when supply is high and demand is low, and inflexible thermal, nuclear or hydro units prefer not to shut down (`2112.11338v3`, p.18). The extreme itself is large: the detrended price minimum was -$2504.87/MWh in NYISO and -$208.37/MWh in ISONE over the study windows (`2112.11338v3`, p.14). Importantly, the slice finds no clean trend between the frequency of negative prices and VRE share; negative prints concentrate at high VRE in SPP and CAISO, the two ISOs with the highest penetration (`2112.11338v3`, p.18, p.19).

Market design also decides whether negatives can exist at all. Spanish day-ahead bids are constrained to between 0 and 180.3 EUR/MWh, so Spain cannot print a negative price, whereas France has no such limit and can go negative or much higher (`2007.00486v2`, p.11). The continuous-time literature adds a framing point that matters for index construction: hourly spot pricing is a Riemann integral over time, while load-duration pricing values the same energy by how many hours it is delivered, a Lebesgue integral (`1710.07918v1`, p.6, p.9). In a three-plant example, moving from spot to load-duration pricing cut total purchasing cost from 387.32 to 364 units and flattened profit shares from 77/59/30% to 60/52/48% (`1710.07918v1`, p.12, p.13).

## 2. Storage economics is an option written on the spread

Battery operation in a deregulated market is conditional: the asset is dispatched only when the service payment plus the balancing term exceeds the operating expense, so the payoff is max(S + B - O, 0) and the model is an option with a stochastic underlying and the wear cost as the strike (`2112.09816v2`, p.9). The wear cost is the pivot. With a battery cost of 300 EUR/kWh and 3000 equivalent full cycles, the pure wear cost is 0.1 EUR/kWh, that is 100 EUR/MWh (`2112.09816v2`, p.11). At that cost, energy arbitrage is not profitable in most European markets; it only becomes attractive below roughly 40-50 EUR/MWh of wear cost in Great Britain, France, Germany and Italy, below about 20 EUR/MWh in Spain, and is barely viable in Norway at any cost because hydropower already supplies the flexibility (`2112.09816v2`, p.13, p.14).

The Spanish study sharpens the same result into market-design terms. For day-ahead arbitrage, the wear cost must fall to 15 EUR/MWh for the potentially profitable utilisation rate to exceed 20%; adding ancillary services raises that threshold to 35 EUR/MWh, and upward secondary reserve to 50 EUR/MWh (`2007.00486v2`, p.1). The reason is payment structure: secondary reserve pays a capacity band as well as energy, and its band price fell from 19.58 EUR/MW in 2015 to 8.31 EUR/MW in 2019, shrinking the number of theoretically profitable hours from 2347 to 596 in the upward direction and from 1880 to 74 downward (`2007.00486v2`, p.14, p.16). Frequency services dominate energy arbitrage in every zone studied: the Danish FCR-N service reached close to 100% potentially profitable utilisation in January-September 2021, while Norway stayed under 20% at the same 100 EUR/MWh wear cost (`2112.09816v2`, p.19, p.20).

Two further results constrain how storage should be simulated. First, the trade is not risk-free: in a real community-storage case study, a utility using day-ahead locational marginal prices earned $101 per month per unit against $185 with perfect foresight, so the value is roughly 35% versus 65% of the $284 monthly retail cost (`1407.0433v2`, p.11). Second, storage affects its own revenue: when many independent investors compete, each investor's profit and total profit fall as their number rises, but total invested capacity rises, and heterogeneous efficiency matters more than scale (`2201.02290v2`, p.4, p.5).

## 3. Multi-market operation and the permission gate

The commercial case for a battery is not one market but several: day-ahead arbitrage captures the base spread, intraday re-optimisation captures forecast error, and balancing or frequency response adds a third layer (`2609.29108v2`, p.6). The magnitude is concrete: a 100 MW / 200 MWh system charging at 20 EUR/MWh and discharging at 140 EUR/MWh captures a gross margin of 24000 EUR per cycle before round-trip efficiency, grid fees and wear, and European intraday markets regularly show spreads above 100 EUR/MWh between surplus and scarcity hours of the same delivery day (`2609.29108v2`, p.6).

The same paper supplies the system-design constraint that makes power unusual: an executable action is not a price decision but the intersection of a legal-regulatory gate and a venue-execution gate. Release requires the order to be inside the gate-closure window, the participant to be authorised in that venue, the product to be permitted, and the active regulatory predicates to hold; otherwise the null action is the correct output (`2609.29108v2`, p.6). Residual exposure is computed per interval and bidding zone as forecast demand minus generation, hedges and cleared day-ahead, intraday and balancing volumes (`2609.29108v2`, p.5). For a platform, this means compliance under REMIT, MiFID II, MiFIR and EMIR is a pre-trade constraint on order construction, not a post-trade report (`2609.29108v2`, p.3, p.5). Batteries also face an internal capacity conflict - capacity committed to frequency response is unavailable to intraday arbitrage - which the architecture captures as a shared state vector (`2609.29108v2`, p.6).

## 4. Delivery and settlement mechanics

European power is a multi-horizon control system. The forward layer allocates transmission rights and hedges; the day-ahead layer clears all bidding zones jointly with cross-zonal capacity under the single day-ahead coupling; the intraday layer absorbs forecast error in continuous trading; and the balancing layer resolves the residual physical deviation after market close (`2609.29108v2`, p.2, p.4). Balancing products are platform-specific: automatic frequency restoration reserve (aFRR), manual frequency restoration reserve (mFRR) and replacement reserves are organised through PICASSO, MARI and TERRE respectively (`2609.29108v2`, p.3, p.4).

The settlement mechanics are what separate a schedule from a position. In Spain, secondary reserve automatic generation control is issued every 4 seconds, must respond within 100 seconds and must be sustained for 15 minutes, while tertiary reserve must respond within 15 minutes; imbalance cost is then charged according to the state of the grid and the direction of the deviation (`2007.00486v2`, p.7). Primary reserve in Spain is mandatory and non-remunerable, so there is no revenue there at all (`2007.00486v2`, p.7). Operational timing is tight enough that a community-storage controller was judged unusable on information almost 24 hours old and only minimally usable on 15-minute-old information, and was re-run every 5 to 10 minutes (`1407.0433v2`, p.10). The day-ahead battery studies themselves assume a single full charge-discharge cycle per day, aligned to the daily price peak and trough, which caps how much energy a short-duration battery can move (`2112.09816v2`, p.12).

## 5. Volatility is a panel, is mean-reverting, and is forecastable

Electricity volatility is not an equity-style scalar. Each day's cross-section is a curve of delivery periods - 24 hourly or 96 quarter-hourly - and because spot prices can be negative, the usual log transformation is unavailable (`2605.13320v1`, p.2). Using three European generation zones over 2551 daily observations (61368 data points per zone, 365 weeks), the study estimated a realized covariation matrix and found that deterministic mean-reversion accounts for about 40% of the observed variation; ignoring it leaves the estimate badly contaminated (`2605.13320v1`, p.10, p.13). The common-shock half-life was about 3 days in Germany and about 6 days in Norway (`2605.13320v1`, p.12).

The follow-on forecasting paper shows the panel can be predicted one week ahead and that the prediction is economically useful. A parsimonious matrix-HAR model with daily, weekly, monthly and quarterly components beat a simple exponential-moving-average benchmark, and adding renewable generation shares and price covariates improved calibration (`2606.05991v1`, p.7). The best models reached a Mincer-Zarnowitz R2 of about 40-45% on the realized variance and 94.7% coverage on pointwise 95% prediction bands (`2606.05991v1`, p.7, p.11). Most strikingly, replacing a backward-looking 28-day variance with the forecast realized covariation raised the explanatory power of a spread-risk-premium regression from 24.4% to 47.2% (`2606.05991v1`, p.15).

## 6. Commodity curves beyond power

Electricity is not the whole commodity opportunity. Fitting a Nelson-Siegel curve to the term structure of 21 commodity futures, the level, slope and curvature parameters explained on average 96.5% of the variation in futures price curves; removing the slope term dropped the fit to 37.5% and removing curvature dropped it to 82.7% (`2308.00383v1`, p.11). Liquidity is concentrated at the front: the nearest four contracts represented 82.2% of total open interest on average, which is why the strategies trade only those maturities (`2308.00383v1`, p.8).

The tradeable signal is continuation of the previous day's curve movement. The level strategy was unprofitable, but the slope strategy earned an annualised mean excess return of 1.77% (t-statistic 7.23, Sharpe 1.41) and the curvature strategy 0.68% (t-statistic 5.34, Sharpe 1.23) (`2308.00383v1`, p.13). These are gross numbers. Daily turnover was 1.17 and 1.20 respectively, and after three transaction-cost scenarios the slope strategy netted 0.55% to 1.31% per year while the curvature strategy netted -0.55% to 0.24% and was negative under two of the three cost scenarios (`2308.00383v1`, p.16). The slope strategy also degraded over time: its net Sharpe fell from 1.74 to 0.71 after the 2000 financialisation period and from 1.47 to 0.20 after 2009 (`2308.00383v1`, p.24).

## 7. Cross-commodity coupling: gas and power

Power and gas are linked through the spark spread and through fuel-switching, and the joint model matters for risk. A two-factor specification - a common slow mean-reverting factor plus a commodity-specific spiky diffusion with a normal-inverse-Gaussian invariant density - reproduces both the autocorrelation of each price and the cross-correlation between them without introducing jump processes (`0910.0236v3`, p.9). The fitted speeds separate cleanly: fast reversion of roughly 2 days for gas and roughly 30 days for electricity, and slow common reversion of roughly 64 days (`0910.0236v3`, p.5). Both series were far from normal, with excess kurtosis of 4.5 for gas and 2.3 for electricity (`0910.0236v3`, p.4).

The risk consequences are large enough to change capital allocation. For a portfolio short a gas-fired power plant (heat rate 3, generation cost 5 EUR/MWh, interest rate 5%), the estimated 95% Value-at-Risk was 262.4 at six months, 495.4 at one year and 1081.0 at three years (`0910.0236v3`, p.14, p.16). Removing the cross-commodity correlation raised those numbers to 250.1, 880.2 and 2213.1, while collapsing the spike component to a Gaussian lowered them to 107.7, 275.9 and 565.5 (`0910.0236v3`, p.16). Getting the dependence structure wrong is therefore not a second-order modelling choice.

## Caveats

Several papers here have no market data at all. `1710.07918v1` is a theory paper with a three-plant numerical example, not an empirical study. `2609.29108v2` is an architecture proposal and its own limitations section says it has not been validated on synchronized multi-venue event streams. The battery studies are optimistic by construction: both `2112.09816v2` and `2007.00486v2` assume perfect price foresight to pick the daily minimum and maximum, so their utilisation rates are upper bounds. `1407.0433v2` is a single Michigan feeder with a 25 kW, 50 kWh unit and excludes capital cost from its "profit", and its feeder-loss effect was tiny ($185 versus $182). `2201.02290v2` is a game-theoretic equilibrium on CAISO 2019 with 12 monthly scenarios and a linear price-impact assumption. The two volatility papers, `2605.13320v1` and `2606.05991v1`, are the same German-led panel and report matrix estimands, not live profit and loss. `2112.11338v3` and `2203.02089v1` are regression studies and cannot establish causality. `0910.0236v3` is calibrated on 2003-2008 French and UK data.

The slice also disagrees with itself in useful ways. `2112.11338v3` finds volatility falling with VRE in five of six ISOs but rising in SPP once VRE exceeds about 40% (p.18), and `2203.02089v1` finds hydropower's volatility contribution statistically insignificant at the mean yet significant and stabilising at the extreme. `2308.00383v1` documents a slope premium that weakened after 2000 and 2009, which is a warning against reading its gross Sharpe ratios as current. No paper here establishes that any of these strategies is net profitable in a live account, none prices intraday power liquidity or negative-price settlement penalties, and none covers prediction-market or options mechanics for energy. The corpus is one arXiv category, so it over-represents what academics can publish and under-represents venue operator practice.

## Papers read in depth

- `2112.09816v2` - Potential utilization of Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) in the major European electricity markets (2021). General battery payoff model and utilisation factors across seven European markets; assumes perfect day-ahead foresight.
- `2007.00486v2` - Barriers to grid-connected battery systems: Evidence from the Spanish electricity market (2020). Break-even wear costs for four Spanish applications; single market, 2015-2019, perfect foresight.
- `2201.02290v2` - Strategic Storage Investment in Electricity Markets (2022). Non-cooperative equilibrium of competing storage investors on CAISO data; linear price model, 12 monthly scenarios.
- `1407.0433v2` - Economic Optimal Operation of Community Energy Storage Systems in Competitive Energy Markets (2014). Hierarchical charge and discharge scheduling on a real feeder; one feeder, capital cost excluded, price forecast drives the result.
- `2609.29108v2` - Functional Architecture of European Electricity Trading Markets (2026). Regulatory and venue permission model for AI-supported power trading; no empirical validation.
- `1710.07918v1` - Electricity Market Theory Based on Continuous Time Commodity Model (2017). Continuous-time commodity model contrasting spot and load-duration pricing; theory and a toy example only.
- `2203.02089v1` - A Robust Statistical Analysis of the Role of Hydropower on the System Electricity Price and Price Volatility (2022). Quantile effects of hydropower in ISONE; association rather than causation, single region.
- `2112.11338v3` - Role of Variable Renewable Energy Penetration on Electricity Price and its Volatility Across Independent System Operators in the United States (2021). Six-ISO quantile and skew-t study; relationship is non-linear and one ISO reverses.
- `2605.13320v1` - The fine structure of electricity price volatility (2026). Functional-data estimator of the realized covariation panel in Germany, Norway and Spain; matrix estimands, not P&L.
- `2606.05991v1` - Forecasting of volatility and risk premia in electricity markets (2026). Matrix-HAR forecast of the volatility panel and its use in spread-risk-premium regressions; same panel, single zone in the horserace.
- `2308.00383v1` - Exploiting the dynamics of commodity futures curves (2023). Nelson-Siegel level, slope and curvature strategies on 21 futures; net edge is small and has weakened over time.
- `0910.0236v3` - Joint Modelling of Gas and Electricity spot prices (2009). Two-factor joint model with spike densities and a VaR application; calibrated on 2003-2008 French and UK data.
