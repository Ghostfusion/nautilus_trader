# Financial networks, contagion and systemic risk: direct interbank exposure is a weak channel that switches on when it meets overlapping portfolios

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers the financial-network and systemic-risk slice of a local harvest of the arXiv `q-fin.GN` (General Finance) category: 3095 records, 1997-2026. The slice is the 322 records whose title or abstract touches interbank networks, contagion, cascades, systemic risk or connectedness; 40 of those were shortlisted and 12 were read from the PDFs. It is written for an operator of a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform that trades equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, and it is deliberately operational: what a financial network is measurable as, when distress propagates through it, when it does not, and which of its statistics are worth putting into a risk engine rather than into a research notebook.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from the descriptive topology of national interbank markets, through closed-form cascade thresholds and multiplex layers of maturity and seniority, to distress that propagates before any default and to centrality that depends on the prevailing level of risk. Its single most consequential finding for this platform is that the network of direct bilateral exposures is, on its own, a weak contagion channel - stress tests of the Austrian banking network put the affected fraction in the low single-digit percent - but it becomes a powerful amplifier once a common asset shock is added, multiplying the bankruptcy fraction in a regime that the network topology itself helps determine `1306.3704v1`. The rest of the slice says how to measure the conditions under which that switch is on.

## The short answer

1. Direct interbank exposures are a weak standalone contagion channel, but they amplify overlapping-portfolio losses: in the Austrian network the interaction multiplies the bankruptcy fraction in a specific overlap regime `1306.3704v1`.
2. Contagion is non-monotone in connectivity, and the turning point - the critical degree - is a closed-form function of interest rates, leverage and liquidity `1402.4783v2`.
3. With realistic parameters the critical degree is small, about 5 to 10, while the FedFunds market had a mean degree near 15 with almost half of banks below 4 `1402.4783v2`.
4. Debt concentration, not link count alone, sets cascade size: the network whose hubs are large debtors has an aggregate default cascade of 0.99 against 0.49 when the hubs are large creditors `1410.2549v1`.
5. Homogeneous or randomized simulations overstate fragility: randomizing bank sizes and loan weights inflated the average cascade 1.4x to 2.5x above the empirical network `1310.1634v2`.
6. Topology carries early-warning information only against a heterogeneous null model: the Dutch network diverges from its configuration-model expectation from 2005Q1, three years before the 2008 collapse `1302.2063v4`.
7. The earliest precursor is an excess of unreciprocated three-bank lending loops in 2000Q3-2004Q4, which then becomes the most avoided motif `1302.2063v4`.
8. Aggregating loan maturities destroys structure: complete maturity aggregation is always rejected for the Russian interbank network, and the optimal granularity lies strictly between aggregation and full separation `1906.08617v1`.
9. Distress can propagate without any default: mark-to-market valuation of interbank claims creates endogenous volatility clustering and a down-market effect, and yields lower solvency probabilities than static clearing `2211.15431v4`.
10. Dilutable long-term debt reverses the stability role of the network: indebtedness and connectedness become stabilizing, and a class of exponential networks implements the efficient allocation `2608.13979v1`.
11. Mixing debt seniority is stabilizing, and the optimal ratio for uncorrelated Erdos-Renyi networks is 1.79 senior per junior loan `1501.05400v3`.
12. A node's systemic importance depends on the prevailing level of risk: the risk-dependent centrality reduces to degree at low risk and to eigenvector centrality at high risk, so rankings can interlace without any topology change `1907.07908v4`.

## 1. What a financial network is measurable as

The descriptive papers agree on a small set of stylized facts and disagree on how much they matter. The Dutch interbank network is built from prudential reports of exposures above 1.5 million euro with maturity up to one year, at quarterly frequency, giving 44 snapshots from 1998Q1 to 2008Q4 with 91 to 102 banks in each (p.11). The Austrian dataset covers 12 quarters from 2006 to 2008 with 825 to 846 banks per quarter (p.4). The Italian e-MID overnight market is reconstructed as 257 daily netted-exposure networks, averaging 128 nodes and 355 links in 2006 and 70 nodes and 161 links in 2011 (p.4). The Russian market is unusually granular: 2.4 million individual loan contracts over 57 monthly networks from January 2000 to October 2004, preassigned into eight maturity classes (p.2).

The distributions are consistently heavy-tailed but not clean power laws. Austrian total assets follow a power law with exponent about 0.75 over roughly three orders of magnitude, liquid assets about 0.67, with a flat small-bank regime and a cutoff above about 35 billion euro (p.5). The interbank network's degree distribution is heavy-tailed against an Erdos-Renyi benchmark, degree assortativity averages -0.62 plus or minus 0.03, and average local clustering is 0.87 plus or minus 0.02 against 0.032 for a random graph of the same average degree (p.9, p.10). A degree-preserving rewiring reproduces the negative assortativity and the clustering, but not the excess of short directed loops: the real 2006Q1 network has about 8000 directed 3-cycles and 140000 three-node source-sink patterns, exceeding the configuration-model distribution by more than 8 standard deviations (p.17, p.18). That last residual is the part of the topology that a simple degree sequence cannot explain, and it is the part the early-warning literature builds on.

The simulation study `1410.2549v1` separates connectivity from concentration. Using directed preferential-attachment networks of 1000 nodes whose degree exponents land between 2.2 and 3.2 (p.6), it builds three variants: debt-concentrated, credit-concentrated and symmetric. The aggregate default impact is close across them, 0.48, 0.46 and 0.43 (p.12), but the aggregate default cascade separates sharply: 0.99 for the debt-concentrated network against 0.79 symmetric and 0.49 for the credit-concentrated one (p.13). The leading bank in the credit-concentrated network has total assets 122 times the mean, against 55 times in the symmetric network (p.12). The lesson is that the direction in which the hubs are concentrated - large debtors versus large creditors - is a first-order property, not a detail.

## 2. Early warning is a change relative to a heterogeneous null model

The Dutch study `1302.2063v4` is the cleanest demonstration that the same statistic can be useless or predictive depending on the benchmark. Size, link count and connectance show no significant change at the onset of the crisis (p.2). Against a directed random graph, the dyadic motif z-scores jump abruptly in 2008 with no preceding build-up: the network can be seen, but not foreseen (p.3, p.4). Against the directed configuration model, which preserves each bank's observed in- and out-degree, the picture inverts: z-scores are stationary and below 2.5 in absolute value for quarters 1 to 28 (1998-2004), then change sign and drift from quarter 29, 2005Q1 (p.5). The transition is a three-year pre-crisis phase, not a jump (p.5). A yet stricter reciprocal configuration model leaves triadic motifs 2 and 10 as the significant signatures and removes motifs 5 and 12 (p.5). The earliest anomaly is motif 9, the unreciprocated three-bank lending loop: over-represented from 2000Q3 to 2004Q4, then the most under-represented motif under the reciprocal null model (p.6). The authors' interpretation is mechanical: an unreciprocated loop is a risk externality that no participant prices, because each bank cannot see that counterparty risk loops back to itself (p.9). They also find no significant core-periphery structure once bank heterogeneity is controlled, and sometimes an anti-core pattern (p.8).

A market-based cousin of this idea is `2310.10283v1`, which abandons exposure data for high-frequency stock returns. It builds multiplex recurrence networks over 16 Chinese listed banks using one-minute prices from 4 January 2012 to 30 October 2020, 2005 trading days (p.11), and defines crises as jumps in a market-cap-weighted bank portfolio detected by a bipower-variation test. At a 0.001 confidence level that yields 12 crisis days; at 0.0025 it yields 27 (p.12). Peaks in the average inter-mutual-information indicator, with a nine-day forward risk window, cover 9 of the 12 crises with 3 exact coincidences (p.12), whereas the average edge-overlap indicator covers only 7 of 27 (p.12). Against the standard early-warning benchmark of rising variance, lag-1 autocorrelation and low-frequency power, the MRN indicator is not obviously better in kind: the benchmark's variance trend passes sensitivity in 0 of 12 crises and significance in 1 of 12, the mean power spectrum 2 of 12 and 6 of 12, and lag-1 autocorrelation 2 of 12 and 5 of 12 (p.15). The MRN result is a detection rate on 12 events, which is a small sample for the claim.

## 3. Cascade thresholds in closed form

The most reusable analytical result in the slice is the critical degree of `1402.4783v2`. With a mean-field model of interbank lending parameterised by the external interest rate R, the interbank rate r, the liquidity ratio f and the leverage ratio Lambda, the first critical degree is k* = [ r(1-f) - max(r(1-f) + 2 Lambda - 1, 0) ] / [ (R-1)(1-Lambda) + Lambda ] (p.5). Below that degree an individual shock propagates to the shocked bank's neighbours; above it, losses are diluted and the neighbours survive (p.5). The second critical degree is of order the square root of the first, so with realistic parameters failures hardly extend beyond first neighbours (p.16). In the high-leverage, low-liquidity limit the formula collapses to k* = 1/(R-1), which reads as a cost-benefit rule: the ratio of the potential loss to the potential profit per transaction (p.8). For plausible rates, liquidity and leverage the critical degree is of order 5 to 10 (p.9). The empirical contrast is sharp: FedFunds had a mean degree near 15, but almost half of banks had out-degree below 4 (p.9). The failure distribution inherits the tail of the degree distribution, so a scale-free degree distribution gives a fat-tailed failure distribution - the robust-yet-fragile property (p.7). The authors note the criterion is isomorphic to the condition for cooperation to evolve on graphs, b/c > k (p.9).

`1909.09239v1` generalises the solvency cascade to inhomogeneous random financial networks and adds fractional recovery. Bankruptcy charges are (1/lambda - 1) times the negative part of the solvency buffer, so recovery on interbank debt falls continuously to zero once the buffer drops below minus lambda times the interbank debt (p.15). Under a locally-tree-like independence property that is proved for this cascade mechanism (p.20), the cascade equilibrium is the least fixed point of a generating function G on the type space, and a non-trivial cascade exists when the maximal eigenvalue of the gradient of G at the all-solvent point exceeds one (p.13). The single-shock distribution converges to a non-negative Levy random variable whose jump measure convolves the balance-sheet and exposure densities (p.18). This is the rigorous counterpart to the mean-field approximation above.

## 4. Contagion needs a second channel

`1306.3704v1` is the paper that most changes how a stress test should be built. Its Austrian stress tests run every bank as a seed under three mechanisms: counterparty loss, roll-over risk and overlapping portfolios. Under counterparty loss and roll-over risk considered in isolation, the contagion probability is a few percent and the conditional extent of contagion is small, because a prudent bank can cap its exposure to any single counterparty at its own equity (p.12). Once banks share a common asset, the picture changes. If every bank holds a fraction c of a common asset and that asset depreciates, the fraction of banks going under reaches 30% or more at c of about 0.05 (p.14). Adding the interbank network on top produces a discontinuity: the average extent of contagion jumps at c of about 0.3, and the probability of more than ten bankruptcies rises from below 2% to above 30% (p.16). With endogenous liquidation of the common asset, the contagion probability rises from about 0.14 to 0.49 as c goes from 0 to 1 (p.16). The mechanism is direct: a common-asset devaluation erodes the equity that was the banks' own protection against counterparty loss, so exposures that were previously safe become critical (p.14). This is the single most consequential finding in the slice.

## 5. Multiplexity: maturity and seniority are not details

`1906.08617v1` shows that collapsing maturity layers discards real structure. Fitting a coarse-grained layered stochastic block model to the 57 monthly Russian networks, it finds the optimal maturity granularity always lies strictly between complete aggregation and complete differentiation, so the eight reporting classes are partly redundant but aggregation is always rejected (p.4, p.11). The number of statistically significant lending-pattern bins rises with market development, correlating with the two phases of the Russian market: before month 35 mostly two patterns are discerned (maturities up to 30 days and above), while the emerging-maturity phase supports up to four (p.11). The short layers below 30 days carry 97% of loans (p.5). The bins acquire economic meaning: the important banks in short bins both lend and borrow comparable amounts, consistent with intermediation, while the important banks in long bins are net sources or sinks, consistent with financing (p.13). The authors warn that maturity aggregation, or studying a single layer, is not only an incomplete representation of the mesoscale but also conceals those economic functions (p.2).

`1501.05400v3` makes the same argument for seniority, using a two-layer multiplex network of junior and senior loans and a multilevel threshold-cascade condition. Self-interested banks would prefer all loans senior, but the all-senior network is as vulnerable as the all-junior one; an intermediate mix minimizes the set of network densities for which bankruptcies spread widely (p.5). For uncorrelated Erdos-Renyi networks the optimal seniority ratio is 1.79, that is on the order of 50% to 100% more senior than junior loans (p.5, p.2), and it rises as the junior-default threshold falls, meaning that as buffers erode more loans should be senior (p.8). With heavy-tailed degree distributions the optimum is a junior fraction of about 0.30, a seniority ratio near 2.3, qualitatively the same as the Erdos-Renyi case (p.9). Splitting a network into more seniority levels shrinks the cascade region further (p.9).

## 6. Distress without default, and dilution that stabilizes

`2211.15431v4` changes the accounting rule rather than the topology. Interbank claims are marked to market at the discounted expected payment, so a bank's own balance sheet depends on the market's conditional probability that its counterparties survive to maturity (p.8). This makes the clearing problem forward-backward: today's capital requires future default probabilities, and the solution is a fixed point in the solvency-probability vector (p.17). The economic consequence is that the network generates an endogenous stochastic-volatility term in equity returns, with clustering and a down-market effect - volatility rises when capital falls and the effect is stronger the more stressed the system (p.15, p.16). Early defaults mean the clearing outcome is worse than the static one: in the two-bank example the maximal dynamic solvency probability at time zero is (5/9, 1/3), below the static benchmark of full solvency, and the maximal and minimal clearing solutions differ (p.14). The paper also builds interbank term structures and shows that an inverted yield curve for one institution can arise purely from the riskiness of other banks in the system (p.4). That is a mechanism by which curve slope carries contagion information, not only macro information.

`2608.13979v1` supplies the opposite sign, under a different institutional assumption. If interbank debt is long-term and can be diluted by new senior borrowing, a shocked bank can pledge its interbank claims as collateral and raise liquidity; high indebtedness and dense connections then transfer liquidity from healthy to shocked banks and are stabilizing (p.1). Netting, which improves stability when debt is non-dilutable, undermines it when debt is dilutable (p.3). The payment equilibrium exists and is generically unique (p.12), with multiplicity only on a knife-edge where total resources exactly exhaust shocks (p.13). The geometry is a mirror image of the cascade papers: with non-dilutable debt there is a default radius d = theta y / F around a shocked bank inside which banks fail, while with dilutable debt there is a salvation radius d = F / (l - theta y) around a healthy bank inside which no bank fails (p.11, p.14). A class of asymmetric exponential networks implements the efficient priority rule, saving the largest set of banks that can be saved and writing off the rest, using plain debt rather than a resolution authority (p.3). That network is not pairwise stable, whereas core-periphery networks are, which the authors offer as an explanation for observed structures and their under-insurance (p.1, p.3). The mechanism relies on gross positions: the authors note that gross interbank positions are often an order of magnitude larger than net, giving Barclays' 2021 net position as about an eighth of gross (p.6).

## 7. Who is systemically important depends on the risk level

`1907.07908v4` derives centrality from a Susceptible-Infected process on the network, with the external risk level beta equal to the infection rate times time. The risk-dependent centrality of node i is the i-th entry of e to the power beta A times the all-ones vector (p.10). Its limit behaviour is the operational point: as beta goes to zero the ranking is the degree ranking, and as beta goes to infinity it is eigenvector centrality (p.11). Between those limits the ranking can interlace - two nodes can swap places purely because the external risk level changed, with no change in topology (p.1). Applied to an S&P 100 asset network built from daily returns from January 2001 to December 2017, the rankings are highly volatile during the 2007-2008 crisis, and in the corporate board network a rise in risk-centrality rank during a crisis corresponds to a less resilient shareholder-value reaction (p.2). A practical caution: at high risk the circulability share of the centrality settles near 1/n, and the transmissibility share near (n-1)/n, so at extreme risk the measure mainly reports graph size rather than node identity (p.14).

`2310.10283v1` adds a topological reading of the same question. It reports that at the moments flagged as peaks the maximum spanning tree of the recurrence network generally lacks a dominant core node, while at non-peak times it usually has one stable central node (p.15). Whether that generalises beyond 16 Chinese banks over eight years is untested here.

## Caveats

Most of the empirical evidence is single-country and single-period: the Dutch network is 44 quarters to 2008 `1302.2063v4`, the Austrian network is 12 quarters of 2006-2008 `1306.3704v1`, the Russian market is 2000-2004 `1906.08617v1`, e-MID is 2006 and 2011 only `1310.1634v2`, and the Chinese bank network is 2012-2020 `2310.10283v1`. Four papers in the read set have no data at all and are models: the critical-degree paper `1402.4783v2`, the inhomogeneous random financial network `1909.09239v1`, the dynamic distress-contagion model `2211.15431v4`, and the debt-dilution model `2608.13979v1`; their results are conditional on their assumptions, and the dilution paper says so explicitly by noting its exponential network is not pairwise stable. The slice disagrees with itself on the sign of connectedness: more connectivity is non-monotone or harmful in the cascade papers `1402.4783v2` `1410.2549v1`, while it is stabilizing in the dilutable-debt model `2608.13979v1`; the reconciliation is the accounting rule and the debt maturity, not a contradiction in the data, but no paper here tests both on one dataset. The seniority result `1501.05400v3` is derived for uncorrelated Erdos-Renyi layers and only illustrated for heavy tails, and it assumes loss given default of 100% at and below the defaulted layer. The early-warning results are weaker than they look: the Dutch precursor is one network over one crisis `1302.2063v4`, and the recurrence indicator is evaluated on 12 events in one market `2310.10283v1`. No paper in this slice reports a net-of-cost trading edge, none covers equities, FX, crypto or prediction-market execution, and none establishes that any network statistic predicts returns; the usable content is about measuring and stress-testing exposure, not about alpha.

## Papers read in depth

- `1306.3704v1` - How interbank lending amplifies overlapping portfolio contagion (2013). The strongest finding in the slice: direct exposure is weak alone but amplifies a common-asset shock; one country, 12 quarters.
- `1402.4783v2` - Mapping systemic risk: critical degree and failures distribution (2014). Closed-form critical degree and fat-tailed failure distribution; a mean-field model with no data.
- `1302.2063v4` - Early-warning signals of topological collapse in interbank networks (2013). Configuration-model divergence and the unreciprocated-loop precursor; one network over one crisis.
- `1310.1634v2` - Cascades in real interbank markets (2013). Empirical e-MID cascades and null-model comparison showing homogeneous simulations overstate fragility; two years only.
- `1410.2549v1` - Propagation of Systemic Risk in Interbank Networks (2014). Separates connectivity from debt-versus-credit concentration; synthetic preferential-attachment networks only.
- `1906.08617v1` - Loan maturity aggregation in interbank lending networks obscures mesoscale structure and economic functions (2019). Maturity layers carry economic function and must not be collapsed; one market, 2000-2004.
- `1501.05400v3` - Cascades in multiplex financial networks with debts of different seniority (2015). Optimal seniority mixing for uncorrelated layers; analytical, with heavy tails only illustrative.
- `1909.09239v1` - Systemic Cascades On Inhomogeneous Random Financial Networks (2019). General percolation framework with fractional recovery and a proof of the tree-like independence property; no numerical application.
- `2211.15431v4` - Endogenous distress contagion in a dynamic interbank model (2022). Mark-to-market valuation, endogenous volatility and interbank term structures; theory and small numerical examples.
- `2608.13979v1` - Systemic Risk in Financial Networks Revisited: Debt Dilution as a Backdoor Bail-in (2026). Dilution reverses the stability sign of connectedness and debt; an abstract model, with the stable network not the efficient one.
- `1907.07908v4` - Risk-dependent centrality in economic and financial networks (2019). Centrality as a function of the external risk level, with ranking interlacement; illustration on two networks.
- `2310.10283v1` - Unveiling Early Warning Signals of Systemic Risks in Banks: A Recurrence Network-Based Approach (2023). High-frequency recurrence indicator for bank crises; 16 banks, 12 events.
