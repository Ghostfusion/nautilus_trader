# Portfolio construction and allocation: estimator error, views, and what survives out of sample

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers the general-finance evidence on how portfolio weights are actually built and how fragile the inputs are. The slice is 112 records matching the q-fin.GN allocation-and-construction topics in the local arXiv harvest, spanning 2000-2026, 15 of them carrying a journal reference. From 40 candidate papers screened, 12 were read from the PDFs with page-anchored notes. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets: someone who has to choose a covariance estimator, decide how much to shrink a signal, decide whether to trust a backtest Sharpe, and explain to a risk committee why two honest research teams disagree about the same factor model. The companion brief `strategies/books/15_portfolio_construction_under_frictions.md` covers liquidity- and impact-aware construction from the q-fin.TR harvest; this brief deliberately does not repeat it. The angle here is estimator error and shrinkage, factor and covariance estimation, views and priors, drawdown-aware allocation, return measurement, and the cross-sectional evidence on what actually allocates.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from "the inputs are wrong" to "the measurement is wrong" to "the test itself is a design choice". It starts with estimator error: a covariance matrix that is merely poorly conditioned pushes the mean-variance weight vector away from the direction of expected returns, and the size of that rotation is bounded by the condition number alone `2107.06194v5` (p.4); Markowitz variance itself is only the zero-volume-fluctuation approximation to a market-based variance that can be far larger or far smaller `2507.21824v1` (p.10). It then shows the same fragility in views and factors: Black-Litterman cannot express subjective confidence at all `2406.01199v1` (p.9), skew-normal views cut portfolio volatility at a fixed target return `2310.12333v1` (p.24), and a factor model's ranking flips with the rebalancing rule used to build the test portfolios `2606.19550v1` (p.23). The single most consequential finding is a bookkeeping one: using net returns instead of log returns, and summing instead of compounding, inflates the S&P 500 Sharpe ratio by nearly 30% and, if the arithmetic mean is compounded, overstates the sixty-year terminal value by 89% `2405.10920v1` (p.19, p.8). No optimizer, shrinkage rule or regime model can repair an evaluation that is already overstated by that much.

## The short answer

1. Return bookkeeping is the largest single error source: net returns inflate the S&P 500 Sharpe ratio by nearly 30%, and compounding the arithmetic mean overstates the 1960-2020 terminal value by 89% `2405.10920v1` (p.19, p.8).
2. The mean-variance weight vector is only as aligned with expected returns as the covariance conditioning allows; the angle is bounded below through the Kantorovich inequality by the condition number `2107.06194v5` (p.4).
3. Markowitz variance assumes constant trade volume; the coefficient of variation of volume can make the true market-based variance far below or far above it `2507.21824v1` (p.10).
4. A factor model's ranking is not construction-invariant: FF3 wins in six of eight daily constant-weight cells, while FF5/FF6 win most buy-and-hold cells `2606.19550v1` (p.23, p.20).
5. Publication-bias corrections shrink published cross-sectional mean returns by only 10-15%, and the false discovery rate is under 10% `2209.13623v3` (p.1, p.13).
6. Diversification destroys information: when barcodes carry no co-movement information, the portfolio's information content vanishes as the number of names grows `1901.09795v1` (p.4).
7. Black-Litterman cannot interpolate between prior and views by tuning tau; a Wasserstein-barycenter update can, and it rewards confident correct views `2406.01199v1` (p.9, p.19).
8. Skew-normal returns and views give lower volatility at the same target return: 3.552% versus 4.950% for the Gaussian BL at a 1.25% monthly target `2310.12333v1` (p.24).
9. Volatility targeting alone is not enough; a downside-quantile policy earns SR 0.94 with a 21.28% maximum drawdown versus 0.87 and 21.39% unmanaged `2510.19271v2` (p.18).
10. Deep-learning return forecasts turn a statistically insignificant predictor set into large portfolio gains: annual CER roughly doubles from 4.7% to 10% `2009.03394v4` (p.4, p.23).
11. Spanning tests must be run at the asset level; testing on ad-hoc portfolios introduces aggregation bias and changes the conclusion `2403.17127v1` (p.3, p.11).
12. Regime-switching jumps mean the classical "how many names" rule is far too optimistic: 94 (87) components are needed for 95% (90%) of the variance of 100 assets `2204.13398v1` (p.15-16).

## 1. Estimator error, shrinkage and the alpha-weight angle

The cleanest statement of why unconstrained mean-variance is fragile is geometric. Write the optimal frontier portfolio as theta* proportional to Sigma^{-1} alpha, where alpha is the expected-return vector and Sigma the covariance. The angle phi between theta* and alpha measures how far the optimizer has rotated the bets away from the return forecast. Golts and Jones's result, which `2107.06194v5` extends, is that cos(phi) has a lower bound that depends only on the condition number kappa = rho_max/rho_min of Sigma, via the Kantorovich inequality (v'v)^2 / ((v'Tv)(v'T^{-1}v)) >= 4 rho_1 rho_n/(rho_1+rho_n)^2 (p.4). The practical reading is precise: it is not covariance inversion per se that is the error maximiser, it is inversion of a badly conditioned covariance, which pushes the portfolio direction away from the direction of expected returns (p.2). The paper then extends the bound to gearing-constrained optimisations using the Bauer-Householder weakening of Kantorovich and shows the bound is looser once a gearing constraint is imposed, because gearing can drag the portfolio toward the global minimum-variance corner and saturate any information advantage (p.8). It also restates the classical hierarchy: errors in the sample mean matter more out of sample than errors in the sample covariance, so a shrinkage or conditioning step should target the return vector first and the covariance second (p.3). The evidence is theory plus a Gaussian simulation; there is no market calibration, so the bound is a design constraint, not a measured P&L number.

## 2. Covariance and the variance budget: what Markowitz leaves out

Markowitz variance Theta_M = sum_{j,k} theta_{jk} X_j X_k is the textbook quadratic form in fixed weights. `2507.21824v1` re-derives it as a special case: it is the portfolio variance when the volumes of the consecutive trades with every security are assumed constant, i.e. when the coefficient of variation c of trade volume is zero (p.2, p.9). Relaxing that gives a market-based variance Theta(t,t0) whose second-order Taylor expansion in c is Theta_M - 2 a Theta_M^{1/2} R c + [R^2 - Theta_M] c^2, where R is the mean gross return and a is a covariance term between trade values and volumes (p.10). Three limiting cases follow: when return fluctuations are large (y0 near 1) a positive value-volume covariance can make Markowitz vastly overvalue risk; when return fluctuations are small (y0 << 1) but c is large, Markowitz can vastly undervalue risk, growing toward R^2 as c -> 1; and with zero value-volume covariance the variance rises monotonically from Theta_M toward R^2 (p.10-11). The construction is purely analytical with no empirical estimates, so treat it as a warning that a covariance fitted to close-to-close returns is not the covariance of the value process a large order actually experiences, not as a calibrated correction.

The two covariance papers sit at opposite ends of the same problem. `2107.06194v5` says the damage from a bad Sigma is a rotation of the weight vector, which conditioning can control; `2507.21824v1` says the damage is also a scaling of the variance itself, which no conditioning of a return covariance can see. A platform that runs both a return-covariance estimator and a volume or participation model is therefore better placed than one that only estimates Sigma: the first guards the direction, the second guards the magnitude.

## 3. Views and priors: what a Bayesian or geometric update can and cannot do

Black-Litterman mixes a prior drift pi = gamma Sigma w* with views V ~ N(Pm, Omega_v). `2406.01199v1` makes a gedankenexperiment out of the single-asset case: the posterior drift is (sigma_V^2 mu + tau sigma_R^2 nu_V)/(sigma_V^2 + tau sigma_R^2), and because tau is confined to [0,1] for the prior to be reasonable, the posterior can never equal the view distribution even at full confidence; the paper concludes that tau parameterises the error bars of the estimate, not the investor's subjective confidence, and that the same is true of the Meucci variant with no tunable parameter at all (p.9). Its alternative is a generalized Wasserstein barycenter that minimises a Lagrangian combining a prior-distance term and a views-distance term with weight lambda = t/(1-t); the optimal drift is W mu_P + lambda P' nu_V with W = I + lambda P'P, and lambda -> infinity reproduces the views distribution while lambda -> 0 reproduces the prior (p.13-14). On 250 simulated 50-asset paths (T = 4000 daily observations, quarterly rebalancing), with consistently correct views and high confidence (t = 95%), both geometric variants beat the benchmark and both BL variants with the outperformance test statistic above the 3.125 threshold (p = 0.001); with low confidence (t = 5%) on correct views they beat only the benchmark and BL-I (p.19-20). The stage is deliberately back-validated on synthetic data, so the ranking is a property of the update rule, not a market result.

The third-moment extension moves in a different direction. `2310.12333v1` replaces the Gaussian likelihood with a hidden-truncation skew-normal and shows the posterior of the drift is again skew-normal, so the standard mean-variance objective no longer applies and Simaan's two-component variance (spherical plus non-spherical) is needed (p.7-9). On 217 monthly log-returns of 13 US equities from December 2004 to November 2022, a likelihood-ratio test rejects the symmetric skew parameter (log-likelihood 3652.19 normal versus 3668.80 skew-normal, 2 ln LR = 33.212, p = 0.0015) (p.22). At a 1.25% monthly target with zero non-spherical variance the skew-normal optimum has 3.552% volatility against 4.950% for the classical BL, and its Sharpe ratio is 0.027 versus 0.019 (p.24, p.26). The paper also reports that portfolios become more negatively skewed as the target return rises, and that volatility and skewness are negatively related across the frontier (p.24-26). The limits are that this is a single 13-name US sample with hand-specified views and no costs, so the volatility reduction is a construction result.

## 4. Factor models and the construction of the test

`2606.19550v1` asks whether a factor model's measured performance is a property of the model or of the test assets, and finds the latter matters a lot. Using characteristic-unsorted random portfolios drawn from a CRSP universe that keeps 77.6% of common stocks but 99.7% of aggregate market equity (p.9), it varies stock selection, initial weights, breadth and the weight-management rule. The cleanest comparison holds the selected stocks and initial weights fixed and changes only the rebalancing rule: under buy-and-hold, FF5 and FF6 lead in most of the eight selection-weighting cells, whereas under daily constant weighting FF3 has the smallest mean absolute pricing error in six of the eight cells, with q5's errors reaching 490.3 bp in UNIF-EW (p.20, p.23). Spanning tests tell a different story: adding the q5-specific factors to FF6 raises the annualised maximum Sharpe ratio from 1.311 to 2.205 (Delta SR^2 = 3.143), while the reverse raises it from 2.077 to 2.205 (Delta SR^2 = 0.549), driven mainly by the expected-growth factor with an 864.4 bp annualised alpha (p.14-15). The lesson is that spanning and absolute fit are different objectives and can rank models differently. The evidence is a long US sample (14,598 daily observations, 1967-2024) but the models and universes are all US equity.

This connects directly to `2403.17127v1`. Its high-dimensional spanning test avoids estimating a precision matrix by testing each asset's moment condition with a batch-mean Student-t statistic and combining p-values with the Cauchy combination test, which stays valid under arbitrary dependence (p.5-6). Its empirical panel is 306 test assets and 102 benchmark assets over 250 periods, a configuration in which the classical GRS or Huberman-Kandel tests cannot even be applied because N is larger than T - K (p.11). The conclusion it reaches, that the rejection of mean-variance spanning comes from variance reduction inside the domestic global minimum-variance portfolio rather than from a Sharpe-ratio gain (p.1), is exactly the kind of finding that portfolio-level aggregation hides. Together the two papers say that a factor-model verdict is only as trustworthy as the asset-level test that produced it.

## 5. What actually allocates: the cross-section

If a strategy allocates on cross-sectional predictors, the honest question is how much of the published edge survives. `2209.13623v3` reviews three independent meta-studies and reports four facts: nearly all findings replicate, predictability persists out of sample, published t-statistics are far above 2.0, and predictors are weakly correlated. On the Chen-Zimmermann dataset of 207 predictors from 140 papers (p.3), 183 have t > 2.0, 121 have t > 3.0, 74 have t > 4.0 and 26 have t > 6.0 (p.8); the mean, median and modal pairwise correlation is near zero and 60 principal components are needed to span 90% of the variance (p.9). McLean and Pontiff's 97 replicated predictors lose only 26% of their in-sample return immediately after the sample ends (p.7). Empirical-Bayes shrinkage is therefore small: the fitted variance of expected returns implies sigma_theta near 3.0, and with an average standard error of about 20 bp per month this means a true expected return of about 60 bp per month is common, so the average correction is only 10% of in-sample returns and the simple-model false discovery rate is 0.4% (p.13, p.15); across meta-studies the shrinkage range is 8-17% (p.16). The nuance is the decay: returns fall about 25% three years after the sample ends and about 50% a decade later, and because monthly predictor returns have only 7% autocorrelation at one month and none at three, most of that slow decay is a change in expected return rather than publication bias (p.20-21). A single dataset and a single literature (US equity anomalies) is the main limit.

The same question appears from the forecast side in `2009.03394v4`. A two-asset investor (T-bill plus S&P 500 index) with power utility and gamma = 4, using the twelve Welch-Goyal predictors and weights bounded to [-1, 2], gets negative out-of-sample R^2 from every linear regression but positive R^2 from every neural network: NN1 7.1%, NN2 5.1%, NN3 5.6%, LSTM 1.6% at the six-month horizon (p.21). The economic translation is large: annual certainty-equivalent return roughly doubles from about 4.7% to about 10%, and the monthly Sharpe ratio rises from 0.049 to about 0.175 (p.4), with linear models producing no CER improvement over the historical mean (p.23). The setup is deliberately restrictive, a single market and two assets with constant volatility, so the gains are an upper bound on what a time-series timing model can add, not evidence about a multi-asset book.

## 6. Diversification: how much risk and information is removed

Two papers attack the diversification orthodoxy from different sides. `1901.09795v1` quantifies what a portfolio throws away using mutual information. For a one-factor Gaussian return model, the barcode's information about the portfolio exceeds its information about an individual asset only if the barcode is informative about co-movement (c > a); if barcodes carry no co-movement information (c = 0), the portfolio's information content vanishes as n grows, because the average is dominated by the common component the barcodes cannot see (p.4). Securitised tranches are worse: the mutual information of a tranche is more than tenfold smaller than that of the portfolio, and it falls as the tranche gets safer (p.6). The pricing section shows the incentive problem: with c = 0 the barcode's value for the portfolio is less than the sum of the per-asset information costs by (n-1) lambda^2, so securitisation cannot sustain information gathering (p.7-8). This is a Gaussian model with no market data; its contribution is the mechanism, not a number to fit.

`2204.13398v1` revisits the "how many stocks" question with a Markov-modulated Normal-Inverse-Gaussian model calibrated to Nasdaq 100 daily data from 4 January 2000 to 12 November 2021 (5,483 observations) (p.7). The two regimes differ sharply: the scale parameter is about 2.5 times larger and the volatility parameter more than seven times higher in the second regime, with persistence probabilities of 71.6% (regime 1) and 28.3% (regime 2) (p.14). Regime classification is good (RCM 8.63, below the 10 threshold) (p.15). The punchline contradicts the classical rule: 94 (87) principal components are needed to explain at least 95% (90%) of the variability of a 100-asset universe, far more than the 8-25 names often quoted (p.15-16). The limits are that this is one index, an illustrative factor-model portfolio construction, and no out-of-sample performance is reported.

## 7. Return measurement, drawdown-aware allocation and turnover

The measurement section is the one that should change code first. `2405.10920v1` argues from the value process rather than the return process: a buy-and-hold value process is measured correctly only by log returns, because only the geometric mean is path-independent and compounds back to the terminal value. On the S&P 500 from 1 January 1960 to 1 January 2020 (720 months), the difference between arithmetic and geometric monthly means is only 0.0009, but the arithmetic mean inflates the geometric mean by 16%, and compounding it overstates the terminal value by 89% (p.8). The realised monthly returns sum to 261.99 on an initial 55.61, while the true terminal value is 3225.52; the realised cash compounded at 3% reaches 674.17, about 2.6 times the simple sum (p.19). The Sharpe ratio computed from net returns is exaggerated by nearly 30%, which the paper says translates into inflated alphas and t-values in regressions (p.8, p.19). It also argues that zero-cost long-short returns are ill-defined because the initial value is zero, that excess returns underestimate the compounding of market minus risk-free compounded separately (100 growing to 739 versus 182 over 20 years, an understatement of the difference by 45%), and that the 1926 US market cap is under 0.07% of the 2020 figure (p.7, p.27). The paper is US-equity and S&P-500 specific, but the bookkeeping argument is general.

Drawdown-aware allocation gets a concrete out-of-sample test in `2510.19271v2`. The framework replaces expected utility with recursive quantile preferences indexed by tau, estimated by a quantile actor-critic on eleven daily Fama-French and industry factors, with a five-year estimation window and a two-year out-of-sample block and transaction costs (p.14). The estimated policies form an ordered frontier: the tau = 0.1 policy has the lowest volatility (7.59%), the least negative 5% CVaR (-1.13%), the shallowest maximum drawdown (21.28%) and the highest Sharpe ratio (0.94), while tau = 0.9 earns the highest mean (9.46%) with a 33.34% drawdown and SR 0.79; the unmanaged benchmark sits at 7.96% mean, 21.39% drawdown and SR 0.87 (p.18). Relative to a market-volatility-managed benchmark all three policies earn positive annualised alpha (2.77, 2.47 and 2.16 with t-statistics 3.83, 3.34 and 2.54) with betas rising monotonically in tau from 0.49 to 0.83 (p.19). The gains are concentrated in high-volatility states with wide cross-sectional tail dispersion, where a good-tail-minus-bad-tail factor sort earns 1.04 percentage points more (p.2-3). The limit is that the benchmarks and the strategies are all long-only factor portfolios in one developed equity market.

## Caveats

Four of the twelve papers contain no market data at all: `2107.06194v5` proves an angle bound and illustrates it with a Gaussian simulation, `2507.21824v1` is a pure Taylor expansion of a market-based variance with no calibration, `1901.09795v1` works entirely in a Gaussian one-factor model, and `2406.01199v1` evaluates its update rule on synthetic 50-asset paths. Their claims are about construction and mechanism, not about realized P&L. The empirical papers are narrow: `2310.12333v1` is 13 US equities over 217 months, `2009.03394v4` is a single index with two assets, `2204.13398v1` is the Nasdaq 100 only, `2403.17127v1` is one equity panel, and `2209.13623v3` is the US cross-sectional anomaly literature. The slice disagrees with itself in three places: `2209.13623v3` argues publication bias and sampling error are small, while `2107.06194v5` and `2507.21824v1` treat estimation error as first-order; `2204.13398v1` finds far more names are needed than the Alexeev-Tapon literature it criticises; and `2606.19550v1` finds no construction-invariant factor ranking, which sits uneasily with any single-factor-model claim. No paper here establishes that a construction change produces net-of-cost alpha in a live multi-asset book, none covers options, futures, FX or prediction markets, and none of the allocation papers reports a capacity or market-impact limit. `2204.13398v1` and `2406.01199v1` are the only papers that explicitly model non-Gaussian or multi-regime behaviour, and neither reports out-of-sample portfolio performance.
