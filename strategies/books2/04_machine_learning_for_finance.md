# Machine learning for finance: the evaluation design, not the architecture, decides what survives validation

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers one slice of the local arXiv `q-fin.GN` (General Finance) harvest: 156 records from 2008 to 2026 whose title or abstract touches machine learning applied to finance, 18 of them carrying a journal reference. It reads twelve of those papers in depth - credit and default models, fraud and platform-failure detectors, a loss-function experiment, three studies of large language models used as measurement instruments or forecasters, an agent-based LLM market, and a systematic review of deep learning in asset management. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, and specifically for the person who decides how a model is trained, split, scored and promoted. The angle is deliberately not trading signals: the trading corpus already covers leakage and risk-adjusted benchmarks, and this slice supplies the general-finance evidence about measurement, model choice and validation design.

## What this covers

The slice's arc is that general-finance machine learning has moved from "can it predict" to "what exactly did we measure": network and multi-modal credit models push accuracy up by small, well-measured margins (p.22 of `2402.00299v2`), a controlled experiment shows the loss function moves a portfolio's Sharpe ratio more than the model does (p.2 of `2108.02283v7`), and the three language-model papers show the same headline number can be an artefact of how the measurement was taken. The single most consequential finding is that when seven frontier language models score the same earnings-call text on the same rubric, their cross-provider rank correlation averages only 0.52, and transcript-level differences common to all providers explain only 34.4% of the score variation (`2609.31013v1`, p.3 and p.4), so a textual feature can be an interchangeable-looking input while the variable it feeds depends on which model produced it.

## The short answer

1. Small, honestly-measured gains are what credit models deliver: an attention-based dynamic multilayer graph model reaches AUC 0.812 and F1 0.851 against XGBoost's 0.805 and 0.837, a 0.87% and 1.67% improvement (`2402.00299v2`, p.22).
2. More complex deep models do not reliably beat simpler ones: static graph baselines lose to XGBoost (`2402.00299v2`, p.20), and a plain CNN fusion beats ConvLSTM, ConvGRU and BERT on credit-rating fusion (`2304.10740v3`, p.28).
3. Text can dominate numeric inputs for a credit-rating target: the text channel alone scores weighted AUC 0.915 against 0.808 for the best numeric channel, though that target includes the prior rating (`2304.10740v3`, p.31 and p.32).
4. Multi-horizon default probabilities are learnable from one model: a transformer encoder predicts a 3-month to 3-year term structure with a 10-fold mean AUC of 0.869 (`2111.09902v4`, p.27), and fundamental data contributes 30 percentage points on average (`2111.09902v4`, p.28).
5. Exchange accuracy at fraud frequencies is not evidence: a 99.72% accuracy and 0.987 AUC are reported on data where fraudulent transactions are about 0.2% of the set (`2502.15822v1`, p.4 and p.2).
6. Feature-count discipline matters more than model class for platform failure: performance is roughly flat beyond seven variables, and the best model reduces to six variables at AUC 0.969 (`2311.14577v1`, p.6 and p.7).
7. The loss function changes portfolio outcomes as much as the model: a matched classification-versus-regression experiment gives a value-weighted Sharpe of 2.08 for classification against 1.39 for regression (`2108.02283v7`, p.2).
8. Architecture choice explains 99.90% of forecast error variance versus 0.01% for random seed, yet directional accuracy is indistinguishable from 50% in all 54 model-category-horizon combinations (`2603.16886v1`, p.1).
9. Same-model LLM outputs are largely reproducible for simple tasks: binary classification reaches Fleiss' Kappa above 0.93, but five-class classification drops to 55.66% perfect agreement (`2503.16974v4`, p.4 and p.15).
10. Different-provider LLM measures of the same text are not interchangeable: mean pairwise rank correlation is 0.52 and within-call model dispersion is 0.75 times the overall variation the measure is meant to capture (`2609.31013v1`, p.3 and p.16).
11. A language model can recall the outcome rather than reason about the input: a date-only recall query yields a Lookahead Propensity that is positive in-sample and collapses to below 1e-4 after the training cutoff (`2512.23847v2`, p.3 and p.11).
12. LLM trading agents follow their prompts more faithfully than they pursue profit, which is useful for controlled experiments and dangerous if many agents share a foundation model (`2504.10789v1`, p.2 and p.4).

## 1. Credit and default: small gains, measured honestly

The general-finance credit literature is where this platform can see what a defensible evaluation looks like, because the outcome is rare, delayed and audited. `2402.00299v2` builds a dynamic multilayer graph from Freddie Mac single-family loan data, with one layer for geography and one for the lending company, and passes monthly snapshots through a graph attention layer and an LSTM with a temporal attention mechanism. The double-layer network covers 297,040 training nodes and 108 million edges in the first window (p.19); each window holds six one-month snapshots and predicts 12-month default (p.18). The best configuration, GAT-LSTM-ATT on the double-layer network, reaches AUC 0.812 and F1 0.851 versus 0.805 and 0.837 for XGBoost on borrower features (p.22). The authors are explicit that a static graph network is worse than XGBoost (p.20), that attention over snapshots consistently helps (p.21), and that the gain is under 2%. The limit is the sample: loans originated in 2009-2010 with behavioural data from 2012, so the study carries the financial-crisis signature the authors flag as a robustness gap (p.17).

`2111.09902v4` treats default as a multi-label panel-classification problem over US mid-caps from 1990 to 2020, producing a full 3-month to 3-year probability term structure from one transformer encoder. The winning multimodal architecture with differential training reaches AUC 0.847 (p.26) and a stratified 10-fold mean AUC of 0.869 (p.27). Its Shapley group analysis is the operational part: the fundamental channel contributes 30 percentage points on average, and using fundamentals alone gives AUC 0.791 against 0.836 for all channels (p.28); the pricing channel's contribution decays from 16.2% at three months to 5% at three years (p.29). The limitation is a proprietary, non-shareable dataset and a single market.

`2304.10740v3` fuses earnings-call text with bond, market and financial-ratio streams across 27,854 records and rates companies into eight merged classes. Its best model is a hybrid-concatenation CNN-CNN at weighted AUC 0.932 (p.29), and its out-of-time and out-of-universe tests hold at 0.935 and 0.929 (p.31). The headline is that the text channel alone reaches weighted AUC 0.915 versus 0.808 for the best numeric channel (p.31, p.32) - but the covariate channel included the prior rating, so the comparison is not a clean text-versus-numbers test. The same paper finds ConvGRU worse than LSTM and a plain CNN best (p.28), echoing that capacity is not the constraint.

## 2. Fraud and platform failure: what the headline metrics hide

`2502.15822v1` proposes a hybrid of gradient boosting and a simplified random forest and reports accuracy 99.72%, precision 94.83%, recall 93.45%, F1 94.14% and AUC 0.987 against 0.942 for a random forest and 0.961 for gradient boosting (p.4). The setup is a 70/15/15 split of a public credit-card fraud set plus desensitised bank data where fraud is "usually about 0.2%" (p.2). At that base rate, accuracy is close to meaningless: a model that predicts "not fraud" everywhere scores about 99.8% accuracy. The paper does not report precision-recall area or a cost-sensitive threshold, and its reference list does not match its in-text citations, so the benchmark details should be treated as unverified.

`2311.14577v1` is the better template. It predicts failure of 2,438 Chinese peer-to-peer lending platforms from 29 platform characteristics, defines the costly error explicitly - a false positive is "predicted to survive but actually fails", with the investor losing everything (p.4) - and reports the false acceptance rate alongside AUC. Its best model, XGBoost with forward-selected features, achieves AUC 0.969 and FAR 0.065 on six variables (p.6); a five-variable model keeps AUC 0.966 at FAR 0.076 (p.6). The finding worth importing is that AUC is essentially flat once the feature count passes seven while FAR keeps falling slowly, so the trade-off is between interpretability and tail error, not between accuracy and accuracy (p.7). The limits are a single country, a 2014-2018 window, and mostly non-time-varying features.

## 3. The loss function and the target are the real model

`2108.02283v7` runs the cleanest experiment in the slice: matched model pairs that differ only in the loss function, on 102 firm characteristics, from July 1983 to December 2024, giving 498 out-of-sample months (p.9). Classification under categorical cross-entropy delivers a value-weighted Sharpe ratio of 2.08 for the stacking ensemble against 1.39 for the matched regression ensemble, and equal-weighted Sharpe ratios of 3.77 against 3.12 (p.2). Classification wins in 49 of 50 paired comparisons of Sharpe ratio and factor alpha across weighting schemes (p.9). The mechanism is that cross-entropy trains the model to assign the correct return decile while mean squared error trains it to estimate magnitudes, a harder problem; the linear benchmarks isolate this because logistic regression at a value-weighted Sharpe of 1.57 beats ordinary least squares at 1.03 (p.3). The advantage survives 30 basis points per one-way trade plus a 50-basis-point short borrow, narrowing to 1.85 against 1.09 (p.14), and it narrows but persists among large caps (1.30 against 1.25, p.13). The limitation is US equities and a single, well-known characteristic set.

`2603.16886v1` is the cautionary companion. Across 918 runs of nine architectures on twelve instruments in three asset classes, ModernTCN has the best mean rank of 1.333 with a 75% first-place rate over 24 evaluation points and PatchTST is second at 2.000 (p.1). Architecture choice explains 99.90% of raw RMSE variance against 0.01% for seed, so three seeds suffice for ranking (p.1). But directional accuracy is statistically indistinguishable from 50% for every model, category and horizon (p.1), and error grows 2 to 2.5 times from a 4-hour to a 24-hour horizon (p.1). The returns are stationary with excess kurtosis of 15 to 96 (p.11). The operational reading is that MSE-trained price regressors rank prices accurately without being tradeable; the paper's own remedy is to redesign the loss function for direction.

## 4. Language models as measurement instruments are model-contingent

`2503.16974v4` runs 50 independent generations per task over 50 days, producing over 3.4 million outputs from roughly 750,000 API calls (p.4, p.15). Simple tasks are reproducible: binary classification and sentiment analysis reach Fleiss' Kappa above 0.93 (p.4), with GPT-4o-mini at 0.97 for binary and 0.86 for five-class classification while GPT-3.5-turbo is better than GPT-4o-mini on the five-class task at 0.91 (p.15). Perfect agreement falls from 96.18% for binary to 55.66% for five classes (p.15). Summary length varies with a mean absolute relative difference near 8.5% between runs (p.16), which is why aggregating three to five runs is the practical fix. The encouraging result is that length variation does not poison downstream regressions: 97.35% of inferences about length effects are correct, with Type I and Type II errors at 1.28% and 1.38% (p.5). The limit is that this is same-model, repeated-run reproducibility, not cross-provider agreement.

`2609.31013v1` supplies the cross-provider test the previous paper does not. Seven models from seven providers score 1,946 earnings calls from 501 S&P 500 firms (p.9). Mean pairwise rank correlation across thirteen measures is 0.52; sentiment is the only construct with substantial agreement at 0.85, while a rule-based specificity ratio reaches only 0.23 and integrity 0.32 (p.3). The average within-call standard deviation across providers is 0.75 times the total cross-call variation - so for the typical construct, which model scored the text matters nearly as much as which firm was scored (p.3, p.16). A variance-components analysis splits score variation into 34.4% transcript-level, 33.4% provider-level and 32.2% provider-by-transcript (p.4). Aggregating across providers raises relative-ranking reliability to the conventional 0.80 threshold for ten of thirteen measures but absolute-level dependability for only five (p.5). Cross-model disagreement also does not track analyst or market disagreement (p.4), and the ensemble-LLM sentiment score correlates only 0.29 with dictionary tone (p.17). This is measurement-only evidence: no paper here shows the models are wrong, only that they are not interchangeable.

## 5. Lookahead and memorisation in language-model forecasts

`2512.23847v2` turns the leakage worry into a portable diagnostic. It sends the model a date-only recall query containing a firm name, ticker and date and nothing else, and reads the first-token probabilities of "up", "down" and "unknown"; Lookahead Propensity is the total probability on the two directional labels (p.3). LAP is materially positive throughout the in-sample window and collapses to essentially zero after the model's December 2023 training cutoff (p.3); across 7,806 post-cutoff firm-day queries the maximum LAP is below 1e-4 (p.11). The worked example is the Kodak loan announcement, where a query with no headline yields P(up) of about 0.9999 on a +318% day (p.3). Applying the test, the in-sample headline signal predicts a 0.21% higher next-day return, and a one-standard-deviation rise in LAP amplifies the signal by 0.067 percentage points, about 32% of the standalone effect, with the interaction insignificant post-cutoff (p.4). The recall-only directional signal predicts returns with a t-statistic of 3.53 in-sample (p.19). The lesson is not that LLM forecasts are worthless but that predictive content is task-specific and can load on memory; the paper's own limit is that it identifies the sign of contamination, not its magnitude.

## 6. Language-model agents as a controlled market substrate

`2504.10789v1` implements a continuous double-auction market with a persistent order book, market and limit orders, partial fills, stochastic dividends and a fundamental value, populated by GPT-4o agents that submit structured decisions and natural-language reasoning (p.1). The baseline runs 15 to 20 rounds with eight agents, 1,000,000 units of cash and 10,000 shares each, a fundamental value of 28, a dividend of 1.40 plus or minus 1.00 at 50% probability, and 5% interest per round (p.29, p.30). The central behavioural result is that agents follow their system prompt faithfully regardless of profit implications, so an LLM trader is an instruction-follower rather than a profit maximiser (p.2). The framework varies the price-to-fundamental ratio from 0.1 to 3.5 to trace decision boundaries, mirroring partial dependence plots (p.22), and the paper flags that if many agents share one foundation model their responses correlate, which is a systemic-risk channel distinct from any single strategy (p.4). The limitation is a stylised environment with zero transaction costs (p.29) and no live-market validation.

## 7. The survey evidence on where deep learning sits

`2503.01591v1` screens 934 Scopus articles from 2018 to 2023 and retains 612 with a genuine deep-learning application to financial asset management (p.2, p.12). The distribution is lopsided: price forecasting accounts for 449 of 612, portfolio management 98, algorithmic trading 48 and value/factor investing 17 (p.15). Electricity price forecasting alone is 43% of the commodity-price articles (p.16), so the aggregate counts overstate how much of the field is trading-relevant. The review's recurring caution is the one this brief keeps meeting: the literature needs robust validation, out-of-sample discipline and interpretability, and the venues are predominantly computer-science journals (p.3, p.13). The limit is a single database and a keyword filter, so the census is indicative rather than exhaustive.

## Caveats

Several papers here have no usable public data: `2111.09902v4` uses proprietary mid-cap data, `2304.10740v3` uses licensed WRDS streams with only a synthetic release, and `2502.15822v1`'s dataset is public but its benchmark reporting is thin and its reference list does not match its citations, so its 99.72% accuracy should not be relied on. The credit and failure papers are single-market and single-period - Freddie Mac loans from 2009-2010, US mid-caps 1990-2020, Chinese P2P platforms 2014-2018 - so none establishes that the same feature sets transfer across the markets this platform trades. The language-model results are snapshots of specific checkpoints: cross-provider divergence is measured on seven cost-efficient models as of late 2025, and lookahead is measured on one Llama-3.3-70B checkpoint with a December 2023 cutoff, so the numbers will move as models are replaced. The slice disagrees with itself about complexity: `2304.10740v3` finds a plain CNN best while `2111.09902v4` finds a transformer best, and `2603.16886v1` shows architecture dominating variance while directional skill stays at chance - a reminder that "better model" and "better decision" are different claims. No paper here establishes live profitability, deployable latency, or that any language-model-derived feature survives transaction costs at this platform's horizons.

## Papers read in depth

- `2402.00299v2` - Attention-based Dynamic Multilayer Graph Neural Networks for Loan Default Prediction (2024). Builds borrower networks over geography and lender; the GAT-LSTM-attention model gains under 2% AUC over XGBoost, and the sample is one crisis-adjacent origination cohort.
- `2111.09902v4` - A transformer-based model for default prediction in mid-cap corporate markets (2021, EJOR). Produces a 3-month to 3-year PD term structure from multimodal panel data; strong externally-validated AUC but proprietary data and one market.
- `2304.10740v3` - Multi-Modal Deep Learning for Credit Rating Prediction Using Text and Numerical Data Streams (2023, Applied Soft Computing). Shows text can dominate numeric channels for credit ratings and that simpler CNNs beat recurrent and BERT fusions; the covariate channel leaks the prior rating.
- `2502.15822v1` - Financial fraud detection system based on improved random forest and gradient boosting machine (2025). Reports near-perfect accuracy and AUC on a 0.2%-fraud set; the metrics are base-rate-blind and the benchmark reporting is not verifiable.
- `2311.14577v1` - Predicting Failure of P2P Lending Platforms through Machine Learning: The Case in China (2023, Finance Research Letters). Best template for cost-asymmetric evaluation: AUC 0.969 at false-acceptance 0.065 on six features; single country and period.
- `2108.02283v7` - Machine Learning Classification and Portfolio Construction: Does the Loss Function Matter? (2021 / Financial Analysts Journal). Matched classification-versus-regression experiment isolating the loss function; US equities only.
- `2603.16886v1` - A Controlled Comparison of Deep Learning Architectures for Multi-Horizon Financial Forecasting (2026). Nine architectures, 918 runs, architecture explains 99.90% of error variance while directional accuracy sits at chance; hourly, three asset classes.
- `2503.16974v4` - Assessing Consistency and Reproducibility in the Outputs of Large Language Models (2025). Fifty runs across five finance tasks; same-model reproducibility is high for simple tasks and weaker for complex ones.
- `2609.31013v1` - Same Text, Different Numbers: The Divergence of LLM-Based Measures (2026). Seven providers score the same calls and disagree; measurement-only, so validity is left open.
- `2512.23847v2` - Detecting Lookahead Bias in LLM Forecasts (2025). Date-only recall query yields a Lookahead Propensity that collapses after the training cutoff; identifies contamination sign, not magnitude.
- `2504.10789v1` - Can Large Language Models Trade? Testing Financial Theories with LLM Agents in Market Simulations (2025). Open-source double-auction with GPT-4o agents; stylised, zero-cost environment with no live validation.
- `2503.01591v1` - The Role of Deep Learning in Financial Asset Management: A Systematic Review (2025). PRISMA review of 612 articles; single-database census dominated by price forecasting and electricity markets.
