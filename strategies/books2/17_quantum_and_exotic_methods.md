# Quantum and other exotic methods: a quantum annealer matches classical portfolio search while a quantum walk earns its keep as a return-distribution model

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers the quantum and exotic-methods slice of a local harvest of the arXiv q-fin.GN (General Finance) category: 3095 records spanning 1997-2026, of which 55 records (2003-2025) match this topic and 24 of those carry a journal reference. Forty candidates were screened and eight were read from the PDFs. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, and it asks a blunt question of every paper: is there an algorithm here that a trading system could run, or is the physics a metaphor draped over a model that classical statistics already covers? The answer splits cleanly. Quantum hardware buys nothing today that a workstation does not already have, and the two formal "quantum finance" branches are metaphor, but two lines of work - quantum walks as a generative model of return distributions, and topological data analysis as a way of seeing shape in a feature cloud - are runnable now and change how a strategy is evaluated.

## What this covers

The slice runs from 2003, through a dense 2010-2015 band of quantum-mechanical price models, to a 2020-2025 group that finally executes code on real hardware or real market data. Its single most consequential finding is negative and unusually well documented: on a 40-stock and then a 60-stock portfolio-selection problem, a D-Wave 2000Q annealer found the same "ideal" portfolio as five classical methods that each finished in under a minute, and the annealer's own authors write that "at lower asset levels there are efficient classical algorithms that do better" (`2007.01430v1`, p.13). The positive finding is smaller but real: a discrete-time quantum walk reproduces the asymmetry and bimodality of long-horizon return distributions that a Gaussian cannot, at the cost of four fitted parameters and a model with no closed form (`2505.13019v2`, p.19). Between those two poles sit a genuine NP-hardness reduction for financial-network crashes that has only a ten-node toy as evidence, an honest survey of the field's readiness, and one topological method that is simply useful.

## The short answer

1. Quantum annealing is a heuristic sampler, not an advantage, for portfolio selection: the D-Wave 2000Q matched five classical methods on 60 US equities and was beaten by a genetic algorithm on 40 (`2007.01430v1` p.13, `2008.08669v1` p.15-16).
2. The binding constraint on that work was the QUBO, not the qubits: at 59 assets the quadratic terms spanned 0.0037-0.0045 while the linear terms spanned -1 to 0.2, and 60 stocks consumed 1,300-1,700 of the chip's 2,048 qubits (`2008.08669v1` p.9, p.16).
3. Real covariance matrices carry slightly negative eigenvalues of order 1e-8, enough to inflate a variance-minimising score by several orders of magnitude, so positive-semidefinite repair is mandatory before any binary optimisation (`2007.01430v1` p.11).
4. Forecasting a financial-network failure cascade is NP-hard, the reduction to a QUBO is honest - the qubit count is n(2q+1) plus ancillas - but the only evidence is a 10-institution toy whose crash transition sits between 55 and 62 in perturbation amplitude (`1810.07690v2` p.1, p.4, p.5).
5. The field's own survey of 13 peer-reviewed finance applications finds none using machine learning, no rigorous end-to-end speed-up claim, and an industry estimate of at least five years before significant impact (`2204.10026v1` p.4, p.7-8).
6. The quantum speed-ups that would matter here - linear-algebra and optimisation speed-ups - need deep circuits and quantum random access memory, which the same survey calls difficult to construct (`2204.10026v1` p.8).
7. A discrete-time quantum walk is a four-parameter generative model that reproduces asymmetric return distributions better than a Gaussian by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic, and bimodal ones better by shape (`2505.13019v2` p.19).
8. A two-component Gaussian mixture fits the bimodal cases better than the quantum walk does, so the walk's value is dynamic interpretability rather than raw goodness of fit (`2505.13019v2` p.19).
9. Long-horizon volatility scaling exponents clustered at 0.43-0.54 across 31 assets, close to but not equal to the square-root-of-time 0.5 (`2505.13019v2` p.11-12).
10. Ball mapper is the one non-quantum exotic method here that is runnable today, cheap, and ships its own stability protocol - multiple radii plus permutation checks - and it locates firm failure in a small region of Altman's five-ratio space (`2206.03622v1` p.9-10, p.39).
11. Gauge-theoretic quantum finance yields algebraic constraints, including a volatility-correlation parameter pinned to +/-1, rather than any prediction or measurement (`2306.03237v1` p.3).
12. No paper in this slice shows a quantum algorithm beating a well-tuned classical method on a trading-relevant objective.

## 1. Quantum annealing for portfolio selection: a match, not an advantage

The two Chicago Quantum papers are the most concrete artefacts in the slice, because they ran on real hardware and benchmarked against real classical alternatives. The 40-stock study selects a subset of US liquid equities, a binary problem with 2^40, or 1.1 trillion, possible portfolios, on a D-Wave 2000Q with 2,041 qubits (`2007.01430v1`, p.13). Because the Sharpe ratio is a ratio of a linear form to a square root of a quadratic form, it cannot be handed to the annealer directly, so the authors substitute a quadratic score, the Chicago Quantum Net Score, defined as the portfolio variance minus the squared expected return (`2007.01430v1`, p.3, p.6).

The result is a tie that the paper reports honestly. The annealer beat classical random sampling on average for portfolio sizes 3 to 25, 27, 28 and 33, but underperformed the genetic algorithms, and the authors note that a single equivalent portfolio was found by sampling 500 million portfolios over several hours while classical heuristics reached the optimum in seconds (`2007.01430v1`, p.10, p.13). The 60-stock follow-up is more explicit still: five classical methods - fat-tailed Monte Carlo, a genetic algorithm, a simulated annealer, a Tabu sampler and the D-Wave hybrid solver - each find the ideal portfolio in under a minute, the D-Wave run accumulated 21 seconds of account time, the Tabu sampler needed 267 seconds and was worse by a factor of ten or more, and the hybrid sampler returned no portfolio of the requested size at all (`2008.08669v1`, p.2, p.4, p.6, p.7).

Read as engineering rather than physics, the conclusion is not that quantum annealing fails; it is that an annealer is one more stochastic sampler in the same family as simulated annealing, and must be benchmarked as such. The 60-stock paper's own summary - that the classical methods validate the quantum results - is the correct framing for a platform: the exotic solver is an additional seed generator, and its value has to be measured against the classical baseline on the same objective function, not against brute force.

## 2. The cost that actually bites is the QUBO, not the qubits

Both annealer papers spend most of their length on encoding rather than on quantum mechanics, and that is the transferable lesson. In the 60-stock study, embedding 60 assets consumes 1,300 to 1,700 of the 2,048 available qubits, and a linear extrapolation leaves room for roughly 20 percent more, or about 72 stocks (`2008.08669v1`, p.16). More damaging than qubit count is dynamic range: at 59 assets the linear terms span -1 to 0.2 while the quadratic terms span only 0.0037 to 0.0045, because covariance shrinks as the asset count grows (`2008.08669v1`, p.9). Chain strength and annealing time have to be tuned against each other, with values above 2.0 producing no valid results at all (`2008.08669v1`, p.11).

The 40-stock paper documents a failure mode that has nothing to do with hardware. Covariance matrices computed from real returns came out with slightly negative eigenvalues of order 1e-8; a variance-minimising objective could then find a "minimum risk" portfolio with negative variance, after which the computed Sharpe ratio would be several orders of magnitude too large (`2007.01430v1`, p.11). Their fix is a Cholesky test followed by eigendecomposition with eigenvalues below a threshold set to zero. The same paper notes that moving from a binary include-or-exclude formulation to a discrete weight grid of 0.01 steps multiplies the search space by roughly seven orders of magnitude and reduces tractability to about 32 assets (`2007.01430v1`, p.12).

The practical rule that falls out is that any binary-optimisation experiment on this platform should log the linear and quadratic term scales, normalise them deliberately, and validate the objective's inputs for positive semidefiniteness before the solver is invoked. These are exactly the checks that a badly instrumented exotic experiment omits, and they are what the annealer literature learned the hard way.

## 3. Financial-network crashes as a QUBO: a real reduction, a toy test

The crash-forecasting paper is the clearest example in the slice of a genuine algorithmic contribution that is not yet usable. It starts from the fact that determining the maximum number of institutional failures after a shock to a network of cross-holdings is NP-hard, and that with complete knowledge of a network of 30 institutions the exact computation would take longer than the age of the universe, which the authors put at 13.7 billion years (`1810.07690v2`, p.1). It then recasts the equilibrium condition as a variational problem, encodes market values as binary strings, approximates the discontinuous failure term by a Fourier-Legendre polynomial of degree r, and reduces the resulting many-qubit Hamiltonian to two-qubit interactions using k ancilla qubits per k-body term (`1810.07690v2`, p.2, p.3, p.4).

The resource estimate is explicit and unflattering: the logical qubit cost is n(2q+1) for n institutions at q bits of precision, plus an ancilla term that grows polynomially with degree controlled by r (`1810.07690v2`, p.4). The numerical experiment is a randomly generated network of 10 institutions and 20 assets with prices from 0 to 100, perturbed by a vector of amplitude up to 80; the crash appears as a phase transition when the perturbation amplitude reaches about 60, with the transition region spanning roughly 55 to 62 (`1810.07690v2`, p.5). The authors note that finite-size scaling should sharpen that transition as the number of institutions grows, which makes prediction harder rather than easier, and they close by saying that more complex networks require resources not considered and that the qubit count grows quickly enough to hinder practical application (`1810.07690v2`, p.5, p.6).

For a risk engine the honest reading is that this is a small, exact stress test of a toy network that happens to be expressible as a QUBO, not a systemic-risk forecasting tool. Ten nodes and a hand-built random cross-holding matrix are nowhere near a real interbank or stablecoin network, and no calibration to observed failure data is attempted.

## 4. What the field says about its own readiness

The structured survey is the most useful paper in the slice precisely because it is not selling anything. It filters the literature down to 13 peer-reviewed papers that describe a concrete financial application implemented by 2021, and then reads them through a four-layer framework of use case, methodology, quantum algorithm and hardware (`2204.10026v1`, p.4). Five of the 13 address portfolio optimisation and three address derivative pricing; eight use optimisation, six use Monte Carlo simulation, and none use machine learning (`2204.10026v1`, p.7). Four run on quantum annealers and nine on gate-based machines (`2204.10026v1`, p.9).

The candid statements are worth quoting because they are rare. The authors write that to their knowledge "there is no rigorous end-to-end consideration to paint the full picture" (`2204.10026v1`, p.6), that gate-based optimisation algorithms "require large depth circuits and remain beyond the reach of near and intermediate term devices", and that quantum machine learning algorithms impose additional requirements such as quantum random access memory, "which could be difficult to construct" (`2204.10026v1`, p.8). They also record that classical approximation techniques for the constraint-satisfaction problems where QAOA is typically applied have improved in the meantime, so "the exact quantum advantage for these problems therefore remains an open research question" (`2204.10026v1`, p.7).

On the specific proposals, the survey's reading is that a quantum annealer approached commercial-solver performance on the Nikkei 225 and S&P 500 examples, that reverse annealing was more than 100 times faster than forward annealing but with no known classical comparison, and that the gate-based speed-ups depend on problem-dependent parameters or on ideal simulators (`2204.10026v1`, p.9, p.10). The industry timeline quoted is at least five years to significant impact, with no estimate of what running the hardware costs (`2204.10026v1`, p.4). A platform's research notes should carry that as the prior, and treat any vendor claim of quantum advantage as unvalidated until a classical baseline is reported on the same instance.

## 5. Quantum walks as generative models of return distributions

This is where the slice turns from physics to statistics. The first paper replaces the Wiener process in geometric Brownian motion with a discrete-time quantum walk, whose position variance grows quadratically with the number of steps, a behaviour the authors call ballistic diffusion, against the linear growth of a classical random walk (`2403.19502v2`, p.7, p.8). A freely chosen function f(t) proportional to t to the power -1/2 restores classical diffusive scaling, and the paper is explicit that using a quantum model does not imply that markets are quantum systems (`2403.19502v2`, p.2, p.7).

The knobs are interpretable. The coin's Cayley-Klein parameters and the initial condition control the skew and the dispersion of the resulting distribution, the Hadamard coin at theta equal to pi/4 maximises the Shannon entropy of the outcome, and decoherence - modelled as randomly broken links in the walk lattice with probability p - collapses the distribution to the classical one at p equal to 0.5 while at p equal to 0.1 or 0.3 it remains non-Gaussian with fat tails (`2403.19502v2`, p.11, p.12, p.16). That decoherence knob is the interesting one for a trading platform: it is a single parameter interpolating between a fat-tailed quantum distribution and a Gaussian one, and the paper's own reading is that it models the effect of disruptive information in a market that is otherwise coherent.

The second paper does the empirical work. It analyses 31 assets over up to 30 calendar years and shows that for return horizons beyond about 100 trading days the distributions deviate from Gaussian, becoming strongly asymmetric or bimodal at 504 trading days, which is two trading years (`2505.13019v2`, p.6, p.7). It defines a bimodality measure on a 0 to 1 scale, fits the quantum walk with four parameters at a fixed step count of 100 using hill climbing on mean absolute error, and reports that the walk's Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic is lower than the Gaussian's for every asymmetric case and its mean absolute error lower except for two assets (`2505.13019v2`, p.7, p.13, p.19).

The limits are stated as clearly as the results. For the bimodal cases a two-component Gaussian mixture achieves both a lower mean absolute error and a lower KS statistic, so the walk's advantage is that its parameters are part of a generative dynamic rather than purely morphological (`2505.13019v2`, p.19). The authors also say the bimodality most plausibly reflects non-stationarity and the mixing of regimes such as bull and bear markets rather than any quantum interference in markets (`2505.13019v2`, p.20). There is no out-of-sample forecast evaluation and no trading signal anywhere in either paper: this is a distributional model, and its use is in how a platform describes and prices horizon risk, not in when to buy.

## 6. Topological data analysis: an exotic method that survives contact with data

Ball mapper is not quantum at all, and it is the most immediately usable item in the slice. It builds a graph from a point cloud by covering the cloud with balls of a single fixed radius, making each ball a vertex, and drawing an edge wherever two balls intersect; vertices are sized by the number of points they contain and coloured by any function of those points, such as a forward return (`2206.03622v1`, p.7, p.8). The only user choice is the radius, which is also the weakness: the paper states plainly that there is no algorithm for the optimal radius and that reporting several radii is good practice (`2206.03622v1`, p.9, p.44).

The paper also supplies the validation protocol that most nonlinear feature work on this platform would otherwise lack. It recommends normalising all axes, sweeping radii, checking that the mean of the colouring function within a ball exceeds its standard deviation before trusting the colour, and re-running on permuted input points so that only inference stable across permutations is believed; its own confidence intervals come from 10,000 repetitions (`2206.03622v1`, p.9, p.10, p.13). The random landmark selection in the cover construction is exactly why the permutation check is not optional.

The two applications are more illustrative than conclusive but they are concrete. In the first, a cloud of 3,605 firm-years described by Altman's five financial ratios for 2015 with a failure dummy for 2016 shows failing firms occupying a very small subspace; at radius 0.4 one ball had 20 percent of its constituent firms failing, and the ball count falls from 926 at radius 0.1 to under four at radius 1 (`2206.03622v1`, p.35, p.36, p.39). In the second, an eight-dimensional cloud of rates, spreads, dividend and earnings yields and realised volatility from January 1960 to September 2020, 693 months in which the S&P 500 beat the three-month bill in 57 percent of periods, produces a map in which the high end of the shape shows 70 percent or more up-months and the lowest region about 20 percent, with no ball ever all-up or all-down (`2206.03622v1`, p.39, p.40, p.42). Two variables in that cloud correlate at 0.882 and two others at 0.923, which the authors note is problematic for a regression but not for ball mapper (`2206.03622v1`, p.39) - a useful reminder that shape methods tolerate collinearity that breaks linear models.

## 7. Metaphor versus mechanism: the gauge-theory branch

The gauge-theory paper is the cleanest example of the metaphor branch, and reading it settles the question. It works in the Hamiltonian formulation of the Black-Scholes equation, imposes invariance under local transformations of the stock price, and shows that restoring that invariance requires an additional field, which it identifies with stochastic volatility, so that the Merton-Garman equation emerges from Black-Scholes (`2306.03237v1`, p.1, p.3). A Higgs-like mechanism then gives the volatility field a mass (`2306.03237v1`, p.4, p.5).

The concrete output is a set of algebraic conditions on parameters, not a measurement. The gauge connection forces the volatility-correlation parameter to its extreme values of +/-1, whereas the standard Merton-Garman analysis allows the whole interval from -1 to 1 (`2306.03237v1`, p.3), and combining the martingale condition with those constraints gives a volatility level equal to minus the interest rate, which the paper itself notes implies a negative interest rate for market equilibrium (`2306.03237v1`, p.4). There is no data, no calibration, no out-of-sample test and no market-facing claim anywhere in the paper.

The contrast with the quantum-walk papers is instructive, because both carry the word quantum. The walk papers fit 31 real assets and report an error statistic against a Gaussian baseline; the gauge paper derives conditions that no dataset is ever asked to satisfy. That difference, and not the physics, is what determines whether a trading platform can use a result. The metaphor branch is worth recording in research notes as a formal equivalence between two volatility models, and nothing more.

## 8. What an operator should do with this slice

The actionable content of the slice is narrow and specific. Classical search should remain the reference for portfolio selection, and if an annealer or a quantum-inspired solver is tried, it should be benchmarked against simulated annealing and a genetic algorithm on the same objective, with the QUBO's term scales and the positive-semidefiniteness of its covariance input logged per run. Crash-cascade stress tests should stay with the small systems where they are exact. Nothing in the slice justifies buying quantum access.

The two live threads are distributional and diagnostic. Long-horizon return distributions are not Gaussian, and the quantum walk gives a compact four-parameter generative family that reproduces that, with the honest caveat that a Gaussian mixture fits the shape better and a walk is chosen only when a dynamic interpretation is wanted. Ball mapper gives an exploratory map of a feature cloud with a built-in stability protocol, which is exactly the kind of pre-modelling check that catches the failure mode where a nonlinear model is fitted to structure that does not survive permutation of the data.

The one thing the slice never provides is a trading edge. Every paper here that measured something measured either a computational resource, a distributional fit, or a map; none measured profit, and the annealer papers explicitly framed their results as buy-and-hold portfolio selection rather than anything high-frequency. This slice should be treated as a source of measurement and representation tools, and as a warning about how much work a solvable-looking exotic formulation hides.

## Caveats

Nothing in this slice establishes a tradable edge, and the hardware-facing papers are the weakest evidence in it. The two annealer studies use one year of daily data on 40 and 60 US liquid equities in a single rising market, they select portfolios by a bespoke quadratic score rather than by Sharpe ratio, and they are buy-and-hold exercises with no transaction costs, no out-of-sample period and no capacity analysis; the crash-forecasting result is a randomly generated ten-institution, twenty-asset network with hand-set thresholds and no calibration to any observed failure; the gauge-theory paper has no data at all. The slice also disagrees with itself in two places: the second quantum-walk paper reports that a Gaussian mixture beats the walk on the bimodal cases it was written to explain, and the survey records that classical approximation algorithms have improved for the problems where QAOA is usually proposed, which undercuts the annealing speed-up claims rather than supporting them. The quantum-walk results are phenomenological in-sample fits with no forecast evaluation, so they say what a distribution looks like and not what it will do next, and the ball-mapper applications are demonstrations rather than validated predictors. What no paper here establishes is the thing a platform most wants: that any of these methods beats a well-tuned classical alternative on a cost-adjusted trading objective.

## Papers read in depth

- `2204.10026v1` - A Structured Survey of Quantum Computing for the Financial Industry (2022). Reads 13 implemented finance applications through a four-layer framework and is candid about missing speed-up claims and hardware limits; its limitation is that it stops at 2021 and covers only peer-reviewed, implemented work.
- `1810.07690v2` - Forecasting financial crashes with quantum computing (2018). Reduces an NP-hard network-equilibrium problem to a QUBO with an explicit qubit count; its limitation is that the only experiment is a ten-institution toy with no empirical calibration.
- `2007.01430v1` - Portfolio Optimization of 40 Stocks Using the D-Wave Quantum Annealer (2020). Benchmarks a quantum annealer against brute force, random sampling, a genetic algorithm and a simulated annealer, and documents covariance positive-semidefiniteness failure; its limitation is one year of data on one market with no costs.
- `2008.08669v1` - Portfolio Optimization of 60 Stocks Using Classical and Quantum Algorithms (2020). Extends the same exercise to 60 assets and documents embedding, chain-strength and dynamic-range limits; its limitation is that five classical methods finish in under a minute while the quantum run adds nothing but a seed.
- `2403.19502v2` - On the potential of quantum walks for modeling financial return distributions (2024). Introduces the discrete-time quantum walk as a replacement for the Wiener process with interpretable skew, dispersion and decoherence parameters; its limitation is that it is a theoretical study with no empirical fit.
- `2505.13019v2` - Characterizing asymmetric and bimodal long-term financial return distributions through quantum walks (2025). Fits 31 assets over two-year horizons and reports error statistics against a Gaussian and a mixture baseline; its limitation is that it is an in-sample morphological fit with no forecasting test and a mixture that fits better.
- `2206.03622v1` - Topological Data Analysis Ball Mapper for Finance (2022). Documents a shape-based visualisation with a stability protocol and two finance applications including firm failure; its limitation is that no optimal radius exists and the applications are illustrative rather than predictive.
- `2306.03237v1` - Gauge symmetries and the Higgs mechanism in Quantum Finance (2023). Shows the Merton-Garman equation emerging from Black-Scholes under local gauge invariance and gives volatility a Higgs-like mass; its limitation is that it is a formal derivation with no data and constraints such as a correlation pinned to +/-1.
