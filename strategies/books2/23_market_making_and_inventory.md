# Market making, spreads and inventory: dealer pricing power sets the spread and the yield, and it acts through quantities rather than prices

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads the general-finance (q-fin.GN) material on market making, spreads and inventory: 22 harvested records, 2006-2026, of which 8 carry a journal reference. Its angle is the market structure underneath the quoting formulas - who quotes, who pays, what a quote responds to, and what liquidity costs when nobody is obliged to absorb flow. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets. The companion brief `strategies/books/04_market_making_and_inventory.md` already covers the Avellaneda-Stoikov and Cartea-Jaimungal control lineage from the trading corpus; nothing here restates that, and no id from that harvest appears below.

## What this covers

The slice spans three market forms that share one question. First, discretionary dealer markets, where a small set of intermediaries price bilateral flow: UK gilt repo (`2603.10690v1`), OTC currency (`1601.00085v2`), and the abstract competitive dealer market of `1807.08278v3`. Second, automated venues where the "market maker" is a formula or a policy net: a Hawkes-driven limit order book (`2207.09951v1`), a constant-product pool (`2302.05219v2`), and Uniswap v3 liquidity positions treated as high-frequency market makers (`2509.02941v1`). Third, the historical and cross-market evidence on what a dealer franchise is worth and how a spread responds to an outside shock (`1910.13882v1`, `2601.08263v3`). The single most consequential finding is that funding-market intermediation structure, not inventory skew, is the largest measured spread and price distortion in the slice: dealer market power and dealer-network shock transmission together account for between 2.5 and 5.3 percentage points of gilt yield deviation from a smooth curve (`2603.10690v1`, p.3), an order of magnitude larger than any quoting-rule effect reported anywhere else here.

## The short answer

1. Dealer pricing power in gilt repo explains 0.5 to 1.3 percentage points of bond yield deviation, and heterogeneity plus network transmission adds another 2 to 4 points, for 2.5 to 5.3 in total (`2603.10690v1`, pp.1, 3).
2. That power acts through prices, not quantities: relationship depth predicts repo volumes with high significance, but the estimated spread effects of relationship trading are not significant (`2603.10690v1`, p.25).
3. Quoted width in OTC currency making was profitably driven by three observable factors - conditional volatility, trade count and volume - consolidated into one rolling spread factor with equal weights (`1601.00085v2`, pp.9-11).
4. Dealer-market price impact is transient, not permanent, because the interdealer market lets dealers externalise what they internalise, and price deviation decays as that market becomes more liquid (`1807.08278v3`, pp.3-4).
5. Liquidity cost scales with the regularity of client flow: smooth flow costs order theta, diffusive flow costs order sqrt(theta) and depends on its own quadratic variation (`1807.08278v3`, pp.10-11).
6. A learned quoting policy penalised on absolute inventory, not squared inventory, holds a mean absolute position of 0.42 against 1.47 for a symmetric benchmark, at a 1.62 Sharpe (`2207.09951v1`, p.5).
7. Quoting is tolerant of fees only up to a measurable threshold: the learned policy survives 0.6% limit-order fees, a linear-inventory rule turns negative at 0.4% and best-bid-offer quoting at 0.2% (`2207.09951v1`, p.6).
8. Passive liquidity provision is size-selective: over 30-day holds in the largest pools, the fraction of profitable observations was 0.00 for small positions, 0.05 for medium and 0.49 for large (`2302.05219v2`, p.6).
9. The loss from passive provision decomposes into a non-negative fee effect and a divergence loss that is a deterministic function of the price-ratio change, so a frontier separates profitable from unprofitable allocations (`2302.05219v2`, pp.2-3).
10. High-frequency crypto liquidity dynamics are non-quadratic and regime-dependent: the same pool produced a double-well potential at two-month windows and a single well at one-month windows (`2509.02941v1`, pp.5, 8).
11. Dealer concentration and client reciprocity, not price alone, built the junk-bond franchise: Drexel took 60% of its 1985 profit from junk underwriting at 3-4% fees (`1910.13882v1`, p.7).
12. A cross-market shock can narrow a spread: the 3-month AA commercial paper spread fell about 3.0 basis points on the day of a major DeFi exploit (`2601.08263v3`, p.25).

## 1. Spread determination: repo dealer pricing power, measured in percentage points of yield

`2603.10690v1` uses proprietary transaction-level data on all UK repo and reverse-repo trades collateralised by gilts, January 2016 to January 2022, covering dealer-to-non-dealer and dealer-to-CCP trades (p.8). Dealers supply liquidity against collateral in the repo segment and absorb it in the reverse segment, and the paper recovers a dealer-level markup, (r - c)/r = 1/eta, and markdown, 1/nu, from a structural demand-and-supply model with granular instrumental variables (pp.14, 21). Mispricing is the absolute deviation of each gilt's yield from a daily spline benchmark (p.9).

The magnitudes are large by the standards of any quoting study. Frictions from markups and markdowns explain 0.5 to 1.3 percentage points of yield deviation (p.1); dispersion across dealers and the transmission of heterogeneously persistent dealer shocks through the network explain a further 2 to 4 points (p.1); combined, 2.5 to 5.3 points (p.3), and strongest at the long end (p.3). At the transaction level, a 1% higher market power raises the repo segment spread by 0.039 percentage points, about half its standard deviation, but raises the reverse-repo spread by 0.53 points against a standard deviation of 0.08 (p.25). At the bond level the volume-weighted average markup moves mispricing by 0.52 points and markdown by 0.64 points, and a 1% increase in markdown dispersion moves it by 1.3 points (p.28). By maturity, a 1% markdown increase maps to about 1 percentage point of mispricing at the short end and 0.5 for the rest of the curve, while markup matters only for medium-term gilts at 1.2 points (p.29); long-term mispricing is driven by markdown dispersion, and once that dispersion exceeds one standard deviation long-term mispricing "exceeds several percentage points" (p.30). Reverse-repo shocks add about 1 point transitorily and 4 points persistently, while persistent repo shocks reduce mispricing by about 3 points (p.32).

Two structural facts deserve separate mention because they contradict the usual retail-dealer intuition. There are 15 dealers and 3,380 repo non-dealers grouped into 727 families, forming 2,112 dealer-family dyads (p.10); dealers average nine non-dealer counterparties while non-dealers use fewer than three dealers (p.16). Five dealers explain 40% of all funding, yet those five account for only 8% to 18% of total variance contributions (p.12), and the inter-dealer segment is about 70% of daily volume while under half of over-the-counter trades are overnight (p.11). The limit of the evidence is severe: one market, one collateral type, one 73-month window, markups recovered from a model rather than observed, and no out-of-sample trading result.

## 2. What actually moves a dealer's quote: three factors and a rolling feedback loop

`1601.00085v2` is the operational counterpart to the repo evidence: a bid-offer adjustment model for a currency market maker that changes the quoted width as market and trading conditions change. Three factors enter - a conditional-volatility factor built as a GARCH-style recursion on log return innovations at 60-second intervals, a trade-count factor, and a volume factor, the last two measured as the log ratio of the latest interval to a 30-day rolling average (pp.9-11). The three are consolidated by a weighted sum with equal weights into a "spread factor", whose 30-day rolling mean and standard deviation define the action thresholds: widen when the factor exceeds half a standard deviation above the mean, narrow when it falls a third of a standard deviation below, with the size of the change proportional to the deviation (p.11). The change is capped by constants m = 2 and n = 3 (p.12).

The test used the EUR/USD pair from 24 July 2013 to 24 October 2013, with the trade price proxied by the mean of high, low, open and close over each minute and trade count and size simulated from a lognormal so total volume matches publicly reported figures (p.12). Reported outcome: a USD 513,050 increase in P&L for the window (p.12); the spread was widened 47,347 times and narrowed 48,244 times, hitting the upper bound 19,605 times and the lower bound 27,535 times (p.13); volume affected by the widened spread was about USD 444.95 billion and by the narrowed spread 443.19 billion (p.13). The limit is that the exercise is a single pair over one quarter, with simulated trade counts and volumes standing in for unobservable OTC flow and an arguably optimistic 30-day trailing calibration (p.12). The useful content is the architecture - measurable drivers, a rolling normalisation, asymmetric thresholds, explicit caps - not the profit number.

## 3. Inventory risk, internalisation and the endogeneity of dealer-market price impact

`1807.08278v3` solves a continuous-time Grossman-Miller intermediation model in which liquidity takers' demand is absorbed by dealers with quadratic inventory costs, who can gradually transfer positions to an exogenous open market with finite liquidity (pp.1-2). The equilibrium price in the dealer market is the expected dividend plus a term proportional to aggregate inventory measured in units of aggregate risk tolerance, S_t = D_t + (1/(1/lambda + 1/theta)) u_t (pp.3, 9), so the premium that clients pay is the compensation dealers need for warehousing.

Three results matter operationally. First, the price impact is transient rather than permanent, because client flow can be passed on to the open market; the classic Grossman-Miller permanent-impact result is recovered only in the limit of no open-market access (pp.3-4). Second, the cost of liquidity depends on the smoothness of the client flow. For smooth demand the liquidity cost is (M+1)/M times the open-market trading cost, order theta, and independent of dealer inventory cost (p.10); for diffusive demand it is of order sqrt(theta), depends on the quadratic variation of the flow, and does expose the dealers' holding cost (pp.4, 11). Approximation by a smooth strategy does not avoid the cost of a rough one (p.4). Third, competition matters more than coordination: a market with many small dealers is equivalent to one with a single representative dealer if the liquidity cost is rescaled by a factor of two, and as the number of small agents grows the welfare loss from segmentation disappears, with the ratio of segmented to integrated welfare tending to one (pp.12, 17). The limit is that this is a stylised linear-quadratic equilibrium with no asymmetric information, no fees and no empirical calibration.

## 4. Quoting policy under inventory in a self-exciting book

`2207.09951v1` trains a Soft Actor-Critic controller on a weakly consistent, multivariate Hawkes process limit-order-book simulator with eight event types, where the market maker's own limit and market orders feed back into arrival intensities (p.3). The policy observes inventory, the spread and a trend variable, chooses offsets on both sides, and is rewarded by terminal wealth minus a running penalty on the absolute value of inventory rather than the squared value, which the authors use for a value-at-risk reading (pp.4-5). Training used 10^6 steps and evaluation 10^3 Monte Carlo simulations of 100 steps with inventory constraint c = 3 and penalty coefficient 0.005 (pp.4, 7).

Against a symmetric best-bid-offer benchmark and the best member of a linear-inventory class that contains the Gueant-Lehalle-Fernandez-Tapia approximations, the learned controller reported a mean P&L of 13.76 versus 9.87 and 8.21, a Sharpe ratio of 1.62 versus 1.20 and 1.42, a mean absolute position of 0.42 versus 1.47 and 0.55, a mean P&L per unit mean absolute position of 32.51 versus 6.73 and 14.99, and thinner P&L tails with kurtosis 4.21 versus 6.06 and 10.56 (p.5). Under perturbed background intensities the Sharpe ratio falls monotonically from 1.38 to 1.02 to 0.86 (p.6). The fee result is the most transferable: the learned policy tolerated limit-order fees up to about 0.6%, the linear rule turned negative at 0.4% and symmetric quoting at 0.2%, and the mean number of transactions fell from 24.54 at zero fees to 15.0, 11.28 and 7.85 at 0.2%, 0.4% and 0.6% (p.6). The limit is that all of this is inside a simulator: the fills, the jump-size distributions and the excitation kernels are assumed, and no live or historical book is used.

## 5. Passive market making without discretion: the constant-product profitability frontier

`2302.05219v2` formalises the economics of a constant-product market maker and separate the two components of liquidity-provider return: a non-negative fee accumulation effect and a divergence (impermanent) loss that is a deterministic function of the change in the price ratio (p.2). Dividing the fee effect by the cost side produces a "profitability frontier" in the reserve space, a convex set of allocations for which provision beats holding, with price limits that the authors derive in closed form for both fee cases (pp.2-3).

The empirical part uses every Uniswap V2 transaction: 132,657 pools, 2,371,811 mint events, 1,048,613 burn events and 93,749,446 swap events between block 10,000,835 (4 May 2020) and block 16,308,189 (end of 2022) (p.5). Because a mint and a burn are separate transactions, and a full cycle can require up to five blockchain transactions including ERC-20 approvals, the fixed network cost is large relative to a small position (p.4). The result is an implicit lock-in for small providers: in the twelve largest pools, positions defined by a fee-to-size ratio of 0.10 (small), 0.05 (medium) and 0.01 (large) were profitable on 30-day holds in 0.00 / 0.05 / 0.49 of observations for USDC/WETH, and 180-day and 360-day profitability rose to 0.20 / 0.55 / 0.69 and 0.44 / 0.47 / 0.53 (p.6). The WBTC/WETH pair, where both legs are volatile, was 0.00 / 0.00 / 0.12 at 30 days and 0.00 / 0.09 / 0.72 at 360 days (p.6). The limit is that snapshots are daily and positions are virtual, so the study measures the distribution of outcomes, not realised LP P&L; it also offers no optimal-control policy.

## 6. What high-frequency liquidity providers' price dynamics actually look like

`2509.02941v1` treats Uniswap v3 pools as a laboratory of high-frequency market making and estimates the drift and diffusion of log price non-parametrically with a Kramers-Moyal expansion, then integrates the drift to obtain the potential that the price is pulled toward (pp.3-4). Data are three pools on Arbitrum: USDC/WETH for 2024 (about 300k transaction records) and for the first half of 2025, and WBTC/WETH for 2024, with each observation the log price averaged over 15 consecutive transactions at roughly 100-second spacing, and log-price tails trimmed at 0.5% (pp.3-4).

The finding is that the linear-diffusion default is wrong at intraday horizons. At lags from 10 minutes to 6 hours the estimated drift is strongly non-linear and the implied potential is non-quadratic; for USDC/WETH in 2024 two of three two-month windows produced a clear double-well potential while a one-month window produced a non-harmonic single well (pp.5, 8). Above hourly sampling the potential usually has one global minimum, sometimes with a small local minimum; the authors read a single well as a resilient mean-reverting state and a double well as a metastable state with two competing price regimes, which they associate with uncertainty or stress (pp.7-8). They also argue that fast hidden drift components self-average out at windows beyond five days, so only slow-varying components are worth modelling (p.10). The limit is that the conclusions are graphical and window-dependent, the sample is three pools on one chain, and the interpretation of the double well as a stress signal is not tested against an external labelling of stress.

## 7. Dealer and client structure in an illiquid market: reciprocity, concentration and the franchise

`1910.13882v1` is a narrative case study of Michael Milken and the junk-bond market, and it is the slice's only evidence on how a dealer franchise is actually built in a market with no central venue. The numbers are historical aggregates: Milken's salary and bonus rose from USD 43 million in 1983 to 123 million in 1984 and peaked near 500 million in 1987, while the junk-bond market grew from near zero to more than USD 125 billion (p.2). In 1977 only a handful of non-investment-grade issuers existed and only 6% of roughly 11,000 corporations were investment grade; new junk issuance ran below USD 1.5 billion a year from 1977 to 1981, then exploded, with Drexel issuing USD 1.1 billion for MCI in 1983 and taking 60% of its 1985 profit from junk bonds at underwriting fees of 3% to 4% (p.7).

The structural claim is the interesting one for a platform: the dealer ran on "an intricate network of reciprocal obligation", selling the bonds of one issuer only if the buyer agreed to take another issuer's bonds later, and it committed principal by buying back the bonds it underwrote (pp.7-8). Client loyalty was treated by the sentencing judge as the mechanism through which the defendant profited - by increasing clients' dependence and therefore the edge over competitors (p.12). Concentration is documented with a Herfindahl index for junk bonds (p.9), and the financing it enabled was heavily levered: debt was 60% to 90% of the transactions used to acquire majority ownership, though LBOs were only 15% to 25% of all completed mergers (pp.11-12). The limits are real: this is a secondary-source narrative with no price data, no spread series and no measured market-making behaviour, so it can support hypotheses about client structure but nothing about quoting.

## 8. Cross-market spread response: segmentation decides the sign

`2601.08263v3` asks whether DeFi operational shocks destabilise a traditional short-term funding price, the spread between 3-month AA nonfinancial commercial paper and the 3-month Treasury bill. The sample is the 50 largest exploit-driven shocks from 2021 to 2024, with event dates mapped to the next US trading day when needed and macro-event days excluded from the stacked panel (pp.12-13). Mean loss per event was USD 88.80 million with a maximum of 625 million, and the spread itself has a mean of 12.34 basis points over 1,005 daily observations (p.6).

The naive contagion prior is a widening; the measured pattern is a narrowing of about 3.0 basis points on the event day and about 2.4 basis points on the next trading day, against a joint pre-trend test that fails to reject zero (p.25), with placebo empirical p-values of 0.000 and 0.002 for the two days from 500 covariate-matched Monte Carlo draws (p.29). The authors' mapping is explicit: a segmented market narrows when demand amplification into the prime-eligible segment exceeds the supply pressure loading on it (p.12), and regulatory segmentation under SEC Rule 2a-7, which requires government money market funds to hold at least 99.5% of assets in cash, government securities or fully collateralised repo, makes prime funds the plausible marginal destination for recycled liquidity (pp.1, 4). Supporting evidence is weaker than the headline: larger losses coincide with higher gas prices with a coefficient of 0.140 and R-squared of 0.078 (p.19), a gas-price cutpoint is estimated at 32.93 Gwei (p.20), a gradient-boosted model ranks gas price first at 41.0% relative importance against 34.8% for VIX (p.22), and the pricing mechanism itself is inferred rather than identified because daily fund-level routing is unobserved (p.2).

## Caveats

Only three of the eight papers contain measured market data on a real venue - `2603.10690v1` (one collateral type in one country, 2016-2022, with model-recovered markups), `2302.05219v2` (Uniswap V2 daily snapshots and virtual positions) and `2601.08263v3` (one aggregate spread series with an inferred routing mechanism) - and `2509.02941v1` adds three pools on one chain. Everything else is theory, simulation or narrative: `1807.08278v3` is a linear-quadratic equilibrium with no asymmetric information, `2207.09951v1` is entirely inside a Hawkes simulator, `1601.00085v2` simulates its own trade counts and volumes and reports one quarter of one currency pair, and `1910.13882v1` has no price data at all. The slice disagrees with itself on the value of relationships: the Milken narrative treats client reciprocity as the dealer's franchise, while the repo transaction data finds relationship metrics predicting volumes but not spreads (p.25). It also disagrees on whether a shock widens or narrows a spread, and the resolution offered here - segmentation itself - is inferred rather than measured (`2601.08263v3`, p.2). Nothing in the slice establishes an out-of-sample, net-of-cost profitability claim for any quoting rule; the only P&L figures are a one-quarter backtest of a single FX pair (`1601.00085v2`, p.12) and a simulator (`2207.09951v1`, p.5). Finally, the slice says almost nothing about prediction markets or options, and its DeFi content is one chain, one pool family and one year.

## Papers read in depth

- `2603.10690v1` - When David becomes Goliath: Repo dealer-driven bond mispricing (2026). Dealer market power and network shocks priced into gilt yields at 2.5-5.3 pp; one market, one window, model-derived markups.
- `1601.00085v2` - Dynamic Multi-Factor Bid-Offer Adjustment Model (2016). A deployable three-factor quote-adjustment loop with rolling thresholds; simulated flow and one quarter of EUR/USD.
- `1807.08278v3` - Liquidity in Competitive Dealer Markets (2021). Transient dealer-market impact and an order-of-magnitude gap between smooth and rough client flow; stylised equilibrium, no data.
- `2207.09951v1` - Deep Reinforcement Learning for Market Making Under a Hawkes Process-Based Limit Order Book Model (2022). Absolute-inventory penalty and a measurable fee tolerance; simulator-only, assumed fill and jump laws.
- `2302.05219v2` - Decentralized Exchanges: The Profitability Frontier of Constant Product Market Makers (2023). A closed-form profitability frontier plus full Uniswap V2 transaction coverage; virtual positions, no realised P&L.
- `2509.02941v1` - Non-Linear and Meta-Stable Dynamics in Financial Markets: Evidence from High Frequency Crypto Currency Market Makers (2025). Non-parametric evidence that intraday liquidity-provider dynamics are non-quadratic and regime-dependent; three pools, window-dependent shapes.
- `1910.13882v1` - Michael Milken: The Junk Dealer (2019). The franchise-as-network account of dealing in an illiquid market, with historical aggregates; no price or spread data.
- `2601.08263v3` - A Blessing in Disguise? DeFi Exploits and Short-Horizon Responses in U.S. Commercial Paper Spreads (2026). A cross-market spread that narrows after an outside shock, with a segmentation explanation; one aggregate series, mechanism inferred.
