# Bubbles, crashes and criticality: bubble regimes are diagnosable, their timing is only probabilistic

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads 153 harvested records on bubbles, crashes and criticality, spanning 2000-2026, of which 55 carry a journal reference and 24 appeared from 2021 onward. From 40 screened candidates, twelve papers are read in depth, drawn from four families: the log-periodic power law (LPPL) model of the Johansen-Ledoit-Sornette (JLS) school, that school's own clarifications and calibration work, the econometric right-tailed unit-root bubble tests of the Phillips-Shi-Yu family, and network or recurrence-based indicators of criticality and systemic risk. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, who needs to know what a "bubble alarm" can support before it is wired into risk limits, position sizing or a strategy gate.

## What this covers

The slice runs from the definition of a bubble as faster-than-exponential price growth decorated by log-periodic oscillations, through the engineering of a calibration stable enough to automate, to the published out-of-sample record and its failures, and then to two diagnostics that do not use price dynamics at all: right-tailed unit-root tests on valuation ratios, and recurrence-network indicators on a basket of bank stocks. The single most consequential finding is that the defensible output of the LPPL method is a distribution over the critical time, not a crash date: on synthetic series with noise at 5% of the largest log-price, the critical time is recovered with a standard deviation of about 20 days against a 60-day forecast gap (`1107.3171v3`, p.15-16), and about one bubble in three ends without a crash at all (`1001.0265v2`, p.7). Everything downstream of that - alarms, sizing, hedges - has to be probabilistic, and the clearest ex-ante channel found is not a short position but a reduction of exposure and leverage while the regime persists.

Selection rule: a paper had to carry a reproducible quantitative diagnostic of a bubble, crash or critical transition - an LPPL calibration, a unit-root exuberance test, or a network indicator - together with either an out-of-sample record or an explicit mechanism; purely policy, narrative and undated-prophecy papers were dropped.

## The short answer

1. A bubble here is a dynamic regime, not a mispricing: faster-than-exponential growth with log-periodic oscillations, and the method says nothing about fundamental value (`1404.2140v1`, p.7, p.20-21).
2. Calibration is now deterministic enough to automate: re-parameterisation cuts the nonlinear search from four dimensions to three and removes the quasi-periodic cost landscape (`1108.0099v3`, p.4, p.11-12).
3. On six-month windows over the SSE Composite from July 1999 to May 2011, the cost function never showed more than three distinguishable local minima, and bubble-regime windows usually showed one (`1108.0099v3`, p.12-13).
4. The defensible output is a distribution over the critical time: with noise at 5% of the largest log-price, a true t_c = 300 days is recovered at 296.07 (standard deviation 20.44) with Gaussian noise and 295.15 (20.81) with Student-t noise over 2000 estimates (`1107.3171v3`, p.14-15).
5. The strongest ex-ante case in the slice is oil: an 80% confidence interval of 17 May to 14 July 2008 for the peak, with the actual peak on 3 July 2008 and the steep descent starting 11 July (`1001.0265v2`, p.8).
6. Roughly one bubble in three lands smoothly, so a bubble alarm cannot be treated as a crash signal or a short signal (`1001.0265v2`, p.7).
7. Long-horizon directional skill is mostly ambient drift: S&P 500 anti-bubble predictions hit 90-95% for horizons beyond 120 days but only 53% for one day, and the paper states the high-horizon figure reflects the bear trend (`physics/0301023v2`, p.14-15).
8. The model covers endogenous crashes only: of 49 drawdown outliers, 25 are endogenous, 22 exogenous and 2 tied to the Japanese anti-bubble (`1107.3171v3`, p.9).
9. Timing is unstable across the data series of one episode: for Royal Ahold the equity fit puts t_c at 2004/06/19, the post-bubble fit puts it at 1996/07/24 and the sales fit at 2002/05/20 for a February 2003 collapse (`cond-mat/0403563v1`, p.8, p.10), while the gold study fits omega = 15.86 against the documented 2.9-15 range (`1012.4118v1`, p.6).
10. A different family answers a different question: over 1999-2000 the right-tailed GSADF test finds exuberance in the NIFTY 50 (3.728618, p = 0.0000) but not in the SENSEX (0.148245, p = 0.4640), so the two diagnostics disagree about which index was "in a bubble" (`2207.13444v1`, p.3).
11. Criticality is measurable in structure as well as price: a multiplex recurrence network flagged 9 of 12 Chinese bank-crisis days, with 3 exactly coinciding, against 0-2 of 12 for the standard variance, autocorrelation and power-spectrum benchmark (`2310.10283v1`, p.12, p.15).
12. The dangerous nodes in a crash network are small ones: the critical market confidence obeys alpha_c = 1 - c, and randomising the bipartite stock-investor network steepens it to alpha_c = 1 - 2c, making resilience a portfolio-overlap property (`1908.07244v1`, p.7, p.16-17).

## 1. What the LPPL model actually claims

`1404.2140v1` states the definition with unusual clarity: a bubble is a period of unsustainable growth in which the growth rate itself grows, so the price follows a faster-than-exponential (hyperbolic) path that ends at a critical point where the model stops having a solution; log-periodic oscillations are then added as a second, independently motivated signature, and the full formula carries seven parameters (p.7, p.18). The separation from conventional analysis is explicit: the method identifies dynamic regimes that are not sustainable and has "nothing to say in itself about any possible mispricing", so the authors distinguish assets that are fundamentally over- or undervalued from assets that develop a positive or negative bubble (p.20-21). The economic content is a crash hazard rate that diverges at the critical time t_c, which is therefore the most probable time of a regime change rather than a certain crash: the derivation requires 0 < m < 1, with m < 1 keeping the hazard integral below one and m > 0 keeping the price finite even at t_c (`1107.3171v3`, p.5-6). The empirical filters that separate meaningful fits from "fitting an elephant" with four parameters are 0.1 <= m <= 0.9, 6 <= omega <= 13, |C| < 1 and B < 0 (`1108.0099v3`, p.6, p.10). A practical folklore accompanies the theory: across the examples surveyed by Sornette, the crisis starts on average 1.4 months before t_c (`1012.4118v1`, p.4). For a platform, the definition is attractive because it is testable on any series without a dividend model, and dangerous for the same reason: a seven-parameter fit is cheap to obtain and easy to over-read.

## 2. Calibration: from metaheuristics to a bounded search

The methodological core of the slice is `1108.0099v3`. The original LPPL needs three linear and four nonlinear parameters, and because the three linear ones can be slaved to the nonlinear ones, the search is four-dimensional with a quasi-periodic, multi-minimum cost surface that has historically required taboo search or genetic algorithms and even then gives no convergence guarantee (p.4, p.6, p.10). Expanding the cosine into C1 = C cos(phi) and C2 = C sin(phi) turns the model into four linear and three nonlinear parameters, eliminates the quasi-periodicity, and leaves a smooth surface where controlled local search suffices; a second subordination step slaves m and omega to t_c so that the fit quality can be read as a function of the critical time itself (p.11-12). The supporting numerical study fits 6-month moving windows over the SSE Composite from July 1999 to May 2011 and never finds more than three clearly distinguishable local minima of the reduced cost function for t_c scanning from one day to 90 days past the window end; most bubble-regime windows give one minimum, and extra minima correspond to poor fits (p.12-13). A companion clarification paper adds the operating rules that make the ensemble work: scan many window starts t1 rather than one, because the start of a bubble is "fuzzy and degenerate", and keep the whole ensemble of locally optimal fits rather than hunting a single global optimum (`1107.3171v3`, p.12-13). The additional validity filter is the non-negative risk condition b = -B m - |C| sqrt(m^2 + omega^2) >= 0, which guarantees a non-negative crash hazard (p.7). `physics/0301023v2` shows the same discipline on the 2000-2002 S&P 500 anti-bubble: an extended jack-knife picks six harmonics, the fitted anti-bubble start is 8 August 2000 +/- 10 days, and adding free phase parameters improves the residual only marginally while destroying predictive skill (p.9, p.12). `1107.3171v3` recommends block-bootstrap ensembles reshuffling residuals in 25-day blocks, which preserves dependence up to a month without assuming Gaussian noise (p.17). `2607.21826v1` applies exactly this pattern on crypto: a fixed window of 72 daily points for Bitcoin and 69 for Ether, each with 20 alternative left endpoints (52-72 and 49-69 points), "Sornette bounds" as the signal filter, and a genetic algorithm for the global optimum (p.5-6).

The subordination of m and omega to the critical time is what makes that inspection possible: the reduced cost function F2(t_c) carries fitted functions m-hat(t_c) and omega-hat(t_c), so fit quality can be plotted directly against the parameter domain and the constraint region can be drawn on the same axes (p.12-13).

`1404.2140v1` is useful for keeping the mechanism honest. It separates the positive feedbacks into a technical class - option hedging, portfolio insurance, trend following, procyclical bank financing, stop-loss orders and market-maker spread optimisation - and a behavioural class built on imitation and herding, and it argues that imitation is close to optimal under serious uncertainty, which is why bubbles recur (p.9, p.12). A platform that imports the diagnostic should not also import the mechanism story as a generator: the slice contains no calibration of either class to observed flow.

## 3. The out-of-sample record, read honestly

`1001.0265v2` is the clearest documented ex-ante case. The 2008 oil bubble was flagged with an 80% confidence interval of 17 May to 14 July 2008 for the end of the unsustainable phase using data through the last week of May; the actual peak was 3 July 2008 and the steep descent began 11 July, both inside the interval (p.8). For the 2009 Shanghai bubble, the prediction submitted on 10 July 2009 (data through 9 July) gave 20%/80% quantiles of 17-27 July 2009 and 10%/90% quantiles of 10 July to 10 August 2009; re-run five days later it tightened to an 80% probability of 19 July to 3 August, and Chinese stocks fell sharply on 29 July with the Shanghai index down 22% in August (p.8-9). The same paper supplies the crucial negative result: a bubble can land smoothly "approximately one-third of the time", so the alarm is probabilistic in outcome as well as in timing (p.7). The S&P 500 record in `physics/0301023v2` is more sobering than the abstract suggests. Ten retroactive one-month predictions from January 2001 to October 2002 are presented; the first two fail to give the correct trend and two more capture the trend but are too pessimistic (p.14). The success rate for the sign of the return rises from 53% at a one-day horizon to above 70% beyond 20 days and 90-95% beyond 120 days, and the authors state plainly that the high-horizon figure "only reflects the overall downward trend" of the period; the plateau of about 75% over 20-70 days is what they call genuine skill (p.14-15). The corresponding daily Sharpe ratio of the retrospective strategy is 0.1 to 1.4 against 0.06 for short-and-hold, with no transaction costs included in the headline result (p.15). The forward prediction from that paper - a peak near 1000 in the first quarter of 2003 and a dive to 650 in early or mid-2004 (p.16) - is the kind of claim later work in this corpus should be checked against. On the synthetic side, `1107.3171v3` reports negligible bias and a t_c standard deviation of about 20 days, three times smaller than the 60 days separating the last observation from the true critical time, which the authors call significant skill (p.16). `1404.2140v1` describes the operational version: about 25,000 financial time series monitored daily on one supercomputer, an ensemble alarm index per asset, and a June 2005 warning that the US real-estate market would change radically by mid-2006 (p.15, p.22).

The crypto study is explicit about what its confidence bands mean: Phillips-Shi-Yu alerts are graded low (50-70%), medium (70-90%) or high (90-100%), and its Table 1 reports Bitcoin PSY windows of 11Dec2016-06Jan2017 and 21Mar2017-2May2017 at medium confidence and 5May2017 onward at high confidence, while a 28% slump over two weeks in September 2017 produced no valid JLS signal and is classified as a local swing tied to exogenous news (p.7, p.12). That classification is the honest failure mode of the method and the reason its scope is bounded.

Finally, the scope limitation is stated by the model's own authors. `1107.3171v3` writes that the JLS model applies only to endogenous crashes preceded by bubbles, that the specific unfolding of each crash and the post-crash dynamics are beyond its premises, and that about two-thirds of major crashes are endogenous while the remaining third are exogenous and fundamentally unpredictable (p.9-10). Any alarm built on this literature is therefore a statement about one class of instability, and the risk system still has to carry the exogenous class separately.

## 4. Negative bubbles, rebounds and how to score an alarm

The same machinery has a mirror image. `1001.0265v2` defines a negative bubble with the same power law and the sign of B reversed, so that a positive B gives a downward-accelerating log-price, the upside-down image of a positive bubble, while m keeps the constraint 0 < m < 1; the accelerated decline is then a regime that ends in a rebound rather than a crash (p.10). It converts detection into a supervised problem: the S&P 500 series from 1950-01-05 to 2009-06-03 is cut into 11,662 windows with start steps of 50 calendar days, end steps of 50 calendar days, a minimum window of 110 days and a maximum of 1500 days; fits before 1975-01-01 form the learning set, and a rebound is defined as a local minimum over a window extending 200 days on each side (p.12, p.13). Fits whose critical time lands near a rebound form Class I and the rest form Class II; the statistically different features of the two classes define a rebound alarm index in [0, 1], and an alarm stays active for 40 further days (p.12-13). The evaluation is an error diagram: failure-to-predict is plotted against the fraction of time spent in alarm, the anti-diagonal is random guessing, and a useful detector bends toward the origin (p.13-14). Nine rebounds occur after 1975-01-01 and ten before, and the authors report a fast drop in failure-to-predict as alarm time increases (p.15, p.15-16). This scoring rule is worth copying even where the LPPL model itself is not adopted, because it makes a detector's false-alarm cost explicit and comparable, and it is the only such evaluation in the slice.

## 5. Where the published forecasts over-reach

Three cases show the ceiling. `cond-mat/0403563v1` fits Royal Ahold over multiple decades and reports, on the same figure, an equity power-law fit with t_c = 2004/06/19 and a post-bubble log-periodic fit with t_c = 1996/07/24 and omega = 12.31 (p.10); the two critical times disagree by eight years, and the equity fit points to a change of regime in mid-2004, well over a year after the actual collapse on 24 February 2003 (p.4, p.10). The paper itself is candid: "no precise timing can be claimed here", the rule of thumb is only that t_c is robust to about one year before a crash, and the sales-based fit gives t_c = 2002/05/20 for a February 2003 collapse while the earnings-based t_c = 2002/09/28 is explicitly called not robust because quarterly earnings are prone to manipulation (p.10, p.11, p.8). The Wilks test log-likelihood ratio of 13.5 for the seasonal oscillation (chance probability 0.12%) supports the model form on sales, not the timing (p.9). The paper's own reading of Ahold is a sequence of four parameter regimes: a bullish phase from 1993 to mid-1997 with conformity kappa = 1.5 and inclination beta = -0.25, a hesitation phase of a few months with beta = -4, a destabilization phase from 1998 to the end of 2002 with kappa = 3.5 and beta = -4, and a return to stability once kappa falls below 1 + mu (p.19, p.27-30). The parameters are assigned by the analyst to reproduce a known history, not estimated from data, which is exactly the degree of freedom that makes the case study illustrative rather than predictive.

`1012.4118v1` forecasts the burst of the gold bubble for late April to early May 2011 from t_c = 2011.45, then refines it to May and to May-June 2011 with a target price of $1500-1700 in current dollars (p.7, p.10, p.11); its fitted omega of 15.86 sits above the 2.9-15 range the authors cite from Sornette, and the paper contains no ex-post evaluation of the forecast and warns that anything like an intervention by the Federal Reserve or the People's Bank of China removes the foundation of the calculation (p.6, p.1). `2607.21826v1` is the most careful of the applications: signals are expressed as a date plus or minus a standard deviation with a signal count, so Bitcoin is 14Dec2017 +/- 3 (50 signals) under OLS and 15Dec2017 +/- 9 (52) under GLS, and Ether is 12Jun2017 +/- 2 (6) or 15Jun2017 +/- 4 (15), anticipating the ~30% Bitcoin crash in the second half of December 2017 and the 12 June Ether crash (p.12, p.7, p.10). But its evaluation window ends on 16 January 2018, so the signals after December 2017 are not scored, and the PSY panel in its own Table 1 contains a reversed window, "06Dec2017 - 14Mar2017", which is a reporting defect in the very table that carries the evidence (p.1, p.12). The pattern across the three is consistent: the regime statement survives, the date statement is a window with a standard deviation of days to weeks, and nothing in the slice establishes a magnitude for the move after the change of regime.

## 6. The alternative: valuation-based exuberance tests

`2207.13444v1` occupies different ground. Rather than fitting price dynamics, it applies the Phillips-Shi-Yu generalized sup augmented Dickey-Fuller test to price-earnings ratios of six Indian indices over the dot-com episode of 1999-2000, with critical values from 1000 Monte Carlo replications and lag order zero (p.1, p.2-3). The null is a unit root and rejection indicates an explosive, exuberant regime; the GSADF statistics are 3.728618 (p = 0.0000) for the NIFTY 50, 3.728618 (p = 0.0000) for the NIFTY NEXT 50, 2.858897 (p = 0.0010) for the NIFTY 500, 2.484461 (p = 0.0290) for the S&P BSE 100, 0.653972 (p = 0.4920) for the NIFTY BANK and 0.148245 (p = 0.4640) for the S&P BSE SENSEX (p.3). The paper concludes that four of the six indices showed a bubble and two did not (p.5-6). Two defects are worth carrying forward: the NIFTY 50 and NIFTY NEXT 50 statistics are identical to six decimal places, and the single-bubble SADF for the NIFTY NEXT 50 is significant at the 5% level (0.877954, p = 0.0290) while the same index's GSADF is reported at the 1% level, so the index-to-index comparison is not clean (p.3). The value of this family is that it answers a different question - whether a valuation ratio is on an explosive path - and it is well represented in the wider literature: the review `2310.00490v1` lists Phillips and Yu's 2011 dating paper among its ten most-cited documents with 659 global citations (p.9). The two families are not substitutes, and `1404.2140v1` explains why: valuation-based tests need a fundamental anchor, while the LPPL approach deliberately refuses to estimate one (p.20-21).

## 7. Criticality measured in networks and baskets

The second half of the slice abandons price dynamics for structure. `1908.07244v1` builds a bipartite stock-investor network from Chinese mutual-fund holdings and studies the single-stock price limit as an individual risk-tolerance parameter. The model yields an unexpected linear relation between the critical market confidence and the price limit, alpha_c = 1 - c, so raising the limit barely reduces the confidence needed to keep the system stable (p.7). The nodes that drive collapse are not the "superspreaders" of the contagion literature but relatively small stocks with high nestedness and branching: they share all their investors with more popular neighbours, absorb the first-round shock and broadcast it onward, and the simulated cascade runs from the network periphery to the k-core, matching the observed ordering of limit-down stocks on four crash days in 2015 (p.11-13, p.14). Randomising the network steepens the relation to alpha_c = 1 - 2c, and even partial randomisation adjusts it, which is the paper's policy result: resilience is a portfolio-overlap property (p.16-17). The authors temper this themselves, noting the "slim prediction power" of a network built on mutual funds when individual investors are the majority of traders (p.14). `2310.10283v1` moves the same idea to higher frequency. Using 1-minute closing prices of 16 Chinese listed banks from 4 January 2012 to 30 October 2020 - 2005 trading days - it builds multiplex recurrence networks per 240-minute window, tracks average mutual information and average edge overlap, and defines crises by BNS jump tests at 5-minute sampling (p.11-12). At a 0.001 confidence level 12 extreme fluctuations are detected and average mutual information covers 9 of them, 3 exactly coinciding; at 0.0025 the average edge overlap covers only 7 of 27, which the authors attribute to overlap measuring linear rather than nonlinear coherence (p.12). Against the standard early-warning benchmark of rising variance, lag-1 autocorrelation and low-frequency power, no benchmark indicator passes both sensitivity and significance for more than 2 of the 12 crises, and variance passes 0 (p.15).

The topology adds a second reading: the maximum spanning trees at indicator peaks lack a dominant core node, while non-peak periods have a stable central node (p.15-16).

The slice closes with a survey of the field. `2310.00490v1` maps 616 articles from 294 sources between 1976 and 2023, of which more than 90% appeared after 2006; its most frequent keywords are "systemic risk", "financial crisis", "banking crisis", "financial distress", "machine learning models" and "currency crisis", its output first exceeded one standard deviation above the mean in 2019-2022, and its four clusters are bankruptcy prediction, banking crisis, currency crisis and emerging markets, and machine-learning forecasting (p.1, p.4-5, p.10). Read against the rest of this slice, the field has largely moved from dating bubbles to classifying institutions and currencies, which is the opposite of the price-path question the LPPL work answers; the review's own recommendation is to add alternative data such as news sentiment and network structure (p.1).

## 8. What is actually established

Four things survive scrutiny. First, the faster-than-exponential growth signature is measurable and the LPPL functional form fits bubble episodes across equities, indices, commodities and crypto (`1404.2140v1`, p.11; `1012.4118v1`, p.6; `2607.21826v1`, p.12). Second, the calibration problem is solved well enough to automate, with a three-parameter nonlinear search, an ensemble over window starts, and explicit validity filters (`1108.0099v3`, p.11-12; `1107.3171v3`, p.7, p.12). Third, the output is a probability distribution over a regime change, with a timing uncertainty on the order of 20 days on synthetic data and roughly a one-in-three chance of no crash (`1107.3171v3`, p.16; `1001.0265v2`, p.7). Fourth, structural criticality is a separate and shorter-horizon signal family, measurable from public high-frequency data and from holdings data, with documented hit rates against explicit benchmarks (`2310.10283v1`, p.12, p.15; `1908.07244v1`, p.7). What is not established: that any of these produces net-of-cost alpha, that a single fit can be trusted without an ensemble, and that an exogenous crash can be anticipated at all, since the model excludes it by construction (`1107.3171v3`, p.9-10). The discipline to import is the error diagram: plot failure-to-predict against alarm-period fraction, so a detector is scored against the random line rather than against a narrative (`1001.0265v2`, p.13).

## Caveats

Most of this slice is case study, and the case studies are single-market and single-period: `cond-mat/0403563v1` is two Dutch companies read retrospectively; `physics/0301023v2` is one index over one anti-bubble and its forward prediction is unscored in the paper; `1012.4118v1` is one commodity, uses an out-of-range omega, and contains no ex-post evaluation; `2207.13444v1` covers one two-year dot-com window with price-earnings ratios only and a duplicated test statistic; `2607.21826v1` covers about 13.5 months of two crypto assets and ends before the post-January-2018 signals can be scored; `2310.10283v1` is one country's listed banks over 2012-2020; `1908.07244v1` uses mutual-fund holdings while individuals dominate Chinese trading, which the authors call slim prediction power. The slice disagrees with itself on what a bubble is: `2207.13444v1` tests explosive valuation ratios, while `1404.2140v1` explicitly declines to estimate fundamental value and treats the two notions as different objects (p.20-21). Two of the applications violate the validity filters their own school publishes, and the deep-reading set contains no independent replication of the LPPL out-of-sample record beyond the two cases in `1001.0265v2`. Nothing here establishes that any alarm supports a profitable directional trade: the only Sharpe figures (`physics/0301023v2`, p.15) are retrospective, exclude transaction costs, and are compared against a symmetric short-and-hold in a market that fell. No paper in this slice quantifies the magnitude of the post-regime change, and none tests prediction markets, options surfaces or FX, so the transfer to those books is unverified.

## Papers read in depth

- `cond-mat/0403563v1` - Bubble, Critical Zone and the Crash of Royal Ahold (2004). Couples super-exponential sales and earnings, a post-bubble destabilization regime, and an opinion-formation model; a single retrofitted company and two fits that disagree on the critical time limit it.
- `physics/0301023v2` - Renormalization Group Analysis of the 2000-2002 anti-bubble in the US S&P 500 index (2003). A Weierstrass-type extension reproduces five crashes and reports retroactive success rates and one forward forecast; the high-horizon skill is trend, as the authors concede.
- `1108.0099v3` - A Stable and Robust Calibration Scheme of the Log-Periodic Power Law Model (2011). The re-parameterisation that removes quasi-periodicity and cuts the nonlinear search to three parameters; the empirical support is one index.
- `1107.3171v3` - Clarifications to Questions and Criticisms on the Johansen-Ledoit-Sornette Bubble Model (2011). The best single statement of the bounds, the endogenous-only scope, and the synthetic recovery of t_c; it is an advocate's document, not a neutral audit.
- `1404.2140v1` - Financial bubbles: mechanisms and diagnostics (2014). The clearest operational definition and the FCO monitoring setup; a white-paper level of detail with no falsification tests.
- `2207.13444v1` - Interrogation of A Bubble in the Indian Market (2022). A right-tailed unit-root test on Indian index valuation ratios; a duplicated statistic and a two-year window weaken the index-to-index comparison.
- `2607.21826v1` - Are cryptocurrencies real financial bubbles? Evidence from quantitative analyses (2026). Runs LPPL and Phillips-Shi-Yu side by side on Bitcoin and Ether with dated signals; the sample ends before most signals are scored and the PSY table has a reversed window.
- `1001.0265v2` - Diagnosis and Prediction of Tipping Points in Financial Markets: Crashes and Rebounds (2010). The clearest ex-ante record in the slice, plus the negative-bubble rebound method and the error-diagram scoring; the successful cases are two.
- `1012.4118v1` - Log-Periodic Oscillation Analysis and Possible Burst of the "Gold Bubble" in April - June 2011 (2010). A dated, falsifiable gold forecast with a two-method cross-check; omega is out of range and no outcome is evaluated.
