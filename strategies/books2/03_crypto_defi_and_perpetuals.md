# Crypto venues, DeFi and perpetuals: the cost of consensus stayed pinned to transfer value while protocol control stayed concentrated

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers 193 records on crypto venues, DeFi protocols and the industrial economics behind them, drawn from the arXiv q-fin.GN harvest and spanning 2012-2026, of which 40 carry a journal reference. From a 40-record shortlist I read 12 papers in depth: mining cost and mining-game theory, Ethereum's fee market and gas derivatives, coin-versus-token issuance dynamics, governance-token concentration, DeFi lending before and after the Merge, a stablecoin-lending run, Mt. Gox trade-graph manipulation, Tether minting and burning events, corporate bitcoin treasuries, and illicit-account detection. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, and it states mechanisms and measurement designs, not trading results.

## What this covers

The slice runs from the physical layer upward. It opens with what consensus actually costs and who supplies it (`2004.04605v2`, `2004.08167v1`, `2606.03153v1`), moves through the priced resource market on Ethereum (`2406.06524v2`), the asset layer of coins against tokens (`1803.03088v2`), the governance layer where "decentralised" becomes a number (`2102.10096v2`), the credit layer of DeFi lending and its runs (`2207.06285v1`, `2303.08748v3`), and ends with data quality: venue manipulation, issuance events, corporate treasuries and illicit flow (`1902.01941v1`, `2501.05232v1`, `2505.14655v1`, `2412.02408v3`). Its single most consequential finding is that the price of security is not a free parameter: over 2010-2020 Bitcoin's mining cost held a stable share of transferred value, a mean of 0.14% and 0.3% since end-2017, while hashrate rose ten orders of magnitude (`2004.04605v2`, p.13, p.14). The same network's capacity is supplied by six pools holding 89.4% of it (`2606.03153v1`, p.22).

## The short answer

1. Bitcoin's proof-of-work spend is pinned to transfer value: the mining-cost/transaction-volume ratio averaged 0.14% over 2010-2020 and 0.3% since end-2017, with a band of one order of magnitude while both inputs moved nine (`2004.04605v2`).
2. The same arithmetic says the protection is thin: at six-block settlement depth, attacks duplicating more than about 1.5% of global transfer volume can be profitable (`2004.04605v2`).
3. Mining capacity is concentrated and its stability is not automatic: the top six pools held 89.4% of capacity, and restoring stability in 2022-2023 would have needed fees above $135,000 per block (`2606.03153v1`).
4. The mining game is well posed, so hashrate is not a random exogenous input: a unique equilibrium exists, real hashrate converges to a stationary level, and security is constant or rising with demand (`2004.08167v1`).
5. Ethereum gas price is heavy-tailed, positively skewed and slowly mean-reverting, which makes it calibratable but not hedgeable (`2406.06524v2`).
6. Coins and tokens are different universes, not one: tail exponents 0.48 against 1.29, and incumbent growth of 1.52% against 0.77% per period (`1803.03088v2`).
7. DeFi protocol governance is not decentralised in any measured sense: no observed token distribution needed more than 100 addresses for a quorum (`2102.10096v2`).
8. DeFi credit risk is event-driven and detectable before publication: Abracadabra's pools halved in a month and most series broke 31 hours before the triggering tweet (`2207.06285v1`).
9. Protocol interventions worked at the Merge - bad debt stayed under 0.04% of total debt - while borrowers extracted more than 300,535 ETHW worth over 13 million USD (`2303.08748v3`).
10. Venue manipulation leaves structure, not just outliers: 12.5% of Mt. Gox accounts traded at extreme prices and abnormal-account graphs correlated with log price at up to 0.871 (`1902.01941v1`).
11. Stablecoin issuance moves bitcoin intraday only when sentiment is positive and the event is publicly announced: 0.68% at 30 minutes per billion minted, and nothing significant for burns (`2501.05232v1`).
12. Illicit-account detection is now accurate enough to screen and too biased to label: 96.80 F1 on 1,888,553 accounts, with false positives concentrated in high-activity legitimate accounts (`2412.02408v3`).

## 1. Consensus cost is pinned to transfer value

`2004.04605v2` builds a lower bound on Bitcoin's energy cost over 2010-2020 by repricing each day's hashes at the most efficient ASIC then available and at the Brent crude spot price. Hardware efficiency doubled every 10 months, with best-fit lambda = 0.0025 (p.8); the extremes in the hardware table are a 2007 ARM Cortex A9 at 877,193 J/Th and a March 2020 Antminer S19 Pro at 30 J/Th (Table II, p.6). Daily hashes rose from 10^15 to 10^25 between September 2010 and May 2020 in four regimes with doubling times of 33, 261, 38 and 198 days (p.9), so hashing activity grew ten billion times while energy consumption grew ten million times (p.1). Estimated daily cost went from about 3 USD in 2010 to over 4 million USD in early 2020 (p.12), and daily transferred value from about one thousand USD to nearly one billion USD (p.12). The ratio has no trend: mean 0.14% over the whole period with 10% quantiles of 0.02% and 0.4% (p.13), and mean 0.3% since end-2017 with quantiles of 0.1% and 0.4% (p.14). Feeding the last-three-years mean into the fair-cost argument with N = 6 gives an attack threshold of about p = 1.5% of global transfer volume, so duplicating more than that could pay (p.14). The authors note the estimate is a lower bound built on ideal hardware and oil-linked energy, and that the attack formula ignores hardware cost and price collapse.

## 2. The mining game: equilibrium, gaps and stability

`2004.08167v1` models miners as a mean-field game and shows the game is well posed: a unique Lipschitz solution exists (Thm 3.1, p.12), the real hashrate converges to a stationary K regardless of initial conditions while nominal hashrate grows at the rate of technological progress (p.6, p.15), and equilibrium security is constant in deterministic settings and increases with demand when the exchange rate is stochastic (p.6-7, p.16). Its comparative statics use c = $0.02 per kWh, r = 5% and 20% technological progress and are explicitly not calibrated (p.17); with those inputs total miner value peaks near a 60% rate of technological progress (p.18), and in the no-friction limit the stationary real hashrate tends to 1/c (p.18). With two miner populations facing different electricity prices, only cheap-power miners operate in the unique stationary equilibrium (p.25).

`2606.03153v1` adds the frictions. With on/off hash supply a mining gap exists exactly when the largest miner finds delay optimal (p.9-10); steady-state stability requires mu'(w) >= -2mu/w (Thm 4, p.14), and with a gap and a block reward above accumulated fees no epsilon-stability exists at all (Cor. 3, p.15). Calibrated to Bitcoin over 2022-2023, when the top six pools held 89.4% of capacity and the largest about 24.6% (p.22) and estimated network capacity reached 496,735,701 TH/s on 31 December 2023 (p.22), the network came close to that region: using a JP Morgan estimate of about $18,000 to mine one bitcoin and an operating cost of $112,500, stability would have required fees above $135,000 per block (p.24, p.25). The model assumes miners price in the target cryptocurrency and treats fees as exogenous.

The constraint that makes gaps possible is the difficulty-adjustment rule: Bitcoin recalibrates every 2016 blocks, roughly two weeks, and the paper works with a recalibration bounded between a quarter and four times the previous rate (p.12). The physical bounds matter for any block-space strategy. In March 2024 the network performed 2^78 hashes in ten minutes while a single Antminer KS5 performs 2^53 in the same window, so individual hardware is irrelevant to the aggregate (p.13). A worked example with ten miners, c = 2 and revenue 2t + 3 has a unique steady-state winning rate of 22/9, a mining gap of 13/22, and no achievable system stability (p.15-16).

## 3. Ethereum's fee market is a priced resource with no hedge

`2406.06524v2` documents the mechanism: the effective fee per gas unit is an algorithmically adjusted and burned base fee plus a user-set priority fee, with any excess of the max fee refunded (p.4). The block target is 15 million gas against a 30 million cap (p.8), a plain transfer costs 21,000 gas, and opcodes range from 1 gas for JUMPDEST to 32,000 gas for CREATE (p.7, p.9). Across 1,284,938 minute-level median gas prices from 28 December 2021 to 10 June 2024, the median is 23.15 with mean 35.31, standard deviation 85.39, minimum 2.00, maximum 18,479.78, skewness 66.32 and kurtosis 6,643.85, fitted by a log-normal with mu 3.20 and sigma 0.79 (Table I, p.25). The estimated Hurst exponent is about 0.38, implying anti-persistent increments, yet the simulation uses H = 0.5 (p.25-26); the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck fit gives kappa 0.00700, mu 3.20 and sigma 0.0937 (p.25), a very slow mean reversion. The paper then values European and double-strike gas options by analogy with weather derivatives (p.17) while conceding the demand process is assumed rather than derived and the underlying is not tradable, so exact replication and hedging are unavailable (p.17-19, p.26). Protocol change is visible in the series: Dencun, live 13 March 2024, cut L2 fees by over 90% in some cases (p.30).

## 4. Coins and tokens are different asset universes

`1803.03088v2` classifies 2,499 cryptocurrencies from 28 April 2013 to 7 February 2018, of which 1,497 were still alive at the end (p.2, note 4). Coins, which run their own chain, show a market-share tail exponent between 0.5 and 0.7, while tokens issued on top of another chain sit between 1.0 and 1.3 (p.3); the maximum-likelihood estimates are 0.48 [0.39, 0.57] for coins and 1.29 [0.83, 1.75] for tokens against theoretical values of 0.50 [0.41, 0.57] and 1.29 [1.12, 1.48] (Table 1, p.9). Proportional growth holds in both markets, with growth rates of 1.52% [1.45%, 1.59%] for coins and 0.77% [0.68%, 0.86%] for tokens at near-identical volatility of 24.67% and 24.6% (Table 1, p.9). Token birth size grows at 1.19% [0.48%, 1.90%] and token birth intensity at 0.59% [0.57%, 0.61%] while the coin parameters are flat, and the exit hazard is zero for both (p.8, p.9). The token count went from under 50 to over 400 during 2017 (p.3) and bitcoin's dominance fell from above 80% to below 50% (p.4). The authors read exponents below and above one as entrenched incumbents against an immature ecosystem; the model carries no non-stationarity, no fitness differences, no forks and no cross-correlation, and the sample ends in February 2018.

## 5. Governance and lending: "decentralised" is measurable, and modest

`2102.10096v2` measures four governance-token distributions, BAL, COMP, UNI and YFI, covering $5.081 billion of Ethereum assets at a combined $1.692 billion valuation on 31 October 2020 (p.2), inside a DeFi market that grew from $400-500 million of assets at the start of 2020 to over $13.6 billion by end-October (p.3). After deleting 366 non-participatory addresses (p.4) and computing Gini and Nakamoto coefficients, no protocol needed more than 100 addresses for a quorum and the paper reports a double-digit Nakamoto coefficient for every one of them (p.6-8). Uniswap produced the lowest Gini and highest Nakamoto coefficient, while Compound is the weak case with only 42.37% of tokens distributed through incentivised participation and 26.05% to founders and team (p.3, p.7). The paper's own comparison sits at the consensus layer: four bitcoin pools control 50.5% of compute and three pools control 62.3% of Ethereum's (p.3). The evidence is one snapshot on pseudonymous addresses, so multi-address holders and off-chain influence are invisible.

The allocation design is visible in the same tables and explains the ranking. Participation incentives carried 65% of BAL, 42.37% of COMP, 45% of UNI and 100% of YFI, retrospective user rewards only 15% at Uniswap and nothing elsewhere, and founders and team 5%, 26.05%, 21.82% and 0% (Table 1, p.3). After pruning, the remaining addresses held 88,801,074 of 911,031,617 UNI and 17,395 of 30,000 YFI, so most supply was never in a voting wallet at the snapshot (p.4). The authors' own conclusion is blunt: decentralised infrastructure does not imply decentralised authority at the application layer (p.7).

## 6. DeFi credit under stress: trust shocks and fork windfalls

`2207.06285v1` studies Abracadabra.Money around the doxxing of Wonderland's treasury manager at 06:47 UTC on 27 January 2022 (p.5-6). Loans in the MIM stablecoin protocol had reached more than $3.1 billion against almost $5.3 billion of collateral by end-December 2021 (p.4); from peak to end-January the wMEMO-MIM pool fell 97% (p.5), total Ethereum pools went from 3,134.72 to 1,616.02 million MIM, a fall of 48.4%, and Avalanche pools from 116.20 to 3.11 million, a fall of 97.3% (Table 1, p.10). The UST pool, 45.7% of Ethereum pools at end-January, fell 33.9% from a high of 1,116.50 to 737.99 (p.10). Bai-Perron breakpoints in most aggregate series land 31 hours before the tweet, while the UST pool breaks 24 and 27 hours after it (p.6-7). MIM's own price moved less than 2 cents (p.17), so this was a collateral run on a trust shock, not a stablecoin depeg.

`2303.08748v3` covers the Merge of 15 September 2022. AAVE and Compound together hold more than 85% of the volume locked in Ethereum lending (p.3, p.5). Borrow rates jumped by two orders of magnitude and borrowing at times reached 100% of available funds (p.1); Compound's 100,000 ETH cap held utilization below 35% and the borrow rate below 10% (p.10, p.11, p.12), while AAVE's ETH utilization still reached 100% (p.14). Losses were small: bad debt stayed below 0.01% of total debt at Compound and below 0.04% at AAVE, with no liquidation spike (p.12, p.17). The profitable trade was the fork: arbitrageurs extracted over 300,535 ETHW worth more than 13 million USD (p.3, p.19), with 49,206 ETH(W) traced from Compound borrowers and 251,329 ETH(W), 39% of AAVE's 643,367 ETH(W) borrowed, moved to exchanges (p.10-11, p.15). Collateral concentration was real: 20.3% of stETH market capitalization sat on AAVE (p.17).

The rate mechanics explain why the two protocols diverged. Compound's post-change curve sets an 80% optimal utilization with 2% base and slopes of 20% and 4,910%, while AAVE's ETH market uses a 70% optimal utilization with 3% base and slopes of 4% and 100% (p.6, p.9); the second slope is what turns a shortage into a near-vertical rate. Cumulative borrowing cost from 9 August to the Merge was about 0.03% on Compound against about 1% on AAVE (p.23, p.24), and the mean AAVE position size fell from about 70 ETH to about 45 ETH as larger lenders exited (p.18). The paper also records that Compound's contract assumed 2,102,400 blocks a year while the chain produced 6,245 blocks a day, or 2,279,425 a year, a mismatch worth checking against any venue that hard-codes block calendars (p.6).

## 7. Venue manipulation and issuance events

`1902.01941v1` mines the leaked Mt. Gox ledger from April 2011 to November 2013, cleaning about 18 million rows into 6.7 million completed transactions (p.2) and flagging accounts whose implied price exceeded 1.5 times the daily high or fell below 0.5 times the daily low (p.3). Of 119,343 accounts, 14,916 (12.5%) are abnormal and 194,790 of 6,775,117 transactions (2.8%) are abnormal, but 2,831,202 transactions, more than 41% of all, are normally priced trades between abnormal accounts (p.3). The abnormal-account graphs look different from the normal one: clustering coefficients 0.30 and 0.42 against 0.03, average weighted degree 505.43 and 3,107.68 against 76.21 (Table III, p.4), and the first SVD base network correlates with log price at 0.56 and 0.58 against 0.05 for the normal graph, rising to 0.811 and 0.871 with ten base networks fitted against 0.239 (Table IV, p.6). Recurrent structures include an account trading with itself 749 times in one day (p.7) and a routing polygon through bridge accounts (p.8). The evidence is correlational and the ledger's completeness cannot be verified.

`2501.05232v1` runs an intraday event study over 367 Tether minting and 220 burning events from 6 October 2014 to 9 January 2021 (p.6, p.12). One billion USD minted lifts bitcoin 0.24% over 5 minutes, 0.38% over 10, 0.51% over 15 and 0.68% over 30, fading to 0.57% at 60 minutes (p.8, p.15). Burning coefficients are insignificant in every window and Wald tests reject symmetry at 10 to 30 minutes (p.16). The response is state-dependent: the minting coefficient reaches 1.56 at 30 minutes under positive sentiment against 0.08 under negative sentiment (p.17), and only minting events both positively received and tweeted by Whale Alert move the price, at 0.57 to 1.12 over 5 to 30 minutes (p.18). Whale Alert tweeted 222 of 367 mints, 60%, but only 11 of 220 burns, 5% (p.12). The burning sample is tiny and the price series is spliced across Bitfinex and Bitstamp.

## 8. Treasuries, illicit flow and what the data layer can carry

`2505.14655v1` regresses 39 listed bitcoin-treasury firms on BTC: average beta is 0.62 with 12 firms above 1 (p.1), and Strategy (MSTR) shows beta 1.37 with R^2 0.44 (p.11) while holding 528,185 BTC against a cross-section median of 1,200 BTC (p.7). Same-day correlation averages 0.292, but one-day lagged correlations average -0.018 and 0.018, so there is no systematic linear lead-lag (p.10). Rolling transfer entropy gives 0.0241 bits from BTC to MSTR against 0.0191 bits back, and 410 of 1,298 windows, 31.6%, are significant in the BTC-to-equity direction against 179, 13.8%, in reverse (p.14).

`2412.02408v3` builds an ensemble semi-supervised detector over 6,903,860 Ethereum transactions and 1,888,553 accounts with only 581 hand-curated illicit accounts, an illicit base rate near 0.1% (p.1, p.4, p.5). Its best configuration reaches 97.86 precision, 95.78 recall, 96.80 F1 and 99.44 accuracy, against 82.68 F1 for plain XGBoost and 80.57 for a random forest (p.8), and beats the strongest earlier result by 2.56 points of precision, 0.90 of F1 and 3.74 of accuracy (p.1). The authors report false positives concentrated in high-activity legitimate accounts and pseudo-label noise accumulating after the third self-training iteration (p.18, p.22), so the output is a screen, not a label.

## Caveats

Two of the twelve papers carry no measurement of the thing they model: `2004.08167v1` is pure theory whose calibration is explicitly illustrative, and `2406.06524v2` prices gas derivatives against an assumed process because no gas-derivative market exists to calibrate against. Almost everything else is single-market and single-period: `2207.06285v1` is one protocol over a 96-hour window, `2303.08748v3` one protocol pair around one event, `1902.01941v1` one exchange and an unverifiable leaked ledger, `2102.10096v2` one October 2020 snapshot, `1803.03088v2` a window ending in February 2018 before the token market matured, `2606.03153v1` a 2022-2023 calibration, and `2501.05232v1` a 220-event burning sample with a spliced price series. The slice also disagrees with itself on the same object: `2004.04605v2` concludes consensus cost is too low to deter double spending, while `2606.03153v1` puts the network near the boundary where no stable mining equilibrium exists at all; both readings are kept here. No paper in the read set prices a perpetual contract, measures funding, basis or open interest, or nets an on-chain result into a tradable return, so the "perpetuals" of the title is a venue label for this slice, not a measured result, and nothing here establishes out-of-sample live performance.

## Papers read in depth

- `2004.04605v2` - The cost of Bitcoin mining has never really increased (2020). Ten-year lower bound on mining cost; the ratio result is the strongest evidence here, but it is a bound.
- `2004.08167v1` - Mean Field Game Approach to Bitcoin Mining (2020). Equilibrium and stability of hashrate; pure theory with an illustrative calibration.
- `2606.03153v1` - Mind the Gap in the Mining Game (2025). Mining gaps, pool concentration and stability thresholds; one two-year calibration.
- `2406.06524v2` - Gas Fees on the Ethereum Blockchain (2024). Fee mechanism plus gas-option pricing; the derivatives side has no market to validate against.
- `1803.03088v2` - Classification of cryptocurrency coins and tokens (2018). Power-law tails by asset type; model omits correlation, fitness and forks.
- `2102.10096v2` - How Decentralized is the Governance of Blockchain-based Finance (2021). Nakamoto coefficients for four protocols; a single snapshot on pseudonymous addresses.
- `2207.06285v1` - Does DeFi remove the need for trust (2022). Hourly pool-level run on a trust shock; one protocol, 96-hour window, no control market.
- `2303.08748v3` - DeFi Lending During The Merge (2023). Rates, liquidations and fork arbitrage through one event; traces only direct exchange transfers.
- `1902.01941v1` - Market Manipulation of Bitcoin (2019). Trade-graph forensics on Mt. Gox; correlational, with a heuristic price threshold.
- `2501.05232v1` - The Intraday Bitcoin Response to Tether Minting and Burning Events (2022). Clean short-window event study; small burn sample and a spliced price series.
- `2505.14655v1` - Cryptocurrencies in the Balance Sheet (2025). Treasury-holder betas and information flow; effectively a single-firm case.
- `2412.02408v3` - Ensemble-Based Semi-Supervised Learning for Illicit Account Detection (2025). Strong imbalance-aware metrics; dataset-specific and prone to false positives.
