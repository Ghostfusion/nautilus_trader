# Macro, rates and foreign exchange: central-bank announcements co-jump the curve, and AI-read data releases price the currency

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers the macro, interest-rate and foreign-exchange slice of a local harvest of the arXiv `q-fin.GN` (General Finance) category: 3095 records, 1997-2026. The slice is the 140 records whose title or abstract touches macro aggregates, interest rates, yield curves, inflation or exchange rates; 40 of those were shortlisted and 12 were read from the PDFs. It is written for an operator of a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform that trades equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, and it is deliberately operational: what a curve is made of, how it moves on policy days, how a data release becomes a position, and how to build the pieces the platform currently lacks.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from the institutional prehistory of the yield curve - when a curve is observable at all - through parametric and information-geometric ways of fitting and comparing curves, the high-frequency evidence that central-bank announcements co-jump the whole term structure, macro-release-driven FX and crypto signals, the inflation-expectation loop, density-forecast combination, and the recovery of interest rates from derivative prices where no bond market exists. Its single most consequential finding for this platform is that the tradable macro information is concentrated in scheduled announcement windows and co-moves the term structure: in US Treasury and European bond futures from 2007 to 2017, co-jumps play roughly twice as large a role in the US market as in Europe, and approximately 30% of announcements are accompanied by a level shift of the whole curve while only about 1% are read as a change in central-bank preferences `1905.01541v1`. That is a mechanism a backtest can only reproduce if the event calendar is a first-class input, not a footnote.

## The short answer

1. A yield curve is an institutional object before it is a mathematical one: it requires standardised instruments, comparable credit, several maturities, observable prices and a secondary market, none of which existed for most of credit history `2609.07958v1`.
2. Where no bond market exists, zero-coupon curves can be recovered from listed derivatives: call-put parity on inverse options combined with inverse futures yields synthetic bonds and implied rates `2509.03964v2`.
3. Central-bank announcements are where the curve co-jumps: co-jump activity is about twice as large in US Treasury futures as in European bond futures, and level shifts of the whole curve accompany roughly 30% of announcements in both markets `1905.01541v1`.
4. Rotations are rare: only about 1% of announcements are read as a change in central-bank preferences, against 30% level shifts, so the default event model should be a parallel shift, not a slope shock `1905.01541v1`.
5. Co-jumps inflate the observed correlation of the curve: measured total correlation exceeds the continuous-only correlation by 0.036 to 0.065 across maturities, so a jump-free correlation estimate understates curve co-movement `1905.01541v1`.
6. AI-read macro data releases give a cross-sectional currency signal with an annualised Sharpe above 0.7 and an alpha worth 74% of the strategy return at a 48-month lookback, and the paper's four look-ahead tests do not reject genuine reasoning `2608.00761v1`.
7. Bitcoin falls about 24 bps on a one-standard-deviation inflation surprise (t = 2.44) while no major cryptocurrency reacts to growth news, so the crypto-inflation-hedge trade has the wrong sign `2301.10117v1`.
8. Household inflation expectations respond asymmetrically to news, and the asymmetry became statistically significant in the early 1990s: rising-inflation news moves expectations more than falling-inflation news `2009.11557v1`.
9. The monetary regime does not neutralise the inflation-uncertainty link: in most of six Eastern European economies higher inflation is associated with higher inflation volatility, with the credibility of the regime determining the exceptions `1303.6192v1`.
10. Regularised mixtures of density forecasts beat every individual ECB-SPF inflation forecaster, and after the Great Recession the optimal regularisation moves probability mass from the centre to the tails, correcting overconfidence `2012.11649v3`.
11. A discount function is a complementary distribution function, so a yield curve is a probability density; maximum-entropy interpolation subject to observed moments is a principled curve construction and curve distance can be measured geometrically `1111.3757v1`.
12. On an illiquid frontier market the two-parameter Nelson-Siegel model fits better than the extra-flexible Svensson model, so extra flexibility is not free `2310.00321v2`.

## 1. What a yield curve is made of

The history paper `2609.07958v1` makes the point that a curve is not implied by the existence of credit. Interest-bearing credit is documented from around 2400 BCE (the Cone of Entemena) and the Code of Hammurabi, circa 1750 BCE, fixes benchmark rates of 33 1/3% for grain and 20% for silver; those rates recur for centuries (p.2). Classical Greek rates fall from 16%-18% at the time of Solon to 10%-12% in the fifth century BCE and 6%-10% by the end of the second century BCE, and normal Roman rates in the first century CE run 4%-6% (p.3). Yet the evidence is too heterogeneous to order rates by maturity, because most loans ran under a year and there was no secondary market to reveal the term structure (p.3). The decisive later step was standardisation and transparency: the Bank of England's posted discount rate sat at 5% from 1746 to 1822 (p.6), and 3% consols gave a continuous, standardised long-end observation from the mid eighteenth century (p.6-7). The practical reading is that a curve needs a benchmark issuer with repeated issuance across maturities, and that interpolation is the last step, not the substance (p.10).

The Burundi study `2310.00321v2` is the modern version of the same story. Domestic public debt there multiplied about 34 times between 2000 and 2019, the bond share of that debt rose from 11% in 2000 to 65% in 2019, and commercial banks went from holding 0% to 90% of the sovereign securities (p.4). The central bank obliged primary dealers to sell at least 10% of primary-market acquisitions onward and to quote within a +/-10% band for maturities up to 7 years and +/-15% above (p.4). That is the institutional scaffolding a curve requires, and it is exactly what the crypto market lacks.

## 2. Where no bond market exists, recover the curve from derivatives

`2509.03964v2` confronts a currency with a deep derivatives market and no fixed-income market. The authors use call-put parity on inverse options, combined with inverse futures, to replicate the payoff of a zero-coupon bond: buying an inverse call, selling an inverse put and shorting an inverse future replicates `F_t(T) - K` zero-coupon bonds in the reference currency (p.17-18), and combining such bonds with an inverse future replicates a zero-coupon bond in the crypto itself (p.18). Because the ratio of the call-put difference to moneyness is not constant across strikes, they estimate both zero-coupon prices by a joint weighted least-squares objective with a closed-form two-parameter solution (p.19-20), after trimming with RANSAC on `Put_ask - Call_bid` against `Call_ask - Put_bid` (p.20). On the trimmed BTC dataset the RANSAC slope averages -1.01 with a standard deviation of 0.02 and the intercept averages 0.02 (p.21), which is what parity requires.

The magnitudes matter. Using Deribit data at hourly frequency from January 2022 to December 2024 (p.20), BTC implied rates at a 90-day tenor average 0.0007 (0.07%) with a minimum of -0.1011; at 180 days they average 0.0004; at 360 days -0.0001 (p.24). ETH implied rates are negative on average at all three tenors, around -0.0014 to -0.0023 (p.24), which the authors flag as puzzling given an Ethereum staking yield near 2% (p.24). The reference-currency curve behaved very differently: Deribit USD rates were very low or negative in the FTX and Terra turmoil of late 2022, rose above 20% at points in 2024, and then stabilised between 10% and 15% (p.24). On 5 November 2024 the USD curve recovered from BTC options ran from about 9.15% to 10.12% and the one recovered from ETH options from about 8.10% to 8.73% (p.23). A platform quoting crypto options needs these curves as a matter of pricing hygiene; the paper also notes that Deribit has held over 80% of total options open interest since 2022 (p.13), so single-venue dependence is itself a risk.

## 3. The curve jumps on policy days, and it usually jumps in parallel

`1905.01541v1` is the strongest evidence in the slice for event-driven rates trading. Using one-minute and five-minute CME and Eurex bond futures for 2, 5, 10 and 30 years over 5 January 2007 to 28 December 2017, on 2705 US and 2780 European trading days (p.5, p.8), the authors separate the continuous covariation of the curve from co-jumps using wavelet coefficients and a Hausman-type bootstrap test (p.13-14). The 9-hour US window used carries 83% of all trade volume (p.5).

The results are large and uneven. For the 5-year/10-year pair the US market shows significant co-jumps on 444 of 2705 days and the 5-year/30-year pair on 365; for Europe the corresponding counts are 217 of 2780 and 240 (p.15). The share of quadratic covariation attributable to co-jumps is 7.11% for the US 5-30 pair and 5.86% for the US 5-10 pair, against 1.58% and 2.31% in Europe (p.15), and the paper's summary is that the role of co-jumps is about twice as large in the US as in Europe and about twice as large at the long end as at the short end (p.15). Co-jumps also change the correlation structure: total correlation exceeds continuous-only correlation by 0.036 to 0.065 in both markets, with the US 5-10 pair at 0.959 total versus 0.894 continuous (p.30). A backtest that models the curve with a continuous covariance and no jump component will systematically understate how much the curve moves together on stress days.

The announcement evidence is the actionable part. Using 103 FOMC and 119 ECB announcement days (p.7, p.9), the probability of a co-jump rises sharply in the 30-minute window after an FOMC release in the US, and the effect is weaker for the ECB (p.19-20). For the representative 2-year/10-year pair, 32 of the 103 FOMC days show significant co-jumps; 31 of those are positive level shifts and exactly one is negative, the 18 March 2008 rate cut (p.25). On the 2602 non-announcement US days there are only 8 such days for that pair, and only 3 days on which the whole four-contract curve shifts without news (p.26). Across both markets, roughly 30% of announcements are accompanied by a level shift of the whole curve and only about 1% are interpreted as an adjustment in central-bank preferences (p.1, p.26). Event risk in rates is therefore mostly a parallel shift in a narrow window around a known timestamp.

## 4. Turning a data release into a currency position

`2608.00761v1` is the paper this platform should study most closely for FX. The author feeds the realised value, previous figure and consensus forecast of each economic data release to GPT-4o, with the release date deliberately excluded, and asks for a direction (STRENGTHEN, WEAKEN, INSIGNIFICANT OR UNCERTAIN) (p.3, p.9). The calendar covers 544 unique indicators and 174,820 data points from January 1996 to October 2024, with nine G-10 exchange rates and one-month forwards (p.8). The resulting AIFX index is the net balance of positive and negative signals over a lookback window (p.10-11). Sorting the currencies and going long the top two and short the bottom two produces annualised Sharpe ratios above 0.7, strongest for 36- to 60-month lookbacks (p.2, p.12). At a 48-month lookback the alpha after controlling for dollar, dollar-carry, carry, momentum and value is statistically significant and accounts for 74% of the strategy's average return (p.13). A time-series version driven by the US dollar's own AIFX index earns Sharpe ratios of 0.4 to 0.5, while the non-USD leg alone is not significantly different from zero (p.17).

The look-ahead work is what makes the result usable rather than decorative. Asked to guess the release year from the same inputs, the model is right only 5.6% of the time on average within each year (p.5), and at the year level precision is 5.68%, recall 4.17% and F1 2.36% (p.19). A difference-in-differences comparison of GPT-4o and GPT-3.5 across the 2021 knowledge-cutoff boundary finds no significant divergence (p.21). A control portfolio built purely from what the model remembers of monthly returns has a Sharpe of 0.91, skewness of 2.52 and excess kurtosis of 18.61, yet regressing the AIFX strategy on it gives a significant positive alpha, a negative beta and information ratios of 0.75 to 0.85 (p.22-23). The limits are real: the paper reports no net-of-cost performance, the sample is one vendor's calendar, and the signal is a monthly rebalancing of G-10 currencies.

## 5. Inflation surprises hit different assets differently

`2301.10117v1` studies the reaction of six major cryptocurrencies to macroeconomic news announcements in a 30-minute window from ten minutes before to twenty minutes after the release (p.9). The sample runs from January 2013 to May 2021 with 99-101 observations per announcement type (p.6). No cryptocurrency reacts significantly to growth news, with responses under 2 bps per standard deviation (p.9). Bitcoin loses 24 bps per one-standard-deviation inflation surprise, with t = 2.44, and the regression explains 2.5% of announcement-window return variation; Bitcoin Cash loses 18 bps (t = -2.218) (p.10, p.19). The mechanism is not the interest-rate channel: the 5-year Treasury yield rises significantly on both growth (t = 10.1) and inflation (t = 7.5) surprises (p.21), but Bitcoin's reaction to the rate change during the inflation window is positive and insignificant (t = 0.89) (p.11, p.22). The paper's preferred explanation is a consumption-savings shift, supported by a negative loading on a personal-consumption proxy (p.11-12).

`2009.11557v1` supplies the expectations side. Using the Michigan Survey of Consumers from January 1978 to February 2016, 208,777 individual records (p.9), it finds a structural break in median inflation expectations in September 1983 (p.10). Median expectations fall from 6% to 3% across the break and the cross-sectional variance of expectations falls from 34.3 to 15.2 (p.11). Hearing news of higher inflation raises expectations by 1.03 percentage points in the first sub-sample and 0.64 in the second; hearing news of contractionary monetary policy raises them by 0.49 (p.14), which is the signalling interpretation of a Taylor rule rather than the transmission one. The asymmetry is the operational point: in the second sub-sample, higher-inflation news adds 0.46 points against -0.22 for lower-inflation news, and the difference becomes significant after 1991, while easing-monetary-policy news dominates tightening news after 1999 (p.21-23). Exposure to inflation news also collapsed: the share hearing inflation (monetary policy) news fell from 11.33% (12.47%) to 5.61% (5.96%) across the break (p.12).

`1303.6192v1` checks whether the monetary regime changes these relationships, using monthly data from January 2000 to July 2011 for three currency-board economies (Bulgaria, Estonia, Lithuania) and three inflation-targeting economies (Czech Republic, Poland, Romania) (p.5). Inflation standard deviations are higher under currency boards - 3.34, 2.85 and 3.33 - than under inflation targeting - 1.83, 1.58 and 7.71 for the outlier Romania (p.6). The EGARCH asymmetry parameter, positive and significant for Bulgaria (0.615), the Czech Republic (0.523) and Romania (0.116), supports the view that higher inflation is more volatile there, while the sign is negative for Estonia and Poland, which the authors attribute to regime credibility (p.7-8). The paper's conclusion is that no regime clearly dominates (p.9), a useful caution against regime-conditional macro factors in a strategy.

## 6. The inflation-rate loop as a delayed dynamical system

Two theory papers in the slice are worth keeping for the intuition that interest-rate and inflation dynamics can oscillate. `1104.2344v1` builds a two-sector model - manufacturers and bankers - whose income proportionality implies that inflation is proportional to the time derivative of the log of the long-short interest-rate spread, with an explicit delay of six months to a year (p.2). `1603.08311v1` eliminates inflation using the Fisher relation and shows the real long rate satisfies the delay logistic equation, with oscillations appearing when the product of the long nominal rate and the delay exceeds 1/e, damped oscillations below about 1.5706 and sustained oscillations above it (p.10, p.14). The illustrative numbers are concrete: with an 8% long nominal rate the model damps for delays between 1.05 and 44.8 months, and with a 12% rate it sustains oscillations once the delay exceeds 13.09 months (p.16). The authors' policy inequality is that the controlling agency should keep the product of the spread and the delay below 1/e to avoid oscillation (p.20). Neither paper has data; they are mental models for why a curve can overshoot and why a delayed reaction function can produce cycles that look like regime change.

## 7. Combining macro forecasts without overconfidence

`2012.11649v3` is the slice's answer to the question of what to do with many inflation-related forecasts. It combines density forecasts from the ECB Survey of Professional Forecasters, 83 quarterly surveys from 1999Q1 to 2019Q3, screening 103 unique forecasters down to 18 and adding a uniform forecaster for 19 in total (p.21, p.23). The key constraint is that mixture weights must satisfy the unit simplex - non-negative and summing to one - because negative mixture weights can drive the mixture density negative and without sum-to-one the log-score optimum is undefined (p.7-8). With that constraint plus ridge or entropy regularisation, every regularised mixture beats every individual forecaster in the survey pool, including the ex post best one (p.23). The best 4-average log score is about 15% better than the median individual forecaster and 7% better than the ex post best (p.23), while selecting on average only 3.52 to 5 forecasters (p.23). The most interesting structural result is that from the Great Recession onward the optimal regularisation moves probability mass from the centre to the tails, correcting overconfidence, whereas before 2008 it mostly moved mass upward and adjusted location (p.1, p.25). The method is cheap: with a 19-forecaster pool, best-4-average requires evaluating and sorting 5035 subsets per period (p.13).

## 8. Comparing and fitting curves

Two papers give tools rather than findings. `1111.3757v1` shows that with positive interest rates the discount function is a complementary distribution function, so minus its derivative with respect to maturity is a probability density (p.6). The space of admissible term structures is therefore the convex space of densities on the positive real line (p.6), and two curves can be compared by Bhattacharyya spherical distance (p.6) or by the Fisher-Rao metric, with the whole curve evolution representable as a measure-valued process (p.12). Maximum entropy subject to observed moments gives an explicit interpolation - if only the mean is known, the maximum-entropy density is exponential and the rate is the reciprocal of the mean (p.14) - which is a defensible default when quotes are sparse. The limits are acknowledged: for flat simple-yield structures none of the moments exist, and for compounding frequency kappa moments exist only up to order kappa - 1 (p.13), so moment-matching must be guarded.

`2310.00321v2` tests the two standard parametric forms on the Burundi auction data. From 112 raw auction dates with maturities from 13 weeks to eight years the authors reduce to 30 dates each carrying seven securities - three bills and four bonds - giving 210 securities (p.11). Fitting by differential evolution and comparing errors, Nelson-Siegel has a median error of 0.7129 on the 67-day imputed dataset against 0.7274 for Svensson, and the difference is significant on both the original data (t-test p = 2.678e-3) and the imputed data (Wilcoxon rank-sum p = 3.867e-7) (p.15-16). The mean Nelson-Siegel error is stable at about 0.84 across both datasets (p.17), and the authors conclude that Svensson is over-parameterised for an illiquid market (p.18). On a thin curve, adding parameters buys fit on paper and variance in practice.

## Caveats

No paper in this slice supplies order-book or fill-level evidence for rates or FX instruments, so none of them says anything about execution cost in those markets, and none reports net-of-cost performance: the FX result `2608.00761v1` is gross of costs and turnover, and the co-jump paper `1905.01541v1` measures covariation, not tradable edge. The evidence is narrow in time and place. The co-jump study covers 2007 to 2017 in two venues only `1905.01541v1`; the crypto-news result is six assets over 2013 to 2021 `2301.10117v1`; the inflation-expectation result is US households only `2009.11557v1`; the regime comparison is six small open economies over eleven years `1303.6192v1`; the implicit-rate result is one venue `2509.03964v2`; and the parametric-curve comparison rests on 30 auction dates in one market `2310.00321v2`. Two papers in the read set have no data at all `1104.2344v1` `1603.08311v1`, and one is pure mathematics `1111.3757v1`; they contribute framing, not estimates. The slice also disagrees with itself in places: `2509.03964v2` finds crypto implied rates near zero while ETH staking should push them up, and `1303.6192v1` finds no regime that dominates while its own volatility statistics favour inflation targeting. Nothing here establishes that any of these signals survives transaction costs, that a co-jump is predictable before it happens, or that a crypto-implied curve is a usable discount curve at institutional size.

## Papers read in depth

- `2608.00761v1` - AI and Exchange Rate Predictability (2026). Turns macro data releases into a directional currency signal via an LLM, with unusually thorough look-ahead tests; no cost analysis and one vendor's calendar.
- `1905.01541v1` - Co-jumping of Treasury Yield Curve Rates (2019). Wavelet separation of co-jumps in US and European bond futures tied to FOMC and ECB announcement days; 2007-2017 only.
- `2609.07958v1` - Historical Reflections on Interest Rates and the Emergence of the Yield Curve (2026). Explains the institutional preconditions for a curve; a narrative essay with no estimates.
- `2509.03964v2` - Cryptocurrencies and Interest Rates: Inferring Yield Curves in a Bondless Market (2025). Recovers zero-coupon curves from inverse options and futures via call-put parity; single venue and heavy trimming.
- `2310.00321v2` - Modeling the yield curve of Burundian bond market by parametric models (2023). Compares Nelson-Siegel and Svensson on auction data; small sample and imputed maturities.
- `1104.2344v1` - Interest Rates and Inflation (2011). Derives inflation as proportional to the delayed log-change of the interest-rate spread; a two-sector model with no data.
- `1603.08311v1` - Interest Rates and Inflation (2016). Reduces the model to a delay logistic equation and derives oscillation thresholds; simulation only, no estimation.
- `2301.10117v1` - Bitcoin Does Not Hedge Inflation (2023). Event-study evidence that Bitcoin loses value on inflation surprises; six assets and a five-year window.
- `1303.6192v1` - Inflation Uncertainty, Output Growth Uncertainty and Macroeconomic Performance (2013). EGARCH comparison of currency-board and inflation-targeting regimes; six economies, 2000-2011.
- `2009.11557v1` - A note on the impact of news on US household inflation expectations (2020). Michigan Survey evidence on asymmetric news effects; US households only.
- `2012.11649v3` - On the Aggregation of Probability Assessments (2020). Regularised mixtures of ECB-SPF inflation density forecasts; survey data only and ex post optimal penalties.
- `1111.3757v1` - Interest Rates and Information Geometry (2011). Reframes the discount function as a density and gives curve distances and maximum-entropy interpolation; no empirical calibration.
