# Econophysics and agent-based markets: fitted wealth tails and fitted agent parameters are both non-identifiable

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief reads the general-finance half of the econophysics literature: the kinetic wealth-exchange models that predict income and wealth distributions, the Gibrat firm-growth framework behind lognormal size distributions, herding agent-based models that claim to generate the leverage effect, the zero-intelligence limit-order book, and the calibration methodology that ties the whole program together. The slice is the arXiv q-fin.GN census topic "econophysics and agent models": 148 records spanning 2000-2025, 51 of them carrying a journal reference. From 40 screened candidates, 12 papers were read in depth from the PDFs; every number below carries a page marker taken from the file. The reader is an operator of a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, who needs to know which of these models can be trusted as a simulator and which of their parameters can be recovered from data at all.

## What this covers

The slice runs from the microscopic rules of wealth exchange to the macroscopic distributions they produce and then to the discipline of fitting those models to market data. It covers the exponential body and power-law tail of wealth, the demonstration that the tail is an overlap of exponentials and a finite-time transient, Gibrat's law as the lognormal null for firm growth, the way local and preferential interaction changes the distribution, herding and the leverage/anti-leverage effect, the exact scalings of a zero-intelligence limit-order book, minority games and crowd-avoiding queues as models of opinion and coordination, and finally parameter degeneracy, non-unique continuum limits and optimization instability. The single most consequential finding is that the stationary Pareto wealth law the kinetic-exchange program rests on is not a stationary property of any finite population: `0809.4139v2` shows that for finite N the mean-field distribution is not stationary, the power-law tail is transient, and the order of the N -> infinity and t -> infinity limits decides the answer (p.9). A second, parallel thread is identifiability: `2208.14207v1`, `1904.04951v3` and `2202.00831v1` each present a model whose parameters are not recoverable from the statistics used to fit or validate it - degenerate terms, a non-unique continuum limit, and a cycling optimum.

## The short answer

1. The stationary Pareto tail of kinetic wealth exchange is a finite-size, finite-time artifact: for any finite N there is no stationary wealth distribution, and for N = 10^4 the power-law regime lasts only roughly t in [2, 300] before synchronization (`0809.4139v2`, p.5, p.9).
2. The tail is not a single law but a mixture: splitting the saving propensity into ten bins of width 0.1 gives mostly exponential partial distributions whose overlap reproduces the power law (`physics/0504153v2`, p.13).
3. Fitting an agent-based model to stylized facts does not identify its parameters: beta and gamma in the extended Chiarella model enter the same term, so both cannot be calibrated and beta is fixed instead (`2208.14207v1`, p.10).
4. A discretized agent-based model's continuum limit is not unique, and the naive explicit scheme blows up where the continuous system provably does not (`1904.04951v3`, p.2, p.9, p.14).
5. A single investor who re-optimizes a backtested parameter never converges and destabilizes prices even when every other trader is static (`2202.00831v1`, p.5, p.6).
6. Trade-acceptance rules that do not change the equilibrium are not identifiable from it; only the relaxation time moves (`1505.01274v1`, p.6).
7. The zero-intelligence limit-order book has closed-form scalings - spread s = v/lambda and diffusion D = v^3/lambda^2 when cancellations dominate - and the published diffusion law for the market-order-dominated case was wrong (`2510.01814v1`, p.20, p.21).
8. Herding alone generates the leverage effect with an exponential decay of about 19 days, and ablating the asymmetry removes it (`1407.5258v1`, p.6, p.7).
9. Interaction structure changes the distribution: on a network the stationary mean wealth is proportional to degree, and a preference for trading with similar wealth splits the population into classes with no steady state (`0809.4139v2`, p.7; `2305.16238v1`, p.8).
10. Coordination has critical points: minority-game predictability collapses below alpha_c = 0.3374, and a fully crowd-avoiding queue serves fewer customers than random choice (`1305.2121v3`, p.10, p.18).

## 1. Wealth distributions: an exponential body with a Pareto tail

The canonical kinetic exchange model gives N agents wealths {x_n} and, at each step, picks two agents at random to exchange a conserved amount dx (`physics/0504153v2`, p.3). With no saving the dynamics converges to a Boltzmann (exponential) distribution (pp.3-4). Adding a global saving propensity 0 < lambda < 1 makes the equilibrium a Gamma density with a positive mode, f(x -> 0) -> 0, whose effective dimension is D_lambda/2 = (1 + 2lambda)/(1 - lambda) and whose effective temperature is T_lambda = [(1 - lambda)/(1 + 2lambda)] <x>, reducing to the Boltzmann law at D_lambda = 2 (pp.5-6). Quenched, heterogeneous saving propensities instead produce a power-law tail with Pareto exponent alpha = 1 (p.8), against a background estimate that real Pareto exponents lie between 1 and 2 (p.3). The empirical story quoted by `2305.16238v1` is the same two-part shape: wealth and income decay exponentially for 90-95 percent of people in any country, while the top tail follows P(w) proportional to w^-alpha with alpha around 2 (p.1).

The "immediate exchange" model reaches the same family from a different rule. Two agents swap fractions eps_j x_j and eps_k x_k drawn uniformly, and the equilibrium is a Gamma density with shape alpha = beta = 2, that is f_2(x) = 4x exp(-2x) with mode 1/2 and f_2(0) = 0 (`1505.01274v1`, p.3). That is exactly the equilibrium of the Chakraborti-Chakrabarti saving model at saving propensity lambda = 1/4 (p.4), and the fitted shape alpha around 2 sits inside the range (2, 2.5) reported for household income distributions (p.3). The point for a platform operator is that these distributions are generated by the exchange rule, not fitted to markets; they are hypotheses about mechanism, and the mechanism is thin.

## 2. The Pareto tail is an overlap of exponentials, and it is transient

`physics/0504153v2` explains the power law structurally. When the individual saving propensities are sliced, each slice produces a partial distribution that is essentially exponential; the sum, or overlap, of these exponentials reproduces the power law (p.13). The mean wealth of a slice diverges as <x(lambda)> = kappa/(1 - lambda) with kappa = 0.1128, so higher-propensity agents sit further out (p.9). But the power law is fragile in both x and t: for N = 1000 agents the distribution looks power-law-like at t between 2 x 10^6 and 2 x 10^7 trades and then develops structure, keeping the shape only at small x (p.12), and isolated peaks at large x appear that are not observed in real data (p.14). A smoother tail needs N = 10^6 agents and 10^12 trades (p.15).

`0809.4139v2` turns this into a breakdown of the mean-field approximation itself. In the Bouchaud-Mezard wealth redistribution model with multiplicative noise of strength sigma, the mean-field stationary law has tail exponent 2 + 1/sigma^2 (p.3), so a realistic exponent near 2 requires sigma approximately 1 (p.3). The exact moment equations say otherwise. The free regime ends at t1 = 1/(2(1 - sigma^2)) (p.3); correlations then grow and the synchronized regime arrives at t2 = (1 - sigma^2) N (p.4) and t3 = (1 - sigma^2) N/(2 sigma^2) (p.5). For 10^4 agents the power-law window is roughly t in [2, 300], and by t > 10^4 the system is synchronized (p.5). The conclusion is stark: for any finite N there is no stationary wealth distribution, the power-law tail is transient, and P(v_A < x) approaches 1 for any x > 0 (p.9). The variance of the average wealth grows without limit. The authors note that the result depends on the order of the limits N -> infinity and t -> infinity (p.9), and they suggest that a taxation term r(1 - v_i) dt is what restores a nontrivial stationary solution for sigma < 1 (p.10). Any firm-level or account-level tail estimated from a finite panel inherits this caveat.

## 3. Gibrat's law: the lognormal null for firm size

`1606.04796v1` derives the continuous limit of Gibrat's law of proportionate effect, in which a firm's size changes by a random amount proportional to its current size (p.2). The limit is a linear diffusion equation whose source-type solution is exactly a lognormal density (p.9). Two numbers matter. First, the variance of that lognormal grows as e^{2t} - 1 (p.9), and the moments of order n grow at rate n(n - 1) (p.9), so the distribution spreads without bound under pure Gibrat dynamics. Second, the convergence to that source-type solution is bounded by C/sqrt(1 + 2t) for a large class of initial data, a sharp rate (p.10). A different small-perturbation limit gives the opposite end state: the first-order model converges to a Dirac delta at zero, the winner-takes-all limit in which one firm absorbs the rest (p.6). Gibrat's law is therefore a useful null for size and growth series - fit lognormal first, and claim a heavier tail only if the lognormal fails - but it has no stabilizing mechanism of its own.

## 4. Interaction structure, not the exchange rule, sets the distribution

Three papers here say that who trades with whom matters more than how the trade is priced. On a general exchange network, the stationary mean wealth of agent i is proportional to its degree, <v_i> = k_i/z with z the average degree (p.7), so a power-law wealth distribution can arise purely as a topological effect of a scale-free degree distribution (p.7). For networks of small average degree the power-law regime is brief or absent (p.9), and the authors directly contradict Bouchaud and Mezard's earlier numerical claim of stationary power-law tails at z = 4, attributing it to finite-size and finite-time effects (p.9). `1505.01274v1` shows that any acceptance criterion symmetric in the two trading partners leaves the equilibrium distribution unchanged and only changes the relaxation time, so the criterion parameter is not identifiable from the equilibrium at all (p.6); an asymmetric pro-rich criterion that blocks a fraction theta of pro-poor trades produces a non-Gamma distribution that diverges as theta approaches 0.9 (p.7); and a mixture that gives a large acceptance parameter to 95 percent of agents and smaller parameters to 5 percent reproduces a realistic Pareto index near 2 (p.9). `2305.16238v1` replaces the delta-distance preference with a locality constraint: if two agents trade only when |v - w| <= delta, the society splits into a poor and a rich class with no steady state as delta shrinks (p.8). On its Lorenz curves, at delta = 10 about 10 percent of agents hold at least 40 percent of the wealth, and at delta = 5 at least 10 percent hold at least 70 percent (p.9). More frequent re-sorting of neighbours pushes the wealthiest agent's share up and the second-wealthiest down, a quasi-oligarchy rather than a true oligarchy (p.5). Distribution is insensitive to the number of agents but changes with the average wealth level (p.11). The authors label the work a pilot study (p.12).

## 5. Herding, the leverage effect and the eigen-structure of returns

`1407.5258v1` builds a daily agent model with N = 10000 agents whose trading probability and herding degree both depend asymmetrically on the previous return (pp.3-4, p.6). The two key parameters are read from market data rather than fitted to the simulation: the volume ratio gives alpha = 1.01 for the S&P 500 and 1.09 for Shanghai from V+/V- of 1.03 and 1.21 (p.5), and the herding asymmetry maps linearly as delta_R = 38.2 delta_r, giving delta_R = 3 and 2 (pp.5-6). The model then reproduces the leverage effect for five indices and the anti-leverage effect for Shanghai, with an exponential fit whose decay time is about 19 days for the leverage effect and 8 days for the anti-leverage effect (p.6). The causal check is an ablation: making the herding degree symmetric removes the leverage effect, and the asymmetric trading probability contributes little for the five leverage markets (p.7). The horizon distribution is a power law, rho_i proportional to i^-1.12 (p.3), and the authors' explicit methodological stance is that parameters determined from historical data are better than parameters obtained by fitting simulation output (pp.4-5, p.7).

`1504.01811v1` extends the same idea to three levels - agent herding within a stock, within a sector, and across the market - with 150 stocks in 5 sectors and N = 600000 agents (p.5, p.7). Calibrating the co-movement parameters on NYSE stocks from 1990-2006 and HKSE stocks from 2003-2011 (p.7) gives H_M = 0.363 for NYSE and 0.306 for HKSE (p.14). The claim is that the three largest correlation-matrix eigenvalues match between the real and simulated markets: NYSE (26.01, 7.45, 5.13) against simulated (24.62, 7.93, 3.82), HKSE (21.70, 3.06, 1.89) against (21.43, 4.57, 2.97) (p.13). Results are reported as robust to the number of agents (450000 to 750000) and the run length (800 to 1200 days) (p.7). Unlike its companion, this paper reports no formal goodness-of-fit or p-values; the match of the eigenvalue spectrum is the whole argument.

## 6. The zero-intelligence limit-order book: an exact null model

`2510.01814v1` revisits the Santa Fe zero-intelligence limit-order book, in which limit submissions arrive as a Poisson process of intensity lambda*Delta, cancellations at intensity v, and unit market orders at intensity mu (pp.3-5). The contribution is a systematic derivation: an exact master equation, an exact first-order BBGKY hierarchy, and a mean-field Boltzmann equation, with the boundary condition rho_t^a(0) = lambda/(v + mu) derived exactly in the small-tick-size limit and without the mean-field approximation (p.18). The resulting closed scalings are the useful part. When cancellations dominate (mu << v) the spread is s = v/lambda, the diffusion constant is D = v^3/lambda^2 and the instantaneous price impact is v/(2lambda); when market orders dominate (mu >> v) these become s = mu/lambda, D = mu^3/lambda^2 and mu/(2lambda) (pp.20-21). The paper also corrects an error in the earlier dimensional analysis: the market-order-dominated diffusion constant is mu^3/lambda^2, not the v*mu^2/lambda^2 implied by the published argument (p.21). Monte Carlo runs use lambda = 10000, v = 1.0, Delta = 10^-6, cutoff L = 10^6 and mu in [10^-2, 10^3] (p.22). The mean-field prediction is essentially exact for small mu, but for large mu only the exponents survive while coefficients are off, and the large-mu price dynamics show significant mean reversion - they are non-Markovian, so permanent and instantaneous impact need not coincide (pp.22-23). The authors explicitly frame the zero-intelligence book as a null model to be established before strategic behaviour is added (p.2).

## 7. Opinion, coordination and the minority mechanism

`1305.2121v3` reviews statistical-mechanics models of competitive resource allocation: N agents choosing among R resources (p.3). The minority game has control parameter alpha = P/N, and its scaled fluctuations sigma^2/N have a clear minimum near alpha = 0.4 (pp.6-7); the analytic critical point is alpha_c = 0.3374 (p.10), with predictability H vanishing as (alpha - alpha_c)^2 near it (p.10). A simpler P = 1 learning model has a critical learning rate gamma_c = 2, above which the population bimodally herds and fluctuations scale as N^2 (p.5). The Kolkata Paise Restaurant problem gives the coordination counterpart: random choice serves a utilization fraction f = 1 - exp(-g), about 0.63 at g = 1 (p.18); a fully crowd-avoiding rule serves about 0.46, worse than random, while a stochastic crowd-avoiding rule reaches about 0.79 (p.18). Two methodological lessons transfer directly. First, transient warnings matter: the review advises warming up for about 2000P/alpha iterations and averaging over a comparable window, and warns that the standard estimate is too small near a critical point (p.6). Second, only the first two moments of the strategy distribution enter the thermodynamic limit, so a measured alpha_c does not uniquely fix the underlying strategy distribution (p.12). And the review's own falsification caveat is blunt: reverse-engineering an agent population from an observed series has been done for synthetic minority-game data but has never been compared against real traders (p.21).

## 8. Calibration: degeneracy, non-unique limits and surrogate discipline

`2208.14207v1` calibrates the extended Chiarella model, reduced to three agents - one fundamental, one momentum, one noise - against 75 stocks on Nasdaq, the London Stock Exchange and the Hong Kong exchange, on minute data from 20 September to 30 November 2021 (p.5, p.6). It fits alpha, gamma and sigma_N while fixing rho = 0.1 and beta = 10 (p.10), minimizing a stylized-facts distance D = KS + V + ACF1 + ACF2 with all four weights set to 1 (pp.9-10). The paper states its own degeneracy clearly: beta and gamma appear only in the product gamma*tanh(beta*M_t), so estimating both is unreliable and fixing beta is what makes the calibration robust (p.10). The optimizer is an XGBoost surrogate trained on a Sobol pool of 16384 parameter combinations starting from 2000 evaluated points (p.11); the surrogate's error at the predicted optimum is under 5 percent (p.12) and mostly under 10 percent across stocks (p.15). The calibrated model beats an expectation-maximisation baseline on all 25 Hong Kong names and 22 of 25 Nasdaq names, with a distance about 10 percent smaller (p.13) - but a geometric Brownian motion already produces essentially zero squared-return autocorrelation, so the comparison is against a very low bar (p.16).

`1904.04951v3` attacks the same problem at the mathematical level for the Levy-Levy-Solomon and Franke-Westerhoff models (p.1). Its central caveat is that the continuum limit is not uniquely defined when one starts from the difference equations that the literature actually writes down (p.2, p.9). The time-scaling choice changes conclusions: roughly 90 percent of optimal investment decisions sit on the boundary of [0.01, 0.99] in the original formulation (p.10); with memory scaled by the time step the share of extreme decisions falls to 72 percent at dt = 0.1 and all decisions become interior at dt = 0.01, while with fixed memory it stays near 90 percent for any dt and prices oscillate (p.10). A one-parameter perturbation of the fundamentalist noise from 0.79 to 1.15 makes the explicit scheme blow up (pp.12-13), and the authors prove that the blow-up is introduced by the discretization, not inherited from the continuous system (p.14), then give a semi-implicit scheme that stays in the invariant set for any step size and any number of steps (p.15). The mean-field limit converges with an explicit exponential-in-time rate (p.19). No parameter is statistically estimated here at all; the values are imported from the literature (p.9, p.12).

## 9. Optimization instability: when the strategy adapts, the market does not settle

`2202.00831v1` is the most directly operational paper in the slice. One stock trades in a continuous double auction with 1000 normal agents plus a momentum technical agent and a reversal technical agent (p.3). The technical agent's parameter is chosen by particle-swarm optimization that backtests over the whole simulation run, and the random numbers are fixed so that an unchanged parameter reproduces the run exactly (p.4). The result is that the momentum parameter never converges to a stable value; it cycles (p.5), and with both technical agents present the parameter changes irregularly and the styling of the four regimes becomes unpredictable (p.5). Prices are destabilized relative to the no-technical-agent case (p.4), and the paper's headline conclusion is that a single investor optimizing through backtesting makes the market unstable even when every other trader is static (pp.5-6). The simulation reproduces fat tails (kurtosis 18.341) and volatility clustering (squared-return autocorrelation of 0.060 at lag 1) without the technical agents at all (p.7), and the authors argue a model need not reproduce specific stylized-fact values because those values are unstable across assets and periods (p.7). One technical agent's profit is not taken from the other's - both increase profits (p.6). This is the ABM analogue of a crowded strategy losing its edge, and it is a warning about any evaluation that optimizes a parameter by replaying a fixed history.

## Caveats

Five of the twelve papers contain no market data at all: `physics/0504153v2`, `0809.4139v2`, `1505.01274v1`, `1606.04796v1` and `2305.16238v1` are Monte Carlo exercises that cite real-data numbers only as background (for example the Pareto exponent between 1 and 2 at `physics/0504153v2` p.3, and sigma near 1 at `0809.4139v2` p.3). `1305.2121v3` is a review with no dataset, `1904.04951v3` performs no statistical calibration at all (p.9, p.12), and `2510.01814v1` is a theory paper validated against its own simulation (p.22). The herding results are single-family and single-period: `1407.5258v1` uses six indices over unequal windows spanning 1950-2012 (p.4), and `1504.01811v1` reduces every investor to a holder of exactly one stock (p.3) with no significance tests. `2208.14207v1` has three agents, two and a half months of minute data and a fixed rho (p.5, p.6, p.10); `2202.00831v1` is one stock and one parameter and states explicitly that it is not a forecast of real markets (p.2). The slice disagrees with itself in two places: `0809.4139v2` contradicts Bouchaud and Mezard's stationary power law at average degree 4 (p.9), and the kinetic and herding literatures disagree about whether the distribution is set by the exchange rule or by the interaction topology. What no paper here establishes is that any of these distributions predicts returns, that any agent-based model fits live trading data out of sample, or that a parameter recovered from one market transfers to another. The calibration papers validate against stylized facts, and `2202.00831v1` shows that stylized-fact recovery happens without any strategic agents at all (p.7). Treat this slice as evidence about measurement and evaluation design, not as alpha.

## Papers read in depth

- `physics/0504153v2` - Kinetic theory models for the distribution of wealth: power law from overlap of exponentials (2005). Explains the Pareto tail as an overlap of Gamma partial distributions; simulation-only with no market fit.
- `0809.4139v2` - Breakdown of the mean-field approximation in a wealth distribution model (2008). Shows the stationary wealth law is not stationary for finite N; the strongest result, but it is a model result, not an observation.
- `1505.01274v1` - Kinetic models of immediate exchange (2015). Immediate exchange gives a Gamma equilibrium invariant to symmetric acceptance rules; the invariance is a non-identifiability result, not an empirical one.
- `1606.04796v1` - Kinetic and mean field description of Gibrat's law (2016). Rigorous diffusion limit and lognormal source-type solution for firm size; no data.
- `2305.16238v1` - Role of Neighbouring Wealth Preference in Kinetic Exchange model of market (2023). A wealth-similarity preference creates classes and destroys the steady state; the authors call it a pilot study.
- `1407.5258v1` - Agent-based model with asymmetric trading and herding for complex financial systems (2014). Calibrates parameters from market data and reproduces leverage and anti-leverage; six indices over uneven windows.
- `1504.01811v1` - Agent-based model with multi-level herding for complex financial systems (2015). Three-level herding reproduces correlation eigen-structure on NYSE and HKSE; no formal goodness-of-fit.
- `2510.01814v1` - Mean-field theory of the Santa Fe model revisited (2025). Closed ZI-book scalings and an exact boundary condition, correcting an earlier diffusion law; mean-field fails for market-order-dominated regimes.
- `1305.2121v3` - Statistical Mechanics of Competitive Resource Allocation using Agent-based Models (2013). Physics Reports review of minority games and crowd-avoiding queues; no empirical dataset.
- `2208.14207v1` - Calibrating the extended Chiarella model (2022). Stylized-facts distance with an XGBoost surrogate across 75 stocks; beta/gamma degeneracy and only three agents.
- `1904.04951v3` - Robust Mathematical Formulation and Probabilistic Description of Agent-Based Computational Economic Market Models (2019). Proves the continuum limit is non-unique and the naive discretization blows up; no statistical calibration.
- `2202.00831v1` - Instability of financial markets by optimizing investment strategies investigated by an agent-based model (2022). One optimizing investor destabilizes a static market; one stock, one parameter, not a forecast.
