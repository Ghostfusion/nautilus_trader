# Order flow, high-frequency trading and latency: speed is paid at the tape, not inside the book

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers the general-finance evidence on high-frequency trading, latency and order flow: the measured activity of high-speed traders in real exchange data, the venue mechanics that turn millisecond differences into money, order-flow information beyond the best quotes, and the newer regime work on high-frequency flow. Its corpus slice is the arXiv q-fin.GN harvest: 29 records matching this topic across 2008-2026, of which only 2 carry a journal reference. Ten papers were read in depth from the PDFs, with a page marker on every number. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets, and its purpose is to change what the platform models, records and checks, not to sell a strategy. The companion briefs in `strategies/books` cover the same ground from the q-fin.TR harvest; this one takes the general-finance evidence and the prediction-market records that the trading corpus does not, so it may be read alongside them without repeating them.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from real exchange measurements of what high-frequency traders actually do, through the venue rules that make a millisecond decisive, into order-flow information beyond the best bid and ask, and finally into the newest and least understood venue class: on-chain prediction markets. Its single most consequential finding is that high-frequency activity is not a diffuse background but the mechanism that writes the cross-security price response itself: in a NASDAQ dataset that flags HFT in 120 stocks, an overwhelming majority of the initial response of one stock to another's move is attributable to HFT activity, and the cross-sectional correlation of a stock with the market rises with its HFT volume share (correlation 0.80, R^2 0.64) (`1211.1919v1`, p.2, p.5). The second most consequential finding is the flip side: in the 5,140 mini flash crashes measured across four volatile months of 2008-2010, 86.98% of the trades were marked as Intermarket Sweep Orders and 67.85% of the crashes were ISO-initiated, which locates the failure not in human error but in a venue rule that protects only the top of the book (`1211.6667v1`, p.8, p.11).

## The short answer

1. In NASDAQ data that flags HFT in 120 stocks, HFT activity supplies the overwhelming majority of the initial cross-stock price response, and a stock's 30-second correlation with the equal-weighted market average rises with its HFT volume share (correlation 0.80, R^2 0.64) (`1211.1919v1`).
2. Cross-stock price incorporation accelerated by roughly two orders of magnitude in a decade: several minutes in 2000, about 1 minute in 2005, less than 10 seconds in 2010 (`1211.1919v1`).
3. Mini flash crashes are a venue-rule artefact: of 5,140 crashes in four volatile months of 2008-2010, 67.85% were ISO-initiated and 86.98% of the trades inside the crashes were ISO-marked (`1211.6667v1`).
4. The crash aftermath is a durable liquidity shock, not a tick: NBBO spread rose 141.60% and exchange spread 188.05%, and locked or crossed NBBO quotes went from 8.01% to 24.40% (`1211.6667v1`).
5. Fleeting liquidity is present in 37.99% of the crashes, meaning the displayed quote at the NBBO was cancelled faster than the consolidated tape could carry the removal (`1211.6667v1`).
6. Order flow beyond the top of the book carries information: adding deeper levels improves the out-of-sample RMSE of a mid-price regression by about 65-75% for large-tick and 15-30% for small-tick Nasdaq stocks, and for the small-tick names more than half of order-flow activity happens beyond the best quotes (`1907.06230v2`).
7. Correcting for collinearity flips the conclusion about deep flow: multivariate OLS leaves deep-level coefficients weakly significant while Ridge makes them strongly significant, which is a warning about the estimator, not the market (`1907.06230v2`).
8. Prediction-market book feeds cannot reconstruct the aggressor sign: feed-inferred direction matches on-chain ground truth only about 59% of the time, against roughly 81% for Lee-Ready on Nasdaq, and effective half-spread sign-flips on 67% of markets (`2604.24366v2`).
9. Polymarket's microstructure is an order of magnitude more expensive and flatter than equities: about 200 basis points median quoted half-spread in the central price decile, top-of-book carrying 0.136 of cumulative top-10 depth against a 0.10 uniform null, and about 32 effective makers per market (`2604.24366v2`).
10. Prediction-market informed-trading detection is layered rather than unified: account-level skill testing needs at least ten events and therefore classifies one-shot insiders as "lucky winners", while the lifecycle heuristic flags 1,950 accounts of unmeasured precision (`2605.02287v2`).
11. High-frequency flow has regimes: crypto automated-market-maker LOG-price potentials are double-well for two of three two-month windows at sampling frequencies under an hour, and revert to single-well for windows of one month or less (`2509.02941v1`).

## 1. Speed is paid at the tape: HFT and price synchronisation

The strongest real-data result in the slice is that high-frequency trading is what makes related securities move together. Using a NASDAQ dataset that flags HFT activity in 120 stocks during the last week of February 2010, and taking the 40 largest, the average normalised price response of stock i to a price movement in stock j is decomposed into HFT, non-HFT and uncategorised components; an overwhelming majority of the initial price response is due to HFT activity (`1211.1919v1`, p.2). The same paper sets the scale of the activity: HFT accounts for approximately 55% of trading volume in US equity markets and 40% in European equity markets (`1211.1919v1`, p.1). The timescale of synchronisation collapsed within a decade: in 2000 it took several minutes for a price movement in stock j to be incorporated into stock i, in 2005 about 1 minute, and in 2010 less than 10 seconds (`1211.1919v1`, p.2).

The cross-sectional evidence is the part a portfolio construction process can use directly. Across the 120 stocks, the correlation between each stock's 30-second returns and the equal-weighted average 30-second returns of all 120 has a Pearson correlation of 0.80 with the stock's HFT volume share, and a linear fit gives R^2 = 0.64 (`1211.1919v1`, p.5). The correlation network, largely set by HFT, reproduces the GICS sector structure (`1211.1919v1`, p.5). In the paper's two-security model, synchronisation lowers the cost of a buyer-initiated transaction from 0.5 to 0.35 and average pricing error from 0.75 to 0.4375 in the worked example (p.4), and reduces informed traders' average profit per transaction from 0.5 to 0.46875 (p.3), which is how it can lower costs and still leave profits for the fast (`1211.1919v1`).

The companion simulation isolates the mechanism rather than the magnitude. In a zero-intelligence continuous double auction with two venues for an identical security, the run of 100 simulations of 10,000 time steps gives an average transaction price of 100.5 in both cases, but volatility falls from 60.1 (standard deviation 0.6) to 47.4 (0.4), volume rises from 2,255 (33) to 3,580 (6), and the probability that a submitted order transacts rises from 0.226 to 0.358 when the venues are connected (`1311.4160v1`, p.6). The equilibrium is set at 100.5 by construction, so the volatility reduction is excess-volatility reduction, and the paper itself calls the zero-intelligence and uniform-price assumptions simplifications (`1311.4160v1`, p.2, p.3, p.6). The limit on both papers is that the fast mechanism is an assumption, not an identified strategy: `1211.1919v1` observes HFT flags on one week of one venue, and `1311.4160v1` has no data at all.

## 2. The mini flash crash is an ISO sweep

The most operationally sharp evidence in the slice is a forensic study of mini flash crashes, defined as a price moving at least 10 ticks in one direction within 1.5 seconds and by more than 0.8% (`1211.6667v1`, p.5). The dataset is every quote disseminated by the Consolidated Quotation System and every trade on the Consolidated Tape System, timestamped to 25 milliseconds, for September to November 2008 and May 2010: 5,140 crashes, 2,760 up and 2,380 down, covering 27.75% of all mini flash crashes reported in 2006-2011 (`1211.6667v1`, p.6).

The crash mechanic is not a mystery once the trade flags are read. Of the 5,140 crashes, 3,488 (67.85%) are ISO-initiated, 238 (4.64%) are auto-routing-initiated and 1,414 (27.51%) are unclassified; ISO-marked trades are 86.98% of the trades inside the crashes (`1211.6667v1`, p.8, p.11). The average crash moves 2.45% in 413 milliseconds, on 14,352.39 shares across 18.94 trades; the Goldman Sachs example on 7 October 2008 dropped 1.6% in under 400 milliseconds on 58 trades of which the last 57 were ISO-marked, for 86,000 shares and a $1.89 move (`1211.6667v1`, p.3, p.8). The authors' reading is that Regulation NMS protects only the top of the book, so a sweep can print far from the NBBO while deeper liquidity exists elsewhere; they count 13 public exchanges, more than 30 dark pools and over 200 internalising broker-dealers in the US equity market (`1211.6667v1`, p.19).

The consequence for a trading system is the aftermath, which is measurable and persistent. In the 60 seconds after a crash the NBBO spread rises from 0.24% to 0.58% (an increase of 141.60%) and the exchange spread from 0.46% to 1.32% (188.05%) (`1211.6667v1`, p.13). The share of locked or crossed NBBO quotes averages 8.01% in the 60 seconds before the crash, jumps to 24.40% and declines to 8.23% after (`1211.6667v1`, p.14). Quoted volume at the NBBO falls asymmetrically, from 1,575.05 to 1,272.05 shares on the bid (-19.23%) and from 1,323.49 to 1,210.30 on the offer (-9.35%) (`1211.6667v1`, p.15). Fleeting liquidity, defined as the SIP-disseminated quote not being hit during the crash, occurs in 1,953 crashes, 37.99% of the sample, and a logit regression finds it is characteristic of ultrafast crashes of small volume and few trades (coefficients -0.2065 on time, -4.6876E-6 on volume and -0.0067 on trade count, classification precision 54.80%) (`1211.6667v1`, p.19).

Two supporting numbers show the money at stake and the boundary of the evidence. On 6 May 2010 ISO volume was 32% of total volume but accounted for over 50% of the contribution to price variance (`1211.6667v1`, p.5). Separately, the paper cites McInish and Upson's estimate that fast liquidity suppliers earn more than $233 million per year at the expense of slow traders (`1211.6667v1`, p.17). The study is a forensic event study on four volatile months, so its frequencies describe stress periods rather than a normal month, and the crash classifier leaves 27.51% of events unclassified.

## 3. Order flow deeper in the book carries information

The base fact is that the best-quote order-flow imbalance explains most of the contemporaneous mid-price change, and the interesting question is whether anything deeper adds. Using LOBSTER event data for six Nasdaq stocks over all of 2016, restricted to 10:00-15:30 and with hidden orders excluded, and fitting 11 thirty-minute blocks times 180 ten-second windows, i.e. 2,772 regressions per stock, the level-1 OFI slope is statistically significant in 100% of samples for every one of the six names with a mean t-statistic between 11.86 and 30.08 (`1907.06230v2`, p.10, p.13, p.14). The baseline cited from the original OFI work is a mean R^2 of about 65% with significance in about 98% of samples (`1907.06230v2`, p.2).

The multi-level version changes the conclusion. Constructing the multi-level order-flow imbalance vector and comparing out-of-sample RMSE, including deeper levels improves the fit by about 65-75% for large-tick stocks and about 15-30% for small-tick stocks (`1907.06230v2`, p.1). The economic intuition is visible in the raw activity split: for the small-tick names more than half of all order-flow events occur beyond the level-1 quotes (53.10% of Amazon's events, 53.42% of Netflix's) against 29.75% for Oracle (`1907.06230v2`, p.12). The stocks span a mean mid-price from $699.22 (Amazon) to $14.16 (Micron) and a mean spread from $0.367 to $0.011, so relative tick size is the variable that decides where the flow lives (`1907.06230v2`, p.11).

The estimator is part of the finding. Multivariate OLS on the multi-level vector produces weak individual significance - for Amazon the level-2 coefficient has a mean t-statistic of 2.43 with only 60% of samples significant at 95% - because the level flows are correlated; Ridge regression makes even the deep-level parameters strongly significant and the authors argue OLS overfits for some stocks (`1907.06230v2`, p.2, p.15). The limits are that this is six liquid Nasdaq names on a single venue in a single year, with the LOBSTER feed covering only Nasdaq, so the result is about the primary venue and not the consolidated market.

## 4. Dark pools sort and amplify, so the sign is conditional

The slice's dark-pool record is theory, and its value is that it explains why the empirical literature cannot agree on a sign. The institutional backdrop it reports: the US dark-pool share of consolidated volume grew from 7.51% in 2008 to 16.57% in 2015, with about 300 equity venues in the US of which 11 are exchanges and 40 active dark pools as of June 2015, and dark liquidity at 26.2% of traded value in Australia in June 2015 (`1612.08486v1`, p.2, p.6). Execution probability in dark pools is cited at 2-4% in one study and 4.11% for NYSE-listed and 2.17% for NASDAQ-listed against 31.47% and 26.48% on the corresponding exchanges in another (`1612.08486v1`, p.8). Dark volume in total was 43.9% of consolidated volume in Q2 2015, split into retail-wholesaler 40.1%, dark-pool 39.7% and hidden exchange 20.2%, and dark-pool ownership shares were 55.28% bank-affiliated, 24.11% independent, 13.79% market-maker and 6.82% consortium (`1612.08486v1`, p.10, p.11).

The model's contribution is two effects. First a sorting effect: informed traders with strong signals trade on the exchange because they want guaranteed execution, those with moderate signals trade in the dark pool because they prefer the better price, and those with weak signals do not trade, with the mirror sorting on the liquidity side (`1612.08486v1`, p.3). Second an amplification effect: when information precision is high the majority of informed flow stays on the exchange and adding a dark pool improves exchange price discovery, while when precision is low the informed migrate to the dark pool and discovery is impaired (`1612.08486v1`, p.1, p.3). The paper also predicts an inverted-U relation between the information content of dark trades and liquidity (`1612.08486v1`, p.4). No number in this paper is its own measurement - every count above is cited from industry data or other papers - so it should be read as a conditional prediction about which regime a venue is in, not as evidence about a level.

## 5. Prediction-market microstructure must be measured on chain

Polymarket is now a large enough venue to measure at scale, and the measurement itself is the finding. The archive covers 52 calendar days to 15 April 2026: 30,287,264,368 order-book events over 385,198 markets, of which 255,425,405 on-chain fills are matched over a 28-day window (`2604.24366v2`, p.2, p.6). The venue is a hybrid: matching happens off chain and settlement happens on chain, so `change_side` in the feed records which side of the book moved, not which side initiated the trade (`2604.24366v2`, p.4).

That distinction destroys a whole class of measures. Feed-inferred trade direction agrees with on-chain ground truth only about 59% of the time (volume-weighted 0.592, panel mean 0.615), against roughly 81% for the Lee-Ready algorithm on Nasdaq; on the comparable market subset, effective half-spread sign-flips on 67% of markets (16 of 24) and Kyle's lambda on 60% (15 of 25), falling to 50% and 43% in a second non-overlapping window (`2604.24366v2`, p.4, p.12, p.13). After the sign is fixed from the chain, a Glosten-Harris decomposition of the top-100 markets leaves a median effective half-spread essentially at zero (-0.0003 probability points) with a median adverse-selection component of 0.0 (`2604.24366v2`, p.11).

The quoted-level facts are worth carrying into a venue model. The median quoted half-spread is about 200 basis points in the central price decile, against single-digit basis points for liquid US equities post-decimalisation (`2604.24366v2`, p.4); by mid-price decile it is about 400 basis points in [0.4, 0.6] and 1,300-1,800 basis points for markets trading below 0.10, an order of magnitude wider than racetrack longshot premia (`2604.24366v2`, p.7). Depth is flatter than folklore: the top level carries a median 0.136 of cumulative top-10 depth against a 0.10 uniform null, with a median per-market Kullback-Leibler divergence of 0.087 from uniform, and only 9% of markets are strongly top-heavy (`2604.24366v2`, p.7). Maker concentration is low: median volume-weighted Herfindahl index 0.031, i.e. about 32 effective makers, with a p90 of 0.119 and a maximum of 0.40 (`2604.24366v2`, p.9).

Two measurement hygiene results apply to any on-chain venue. Archive-ingestion latency has a median of 41.5 milliseconds with a p90 of 166 milliseconds and a p99 of 6,108 milliseconds, and the authors are explicit that this is the collector pipeline rather than the exchange (`2604.24366v2`, p.9). The self-counterparty wash share has a median of 0.97% with a p90 of 4.5%, a p99 of 10.6% and a maximum of 22.2%, and this is an explicit lower bound because the detector only catches direct self-matches and 128-block flipped pairs (`2604.24366v2`, p.10). Finally, a cross-sectional regression that looks like a time-to-close effect is not one: the coefficient on log seconds-to-close falls from 0.818 in a bivariate specification to 0.008 (HC3 standard error 0.102, R^2 0.57, n = 313) once log market duration, log p(1-p) and log volume are included (`2604.24366v2`, p.11).

## 6. Informed-trading detection in prediction markets needs layers

The complementary record is a methodological comparison of three detection frameworks applied to the same Polymarket population, and its value is that it separates what each layer can and cannot see. Layer one, a wallet-market composite screen, covers more than 210,000 wallet-market pairs and estimates about $143 million in aggregate anomalous profit (`2605.02287v2`, p.1). Layer two, a sign-randomisation skill classifier over 1.72 million accounts, 210,322 markets and $13.76 billion of 2023-2025 volume, re-runs each account's history 10,000 times with the buy/sell direction randomised at the event level: 3.14% of accounts (54,477) are classified as skilled winners, 6.4% as unskilled losers, 29.0% as lucky winners and 61.4% as unlucky losers, with a separate 0.1% market-maker category, and skilled winners plus market makers capturing more than 30% of platform gains (`2605.02287v2`, p.1, p.6). Skill persists out of sample at 44%, against roughly 10% retention in published studies of equity mutual-fund managers (`2605.02287v2`, p.1, p.6).

Layer three, a single-event lifecycle-and-conviction heuristic, flags 1,950 accounts created within seven days before an event that place no further trades after resolution and clear $1,000 of volume and $1,000 of realised profit; their mean realised profit is $15,012.92 and median $2,758.29, and their order imbalance predicts next-period price changes (t = 2.54) and final outcomes (t = 8.65) (`2605.02287v2`, p.8). The critical scope limit is stated by the authors being compared: the skill classifier needs at least ten events, so a one-shot informed trader is excluded by design and the three accounts flagged in the Maduro case study appear as "lucky winners" (`2605.02287v2`, p.8, p.14). The 1,950 flag has unknown precision against any external labelled set, and the sample is category-heterogeneous - sports is 42.05% of markets and 35.37% of volume, politics 7.52% and 35.85%, crypto 36.96% and 18.73% - so a platform-wide skill statistic is mechanism-ambiguous (`2605.02287v2`, p.7).

The per-market alternative is a deadline-anchored information-leakage score, which is interpretable as the fraction of the terminal move priced in before a public news event. On the one case study the article-derived anchor gives +0.113 while the resolution-anchored proxy gives -0.331, a swing of 0.444 that straddles zero (`2605.02287v2`, p.10). On the enforcement side, the first US federal prediction-market insider case alleges $409,881 of profit across thirteen trades, and the largest flagged account's realised PnL of $409,882.03 matches the indictment figure to the dollar (`2605.02287v2`, p.13, p.14).

The design record for leveraged event-linked markets closes the loop by listing what each data layer can support. It separates four conduct channels - market-price manipulation, outcome manipulation, resolution manipulation and informed trading - because they have different evidence and different remedies (`2605.10486v2`, p.3, p.4). Its surveillance criteria are blunt about observability: on-chain fills support concentration, timing, counterparty and position analyses but do not reveal unfilled orders or cancellations, and address-level spoofing findings require attributed order placement, modification and cancellation records (`2605.10486v2`, p.6). Its leverage result is a comparative static, not a number: with fixed influence cost, leverage weakly enlarges the profitable influence set and the threshold is (K + D + X) / (C times delta q), but endogenous price impact, convex detection or enforceable position limits can reverse it, which is why the paper refuses a universal safe-leverage figure (`2605.10486v2`, p.4, p.5). The paper performs no new empirical calculation of its own.

## 7. High-frequency flow has regimes, and they are visible in the drift

The newest evidence in the slice is that intraday flow is not one process with one parameter set. Applying the Kramers-Moyal expansion non-parametrically to Uniswap v3 log-prices on Arbitrum - the USDC-WETH pair for 2024 with about 300,000 records and for the first half of 2025, and the WBTC-WETH pair for 2024, each row averaged over 15 transactions with a typical step near 100 seconds and the extreme 0.5% of observations trimmed - the estimated drift is strongly non-linear and the derived potential is distinctly non-quadratic (`2509.02941v1`, p.3, p.4, p.7). For the USDC-WETH 2024 pool, two of the three representative two-month windows produce a double-well potential at sampling frequencies from 10 minutes to 6 hours, and the WBTC-WETH 2024 pool produces single-well but still non-harmonic potentials (`2509.02941v1`, p.5, p.7).

The dependence on the observation window is the part that matters for anyone estimating a signal from intraday data. Above hourly sampling the potential is typically single-well and sometimes has a small local minimum to the right of the global minimum; under an hour the dynamics move slowly between a single-well profile, which the paper reads as a resilient mean-reverting state, and a double-well or multi-well profile, which it reads as a meta-stable state with competing price regimes and a signal of uncertainty or stress (`2509.02941v1`, p.7, p.8). Shorter windows of one month or less flip the pattern back to single-well for all windows, which the paper attributes to insufficient data in the tails (`2509.02941v1`, p.5, p.8). The practical warning is explicit: if a double well and its instanton transitions are real but omitted from a model, the extra volatility they generate will be misattributed to the noise term (`2509.02941v1`, p.10). None of this is calibrated to a tradable instrument, the pool set is three datasets on one chain, and the potential is estimated over two-month windows, so it is evidence that regimes exist rather than a regime-detection rule.

## 8. Influence is a network property, and the event clock sees stress first

Two further records show how the high-frequency flow record changes shape over time. Constructing weekly directed weighted networks from statistically significant Granger-causal links between one-minute Kraken log-returns over January 2020 to March 2025 - 275 weekly windows of 10,080 points, with the traded universe growing from about 30 assets to about 390 and 99.06% of series passing an ADF test at the 1% level, with Benjamini-Hochberg control at q = 0.05 - the tail exponents of normalised one-minute returns are 3.91 and 3.78 for Bitcoin and 4.08 and 3.66 for XRP, and a representative XBT to ETH link reduces residual variance by 3.24% at an optimal lag of 2 (F = 171.4) (`2606.25466v3`, p.2, p.3, p.4, p.6). Node influence is asymmetric: out-strength scales with in-strength as a power law with exponent 0.909 and a 95% confidence interval of [0.852, 0.965] that excludes 1, with R^2 = 0.727, and adding weekly traded volume as a covariate leaves the volume coefficient insignificant (p = 0.178) and the exponent unchanged at 0.903 (`2606.25466v3`, p.7). Removing the first principal component of the return matrix leaves the structure essentially untouched, with Spearman rank correlations of 0.9990 for out-strength and 0.9947 for link weights (`2606.25466v3`, p.9).

The turnover result is the one to carry into a cross-asset model: ETH holds the top rank throughout 2020-2025 while Bitcoin declines relatively, and 17 distinct cryptocurrencies appear at least once among the top five over the five years, which the authors contrast with the super-stable nodes reported in other complex systems (`2606.25466v3`, p.7). In other words, the leader of the influence network is not a stable input, so any strategy that conditions on "the leading asset" is conditioning on a quantity that rotates.

The last record supplies a stress signal computed from the event clock rather than from physical time. Mapping tick data into a directional-change and overshoot framework with twelve thresholds starting at 0.025% (2.5 pips) and doubling at each step, which the empirical scaling laws put at roughly one transition every 5 seconds, and measuring a normalised trajectory-unlikeliness score with H(1) = 0.4604 and H(2) = 0.70818, the paper shows the measure deteriorating before two known FX shocks (`1402.2198v1`, p.5, p.14, p.15). During the 2007 yen carry unwind it indicates a complete loss of market liquidity in the three weeks preceding the 6% drop on 16 August 2007, and during the Swiss National Bank episode it indicates a complete loss of liquidity in the week preceding the near 1000 pip (about 10%) franc gain against the euro, with conditions restored after the 6 September 2011 intervention (`1402.2198v1`, pp.15-16). The theorem behind it is that under Brownian motion the expected overshoot length equals the directional-change threshold independently of volatility, so the score is measuring the shape of the path, not its variance (`1402.2198v1`, p.5). The evidence is two case studies, both FX, both with the benefit of hindsight.

## Caveats

The slice is thin and uneven. Only two of the 29 records carry a journal reference (`2301.13505v2` and `1805.06080v1`), and neither is in the read set: the first is the Lillo-Mike-Farmer test already covered by the companion trading-corpus brief, the second is a law-review note on insider trading without trading data. Three of the ten read papers contribute no new measurement at all - `1612.08486v1` is a pure equilibrium model whose institutional counts are cited from industry data, `2605.10486v2` states explicitly that it performs no new empirical calculation, and `2605.02287v2` is a methodological comparison whose numbers come from two other papers that had not released labels or code at the time of writing. The most on-topic record in the slice, `2608.00858v3` "Data-Driven Measures of High-Frequency Trading", has no local PDF and could not be read, so the brief is missing the one paper whose title promises a direct measure of HFT activity; a further screened record, `2411.14058v1`, was passed over because it uses daily closing prices and reports no effect size despite its high-frequency framing. The real-data papers are single-market or single-period: `1211.1919v1` is one week of February 2010 on 120 NASDAQ stocks, `1211.6667v1` is four volatile months chosen because they contain the most crashes, `1907.06230v2` is six Nasdaq names in one year, `2604.24366v2` is 52 days in 2026 on one venue, `2606.25466v3` is one exchange over 2020-2025, and `2509.02941v1` is three Uniswap v3 pools on one chain. Kraken, Nasdaq and Polymarket are not interchangeable, so no decay rate, spread level or causality direction here should be transferred across venues without re-measurement. The slice also disagrees with itself in a way that matters: `1211.6667v1` attributes mini flash crashes primarily to regulation and fragmentation rather than to HFT conduct, and the programme-level claim in the prediction-market papers is disputed inside the slice - the methodological comparison argues that a platform-wide skill classification conflates sports, politics and crypto populations with entirely different information technologies. On regimes, `2509.02941v1` finds double-well potentials at one sampling frequency and window length and single-well at another, so the existence of the regime is more secure than any rule for detecting it. Finally, nothing here establishes a net-of-cost, capacity-aware profit for any latency strategy; no paper measures live latency on a modern venue, none reports a fee schedule against its measured edge, and none of the prediction-market results include a strategy backtest.

## Papers read in depth

- `1211.1919v1` - High-Frequency Trading Synchronizes Prices in Financial Markets (2012). NASDAQ HFT flags show HFT supplies most of the cross-stock price response; single week, and the efficiency results are model-based.
- `1311.4160v1` - Simulating the Synchronizing Behavior of High-Frequency Trading in Multiple Markets (2013). Quantifies the connection effect on volatility, volume and fill probability in a zero-intelligence simulation; no data.
- `1211.6667v1` - High Frequency Trading and Mini Flash Crashes (2012). The strongest venue-mechanics evidence in the slice: ISO shares, spread and quote shocks, fleeting liquidity; four volatile months only.
- `1907.06230v2` - Multi-Level Order-Flow Imbalance in a Limit Order Book (2019). Shows deep flow carries information and that OLS understates it; six Nasdaq stocks on one venue.
- `1612.08486v1` - Understanding the Impacts of Dark Pools on Price Discovery (2016). Gives the sorting and amplification mechanism that makes the dark-pool sign conditional; theory with no own data.
- `2604.24366v2` - The Anatomy of a Decentralized Prediction Market (2026). 30 billion order-book events and 255 million on-chain fills; establishes that feed-inferred trade direction is unusable and documents eight stylized facts; one venue, 52 days.
- `2605.02287v2` - Per-Market Information Leakage and Order-Flow Skill (2026). Taxonomy of detection layers and their scope limits; numbers are quoted from unreleased third-party studies.
- `2605.10486v2` - Manipulation, Informed Trading, and Regulation in Leveraged Event-Linked Markets (2026). Evidence-layered surveillance design and leverage comparative statics; no new empirical work.
- `2509.02941v1` - Non-Linear and Meta-Stable Dynamics in Financial Markets (2025). Non-parametric evidence of single-well and double-well potentials in crypto AMM flow; three pools, window-dependent results.
- `2606.25466v3` - Time-dependent weighted directed networks of cryptocurrency interaction from high-frequency returns (2026). 275 weekly Granger networks with FDR control; influence is sublinear and leader turnover is high; one exchange.

## Where to next in the corpus
