# Prediction markets and betting: market-implied probabilities that beat the forecasters, and a tape that will fool your microstructure measures

Date: 2026-10-06. Revision 1.

This brief covers how probabilistic claims are priced and calibrated, how the vig and the odds mechanics of betting markets put a floor under any edge, what the on-chain order book of a decentralized prediction market actually looks like, and what these venues teach about illiquid, event-driven instruments. It is written for an operator running a Rust/Python event-driven algorithmic trading platform across equities, futures, FX, crypto, options and prediction markets. Its slice is the 24 arXiv `q-fin.GN` records that match prediction markets and betting, spanning 2009 to 2026, of which exactly 1 carries a journal reference; eight of those records were read in depth from the local PDFs and every number below carries the page marker where it was read.

## What this covers

The slice arcs from 2009 design proposals for prediction markets, through a 2012 theory result on how Kelly bettors make a market aggregate beliefs, through 2017-2019 evidence on how badly people and sportsbooks price a known edge, to a 2026 cluster of papers on Polymarket and Kalshi that finally has tick-level and on-chain data. The single most consequential finding is a measurement one: the public order-book feed of a decentralized central limit order book does not identify which side initiated a trade, so trade direction inferred from it agrees with the on-chain record only about 59% of the time, which is barely above chance and enough to flip the sign of the effective spread and of Kyle's lambda on most markets (`2604.24366v2`). Everything downstream of trade direction on these venues therefore has to be rebuilt on the on-chain trade record before it can be believed.

## The short answer

1. A prediction-market price is a probability you can trade, and on one liquid venue it beat the best public forecaster: Kalshi's market-implied high temperature had 9.8% lower RMSE than the best single public product at the opening hour and 11.4% lower at the final bulletin over 7,590 city-days (`2609.23969v1`).
2. The same price is not the truth, and the venue tape is not clean: on Polymarket the public order-book feed recovers the trade aggressor only about 59% of the time, and direction-dependent measures computed from it flip sign on most markets (`2604.24366v2`).
3. Ultra-short asset-price contracts are manipulable at settlement: after Polymarket's five-minute Bitcoin contract launched, final-ten-second order flow on Binance spiked about 50%, and 821 wallets extracted $8.2M, 93% of it from retail (`2606.31675v1`).
4. Lengthening the contract horizon is the design remedy: the same push signature is much attenuated at fifteen minutes than at five (`2606.31675v1`).
5. Any leverage above one on a binary claim carried to the adverse payoff leaves an account shortfall that is independent of the pre-resolution mark path (`2605.10400v2`).
6. The vig is the floor: a spread bet pays 91 cents on the dollar, a 9-cent vigorish, and random spread betting returns about -4.4% (`1910.08858v2`).
7. Prediction-market quotes carry a longshot spread premium: the median quoted spread is about 400 bps in the central price decile and 1,300-1,800 bps for markets trading below 0.10 (`2604.24366v2`).
8. If every trader is a Kelly bettor, the market price is a wealth-weighted average of beliefs with bounded log regret to the best participant (`1201.6655v1`).
9. Fractional Kelly is not a separate heuristic: it is exactly full Kelly with a belief blended between your own and the market price (`1201.6655v1`).
10. Even a known 60/40 edge is hard to harvest: of 61 finance-trained subjects given a 60% heads coin and a 20% Kelly fraction, only 21% reached the payout cap and 28% went bust (`1701.01427v1`).
11. Informed-trading detection on these venues is layered: account-level skill, account-level lifecycle and per-market leakage answer different questions and none subsumes the others (`2605.02287v2`).
12. The on-chain book is not a top-of-book market: the best level carries a median 13.6% of top-10 depth, and self-counterparty wash has a 1% median with a 22% tail (`2604.24366v2`).

## 1. Calibration: when the price beats the forecaster

The cleanest calibration test in the slice races a prediction market against the professional forecasting stack. On Kalshi's daily high-temperature ladders for seven U.S. cities, a panel of 7,590 city-days (`2609.23969v1`, p.2; cities and stations, p.5), the market-implied forecast read off the ladder of mutually exclusive contracts had a root-mean-square error of 2.44 degrees F at the end of its first hour of trading against 2.70 for the National Blend of Models, a 9.8% advantage, and 1.88 against 2.12 by the final bulletin on the target morning, an 11.4% advantage (p.7). The market also beat an out-of-sample weighted combination of the entire public stack by 3.1% at the opening hour, 4.3% in the evening and 3.0% at the final bulletin (p.7). The advantage is largest where forecasting is hardest: Miami, where the NBM's preceding-day RMSE is only 1.6 degrees F against 2.6-3.5 elsewhere, is the one city the market does not beat, and each additional degree of NBM error adds about six percentage points to the market's edge with R-squared 0.22 (p.7, p.8). The mechanism is not that traders are better meteorologists; it is that they publish earlier. When the NBM posts an update, a one-standard-deviation disagreement of 1.49 degrees F moves the NBM 0.063 degrees F, while a typical 0.43 degree F revision moves the market only 0.016 degrees F, so the public forecast travels toward the market about four times as far as the market travels toward the public forecast (p.8). The economic size is not trivial: in the Chicago utility zone each degree F of daily high above 75 adds roughly 340-480 MW of evening-peak demand, so a third of a degree of removed error is worth on the order of a hundred megawatts of scheduling error (p.10). The limit is that this is one venue, one variable, and the ladder is a distribution, not a single claim; the authors themselves rely on liquidity screens that keep 86% of observations (p.11).

Why aggregation should work at all is the 2012 theory result. If every participant bets the Kelly fraction f = (p - p_m)/(1 - p_m) against the market price p_m, the competitive-equilibrium price is the wealth-weighted average of the participants' beliefs (`1201.6655v1`, p.2), and wealth is redistributed across rounds exactly as a Bayesian update would redistribute posterior probability over which participant is right (p.3). The market's worst-case log loss exceeds the best participant's by at most ln(1/w_i) for that participant's starting wealth w_i, a bound that holds against adversarially chosen outcome sequences (p.3). The practical caveat is that this is an equilibrium of price-takers; it says nothing about a market in which participants respond strategically to the price.

Two robustness details matter if such a market is to be used as a signal. The headline uses the implied mean of the ladder, which is sensitive to how the open tails are modelled, but the implied median, which does not depend on the tails, agrees with the mean to within 0.05 degrees F pooled and moves the three gaps by less than two percentage points (`2609.23969v1`, p.11). And the public stack closes on the market by leaning on real-time observations: LAMP, the observation-conditioned product, drives most of the combination's improvement, and if LAMP is dropped the market's lead rises to 3.3%, 4.7% and 6.4% at the three anchors (p.7).

## 2. The vig, the odds, and the favourite-longshot bias

Betting markets price a known edge less generously than a financial venue would. A spread bet is engineered to have even odds and pays 91 cents on the dollar, so the bookmaker keeps a 9-cent vigorish (`1910.08858v2`, p.3). Across 16 Las Vegas sportsbooks, random spread betting on NFL, NBA, NCAAF, NCAAB and WNBA games returned a mean of about -4.4%, which is the vig showing up as expected loss, while random moneyline betting returned between -6.4% and 0.8% (p.4). The paper's positive result is that a non-parametric win-probability model, plus an epsilon band that refuses to bet extreme spreads and an expected-value threshold, produced positive returns in all five sports: 16.57% for the NFL and 17.01% for the WNBA under the simple average model, and 8.04% to 13.02% under the frequency-weighted model, after bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations and a Bonferroni correction across the five sports (p.16, p.10, p.11). The authors are careful that the S&P 500 generally outperformed the betting returns over the same years, and that their 1990-2017 sample deliberately excludes the early 2000s, 2008 and 2018 (p.9, p.5). The finding is real but bounded: the edge is identified by a hyperparameter search, so it is exactly the kind of result that a multiple-testing correction is meant to police.

The same favourite-longshot geometry shows up on-chain. On Polymarket, the median quoted spread in basis points of mid is about 400 in the central [0.4, 0.6] price range and climbs to 1,300-1,800 bps for markets trading below 0.10, with the lowest decile showing a median of 1,818 bps (`2604.24366v2`, p.7, p.8). That is an order of magnitude wider than the few-percent longshot premium documented on racetracks and on the older prediction markets, which the authors read as a liquidity-provision constraint on a bounded-upside binary contract rather than as pure misperception (p.7). For a strategy, the operative fact is that the cost of holding a position is a function of the price level, not a constant.

The behavioural floor is a lab result worth internalising. Given a coin biased 60/40 to heads, a $25 stake and a $250 cap, the Kelly fraction is 20% of the account (`1701.01427v1`, p.3). Of 61 finance-trained subjects, only 21% reached the cap, against a simulated 95% for any constant fraction from 10% to 20%, 28% went bust, and the average bet size was 15% of the bankroll with erratic sizing; 18 of the 61 bet their entire bankroll on a single flip, raising ruin probability to 40-60%, and 67% bet on tails at some point (p.3, p.4). The uncapped version of the same game has an expected value of $3,220,637 over 300 flips, for which a log-utility player should still only pay about $10,000 (p.5). This is the clearest evidence in the slice that the failure mode is sizing, not forecasting.

A further behavioural detail from the same experiment is that the subjects did not converge: suboptimal betting of similar magnitude persisted throughout the session, and only 5 of the 61 had ever heard of the Kelly criterion, two of whom still played badly (`1701.01427v1`, p.3). Betting on tails was correlated with a preceding run of heads, which the authors read as gambler's fallacy rather than a genuine doubt about the bias, since 75% of respondents said they believed the 60/40 statement (p.4).

The sportsbook result is also a warning about hyperparameter search. The reported returns are the best over an epsilon grid and an expected-value-threshold grid, selected on the same data that produced them, and the authors lean on bootstrap intervals and a Bonferroni correction to argue that the optimal ROI and optimal epsilon are jointly positive across five sports (`1910.08858v2`, p.10, p.11). Even with that discipline the numbers are gross of market impact and assume independent one-dollar bets with no compounding and no Kelly sizing (p.9).

## 3. On-chain prediction market microstructure

The most complete microstructure picture comes from a 52-day tick-level archive of Polymarket's public WebSocket order-book feed, 30 billion events across 385,198 markets, joined to the authoritative on-chain trade record, on a pre-registered 600-market panel (`2604.24366v2`, p.1). Eight stylised facts come out of it, and each is a design parameter for anyone simulating this venue. Depth is layered rather than concentrated: the top level carries a median 13.6% of cumulative top-10 depth against a uniform null of 0.10, the median market's Kullback-Leibler divergence from uniform is 0.087, only 9% of markets are top-heavy in the strong sense, and 57% have a top-level share within a factor of two of uniform (p.7). The longshot premium of Section 2 is the quoted-spread fact, and the median quoted half-spread on the central price decile is about 200 bps, an order of magnitude wider than post-decimalisation U.S. equities (p.4). Quote-update timing does not cluster on Polygon block boundaries in any economically meaningful way, with a median block-alignment share of 0.102 against a 0.10 null even though 351 of 600 markets reject the exact point null statistically (p.7, p.8). Maker liquidity is diverse: the median volume-weighted Herfindahl index is 0.031, about 32 effective makers, with a p90 of 0.119 and a maximum of 0.40 (p.8). Category-conditional effective half-spreads differ by only about +/-0.04 probability points across Crypto, Sports, Geopolitics and Other, but within-category interquartile ranges are wide, about 0.3 probability points for Crypto and Sports (p.9). Archive-ingestion latency has a median of 41.5 ms, a p90 of 166 ms and a p99 of 6,108 ms, which the authors explicitly label a property of their collector rather than of the exchange (p.9). Self-counterparty wash share has a median of 0.97%, a p90 of 4.5%, a p99 of 10.6% and a maximum of 22.2%, against 25-70% documented on unregulated token exchanges under a different incentive environment (p.10). Finally, cross-sectional depth is explained by log duration (+0.222), log p(1-p) (-1.02) and log volume (+0.41), with the coefficient on log seconds-to-close collapsing to +0.008 (SE 0.102, R-squared 0.57, n=313) once those are conditioned on (p.10, p.11). The limit is that the depth fact is cross-sectional at one midpoint; the paper cannot identify a within-market depth trajectory.

A second, independent Polymarket panel from a one-week window in April 2026 (61,087 candidates, 13,298 passing adequacy gates) adds the boundary-region microstructure: the median half-spread is about 0.0055 in the low boundary region, 0.2700 in the mid region and 0.00525 in the high boundary region, and median/mean/p99 trade sizes are 20/713/10,000 USDC in politics, 16.5/301/2,500 in sports and 10/79/500 in crypto (`2605.10400v2`, p.16, p.6). Final-24-hour activity relative to an earlier baseline is 24.6x in crypto, 2.5x in sports, 0.9x in other and 0.68x in politics (p.16). Trading is concentrated at the close, and it is not uniform across categories.

One more fact rounds out the venue picture. On the top-100 stratum, where the median market has about 11,000 on-chain trades, the Glosten-Harris decomposition converges on 97 of 100 markets and returns a median effective half-spread of essentially zero, -0.0003 probability points, with a median transitory component of 0.00001 and a median adverse-selection component of 0.0 (`2604.24366v2`, p.11). The authors attribute the near-null to the removal of sign errors rather than to a frictionless venue. The panel itself was pre-registered, with the selection rule, volume metric and seed committed and the panel hash recorded before analysis, which is worth copying for any cross-sectional venue study (p.5).

## 4. The measurement trap: trade direction and direction-dependent measures

The load-bearing methodological result of the slice is that the standard source of trade direction is unavailable on a decentralized CLOB. Six standard measures, effective spread, realized spread, Roll, Abdi-Ranaldo, Kyle's lambda and Amihud, all take the form E[sign_t * f(price, mid, size)], and each needs an aggressor sign per trade (`2604.24366v2`, p.1, p.4). On Polymarket the public feed's `change_side` field marks which side of the book moved, not which side initiated the trade, and matching feed-inferred buckets against on-chain `OrderFilled` events at 5-second and exact-price granularity across four disjoint 7-day windows gives a panel-mean sign agreement of 0.615 with a market-clustered bootstrap 95% CI of [0.58, 0.65], and a volume-weighted agreement of 0.592 (p.1, p.12). That is just above the 50% chance baseline, versus the roughly 80% that Lee-Ready achieves on equity venues (p.1, p.4). The consequence is that on the comparable subset of the top-100 panel the effective half-spread changes sign on 67% of markets when feed-inferred direction is swapped for the on-chain record, and Kyle's lambda on 60%; in a second non-overlapping window those rates fall to 50% and 43%, still in or below the chance band (p.1, p.13). Trade-count divergence is direction-unpredictable, with a median STRICT-to-on-chain count ratio of 0.23 and no correctable scaling factor (p.13). The paper's fix is to source direction from on-chain `OrderFilled` events and to accept injected authoritative trades in the measure pipeline, and it argues the same constraint binds on any hybrid venue whose public feed broadcasts post-match book state without the taker identity (p.12, p.13). The limit is the same as everywhere in this slice: it is one venue over a short window, and the two-window swing shows the flip rates are themselves unstable.

The robustness checks show why the direction error is not a small-sample artefact. Weighting the comparable subset by on-chain dollar volume raises the effective-spread sign-flip rate from 67% to 86%, because the largest market in the window, which alone accounts for two-thirds of top-100 volume, is one of the markets that flips; restricting to markets with at least 100 inferred trades raises it to 73% (`2604.24366v2`, p.13). The Kyle's lambda flip rate moves the other way under volume weighting, from 60% to 18%, which the authors attribute to lambda being dominated by zero-bucket noise on the largest market, and they treat the effective-spread result as the load-bearing one (p.13).

## 5. Settlement manipulation: when the contract's reference price is tradeable

The strongest negative result is that an asset-price prediction contract settles on a price its holders can move. Combined monthly volume on Kalshi and Polymarket rose from under $5B in September 2025 to about $24B by April 2026, and the fastest-growing contracts are binary claims on an asset price with horizons shrinking to five minutes (`2606.31675v1`, p.3). Polymarket's five-minute Bitcoin up/down contract launched on 12 February 2026, after the fifteen-minute contract on 9 October 2025 and the four-hour contract on 15 October 2025, which gives a staggered natural experiment (p.5, p.13). The Binance mid sits about 2.5 bps from the Chainlink oracle and finishes on the same side of the strike as the resolution about 85% of the time, so a push of a few basis points reliably carries the resolution (p.6). After launch, absolute order flow in the final ten seconds of each five-minute cycle jumped about 50% above the pre-launch level, and in the roughly 6% of cycles still near-even just before the close the near-settlement order-flow jump was about 3.9 times that in the rest (p.6). The signature of manipulation rather than information is the reversal: within ten seconds the price reverts by about a quarter in near-even cycles and a tenth in the others (p.6). The paper classifies the top decile of relative final-ten-second flow, about 1,600 cycles, as manipulated; these fall disproportionately in thin-liquidity hours, 56% overnight and 44% on weekends against 40% and 27% of normal cycles (p.6). The pushes change outcomes: against the favoured side in near-even cycles a push flipped the winner 65% of the time against 41% in ordinary trading, and even in cycles the market gave a 90-100% chance, a push reversed the outcome 34% of the time against 1% (p.7). Because every trade is on-chain, the paper identifies 821 profitable wallets, about one in three hundred of roughly 243,000 traders, who take $8.2M in pushed cycles while breaking even elsewhere, with 93% of the loss falling on retail, who are on the losing side in 65% of manipulated cycles against 48% of normal ones (p.7). The hedging explanation is ruled out by timing: essentially all of the signed trading falls in the final fifty seconds (p.8). The remedy the model and the data both point to is the horizon, and the fifteen-minute contract shows the signature much attenuated (p.3, p.5).

The model behind the evidence is a sequential-trading game in the Glosten-Milgrom style with two venues, in which the manipulator's uninformed order pools with the informed trader's and therefore lowers the adverse selection the spot maker faces (`2606.31675v1`, p.4, p.5). That is why the liquidity response is asymmetric: near-even closes add depth on Binance, exactly where the model predicts an uninformed push should lower spot adverse selection, while Polymarket depth falls as binary-market makers face direct exposure to a flipping settlement (p.6). The same logic predicts that a longer horizon dilutes the manipulator's pivotality, which the fifteen-minute and four-hour contracts bear out (p.5).

## 6. Informed trading: insiders, leakage and the layers of detection

Three 2026 methodologies for detecting informed trading on Polymarket operate at different layers and should not be conflated (`2605.02287v2`). A composite anomaly screen over more than 210,000 wallet-market pairs estimates about $143M in aggregate anomalous profit (p.1). A sign-randomization skill classifier over the complete transaction history, 1.72 million accounts, 210,322 markets and $13.76B of volume across 2023-2025, randomizes buy/sell direction at the event level 10,000 times and classifies 3.14% of accounts, 54,477, as skilled winners whose order flow predicts both next-period price changes and final outcomes, with 44% of skilled accounts retaining the classification out of sample against roughly 10% for equity mutual-fund managers (p.2). A separate single-event lifecycle heuristic flags 1,950 accounts as suspected insiders, with mean realized profit of $15,012.92 and median $2,758.29, and the flagged accounts' order imbalance predicts price changes with t = 2.54 and final outcomes with t = 8.65 (p.8). The per-market alternative is an information-leakage score that measures the fraction of the terminal price move priced in before the corresponding public news event; on the $269M "US forces enter Iran by April 30" contract the article-anchored score is +0.113 against a resolution-anchored proxy of -0.331, a difference of 0.444 in magnitude and opposite in sign (p.10). The Van Dyke indictment gives an external benchmark: $409,881 across thirteen Polymarket trades, matching the first flagged account's realized profit of $409,882.03 to the dollar, with the three flagged accounts combining for $626,484 (p.2, p.13, p.14). The methodological point is that the layers leave different questions open. A one-shot insider who trades one event fails the ten-event threshold of the skill classifier by design, so the canonical insider pattern falls outside it (p.8), and a platform-wide skilled-winner statistic pools sports, politics and crypto markets whose information technologies are structurally different, with sports at 42.05% of markets and 35.37% of volume, politics at 7.52% and 35.85%, and crypto at 36.96% and 18.73% (p.7). The limit is severe: the paper is a methodological comparison of other authors' results, not a primary-data study, and of eighteen substantive markets in one cluster only one satisfied the full pipeline requirements (p.11).

The practical reading is a pipeline rather than a single test. The comparison paper argues for category-conditioned account risk scoring first, a per-market leakage score second, and human review third, because each stage filters a different dimension: a high-leakage market does not identify the wallet, and a skilled wallet does not identify the contract (`2605.02287v2`, p.3, p.11). The public record gives scale to the problem, with six newly created wallets buying YES at about ten cents before the October 2024 Iran strike for roughly $1.2M, and one 2026 Iran cluster trading over $832M across flagged markets (p.2).

## 7. Illiquid event-driven instruments: what does not port

The final lesson is about instrument design. Carrying crypto-style perpetual-futures mechanics onto a binary claim that pays zero or one does not work, and the failure is an identity rather than a calibration. For a synthetic long of x units entered at price p0 with collateral x*p0/L, terminal equity when the claim pays zero is -x*p0*(1 - 1/L) for any L > 1, independent of the pre-resolution mark path, because mark-to-market gains and the final jump telescope to the entry-to-terminal payoff (`2605.10400v2`, p.2, p.7). A symmetric result holds for a short carried to a payout of one (p.7). The boundary-funding trilemma is that a bounded funding transfer cannot impose a uniform relative-basis pressure arbitrarily close to zero or one, while an uncapped relative-basis rule becomes unbounded and violates payer solvency (p.2, p.8). The empirical replay on a seven-day Polymarket window, 13.69 billion events and 110,828 market identifiers, confirms the structural concern and not the controls: boundary depth is a pooled median ratio of 1.72 against a floor of 1.5 and the median absolute final-hour move is 0.50 against a floor of 0.10, both material (p.6, p.10), but at L = 5 the dynamic-margin configuration is about 6% worse on liquidation incidence than the static baseline rather than the pre-specified 30% better, pooled hypothetical drawdown falls only 5.1% against a 50% floor, and the staged halt removes 80.4% of final-hour liquidations mechanically while terminal account-shortfall incidence rises 2.4% instead of falling 75% (p.11). The sample is sports-heavy, 51.1% of the full usable sample and 77.9% of the three named classes, and the replay is a fixed-path stress test, not an equilibrium (p.6, p.5). For sizing, the theoretical anchor is the Kelly fraction f = (p - p_m)/(1 - p_m) on a binary claim priced at p_m, and its fractional variant, which is equivalent to full Kelly with the belief blended toward the market price (`1201.6655v1`, p.2, p.3).

The paper is careful that the problem is terminal maturity and market closure rather than the label "perpetual": a leveraged cash-settled dated future has the same adverse terminal identity at expiry, and a physically backed financed claim moves the risk into collateral control and lender recovery (`2605.10400v2`, p.3, p.4). What is specific to a normalized probability-like underlying is that the price lies in [0, 1], the adverse loss has a transparent upper bound, and the relative mark-index error becomes singular near the boundaries, which is what generates the funding trilemma (p.4).

## Caveats

The evidence base is uneven. Two papers have no primary market data at all: `1201.6655v1` is a theory and simulation result about Kelly bettors, and `2605.02287v2` is a methodological comparison of other authors' findings rather than a new dataset. `1701.01427v1` is a 61-subject lab experiment, `1910.08858v2` is a 1990-2017 sample of Las Vegas lines that deliberately excludes several years, and both are single-market or single-venue. The four 2026 papers are each single-venue and short-window: `2604.24366v2` is 52 days of one order-book feed, `2606.31675v1` is one contract on one platform over two months, `2605.10400v2` is one seven-day window and is sports-heavy, and `2609.23969v1` is one exchange and one variable, temperature. The slice disagrees with itself on the interpretation of the longshot premium, which `2604.24366v2` reads as a liquidity-provision constraint on a bounded-upside contract while the older racetrack and prediction-market literature it cites reads behaviourally, and `2605.02287v2` explicitly documents that three detection methodologies measure different constructs. No paper here establishes that any strategy is profitable net of the vig, gas and slippage, that calibration generalises across venues, or that an event-linked derivative can be deployed safely; `2605.10400v2` is explicit that its replay identifies mechanical stress, not equilibrium.

## Papers read in depth

- `2604.24366v2` - The Anatomy of a Decentralized Prediction Market: Microstructure Evidence from the Polymarket Order Book (2026). Eight stylised facts plus the trade-direction measurement result; limited to 52 days of one venue's feed and one cross-sectional snapshot for the depth finding.
